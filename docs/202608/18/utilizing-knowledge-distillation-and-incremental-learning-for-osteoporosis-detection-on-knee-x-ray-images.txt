ABSTRACT 

Osteoporosis is a bone disease that mostly affects malnourished and elderly people by reducing their bone mass, resulting in higher chances of bone fractures which are difficult to recover from and sometimes prove fatal. To detect the disease, a doctor may order a Bone Mineral Density (BMD) test to detect osteoporosis and low bone density. This is commonly done using dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry (DXA), which uses multiple low energy X-rays to measure the BMD of our skeleton. Routine screening for osteoporosis is a time-consuming and laborious task, requiring specialized equipment not available in rural areas. According to the American Medical Association (AMA) Survey conducted in 2024, almost 66% of the physicians are using computer-aided diagnosis to ensure in-time disease diagnosis, that is about 28% high as compared to the previous fiscal year i.e., 2023. However, there are few X-ray osteoporosis datasets that are publicly available to train on. In this paper, an automated system has been proposed that detects osteoporosis from X-ray images of Knee and utilizes knowledge distillation and incremental learning techniques to improve the generalizability of DL models across the two datasets that are publicly available. Knowledge distillation is utilized to distill the patterns learned by teacher models onto smaller student models and incremental learning techniques are utilized to overcome catastrophic forgetting when training on multiple datasets. However, experimentation showed that knowledge distilled models had no significant impact and that the best performance with Class Incremental Learning was by training DenseNet-121 using Learning without Forgetting which resulted in an accuracy of 62.6% with a performance gap of 30% when trained on direct 3-class data (92.9%) indicating that direct training is still essential for achieving high accuracy and class incremental learning is just a constrained baseline for data-limited environments. 

Keywords: Cross dataset online learning, Cross incremental learning, Knowledge distillation, KONet, Osteoporosis 

Introduction 

The fragility of a bone is dependent on its bone density. This density is maintained by a cyclical process of bone resorption and formation where it is broken down into calcium and minerals and then reformed. Osteoporosis is a disease that disrupts this cycle by causing reduction in bone reformation, gradually leading to a decrease in bone density as the amount broken down is greater than the amount reformed. This results in weakening of the bones and increased risk of fractures which cost more to treat than it would have been to prevent it beforehand. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), this disease affects millions of people and is prevalent among people over the age of 50 1 especially women in postmenopausal stage are at a higher risk of getting osteoporosis. As far as developing countries like Pakistan are concerned, the incidence is very high affecting up to 97% of women aged 75-84 and 55% of those between 45-54 2, 3 . Based on the statistics 4, in Pakistan, 9.9 million people have osteoporosis, and the numbers may rise to 12.01 million by 2050. It not only results in low bone mass but may also lead to other severe heath concerns such as immobility, loss of height and reduced quality of life. So regular checkups are required for timely detection and treatment of this disease. In clinical settings, Dual Energy X-ray Absorptiometry (DXA) is used to detect osteoporosis by measuring t-score and z-score of a patient and hence the bone mineral density (BMD) value. Unfortunately, the rural and under-privileged areas where it is most prevalent do not have the necessary equipment and specialized personnel to perform such tests and diagnose the disease. In recent years, deep learning has gained much popularity by addressing problems specifically related to medical domain. By learning complex features from raw data, deep learning models have paved the way for scientist and researchers across the globe to overcome various conventional method used for medical related diagnosis resulting in improved performance. Several effective deep learning models such as EfficientNet and DenseNet have been trained in literature to detect and classify osteoporosis using various modalities such as CT scan, MRI and X-ray

However, challenges persist in those studies including high computational requirements for training the model and catastrophic forgetting, where a model forgets previously learned information when trained on new data. In this study, we addressed these challenges by proposing a framework that utilizes knowledge distillation (to reduce computational overhead for lightweight clinical deployment) and incremental learning (to avoid catastrophic forgetting) while using only x-ray images providing a cost-effective and easily accessible screening tool. Because knee X-rays are so common in clinical practice and provide a substantial opportunity for opportunistic screening without additional radiation exposure, they are chosen for osteoporosis detection. Although dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) is still the gold standard for diagnosis, its limited availability in primary care settings needs more easily accessible screening methods. Our proposed system consists of an ensemble model used for diagnosing osteoporosis using X-ray images. Knowledge distillation is used to overcome the computational limitations frequently present in clinical settings by transferring complex feature representations from intricate teacher models to effective student models. This work also utilized incremental learning technique since medical data is frequently collected in temporal streams across various clinical sites. This strategy is intended to help the model retain previously learned information while adapting to new datasets, although its effectiveness requires further validation on larger multi-center cohorts. Literature Review 

An extensive review of papers from the literature has been done involving osteoporosis detection and incremental learning implemented in medical diagnosis, as discussed in subsections below. 

Osteoporosis Classification 

Several papers have been published involving bone mineral density (BMD) prediction. Some deep learning models have been used to predict the bone mineral density and T-score using tabular and X-ray data such as 5, which used Resnet50 to predict BMD and T score, and 6, which applied several feature selection techniques and then trained multiple classifiers in parallel to create an ensemble. Yang et al. 7 showed that attenuation values of vertebra in chest Computed Tomography (CT) can also be used to detect osteoporosis using Logistic Regression (LR). Other research such as 8, applied pre-processing and smudging and then trained on a custom CNN. 9, trained AlexNet, Resnet-18 and VGGNet-16 and 19 on X-ray images for osteoporosis detection. In 10 , a two-step approach was used to classify osteoporosis by first segmenting the chest CT scans using a U-net architecture and then passing segmented image through a modified DenseNet to get predictions. A variety of CNNs have also been trained for osteoporosis classification, for instance, in 11 , CT scans of spines were used to train VGG-16 and 19, MobileNetv2, Xception, ResNet-50 and InceptionResNetV2 using transfer learning from models already trained on ImageNet dataset to counteract small dataset sizes. In 12 , Spinal and Abdominal CT scans were used to train a U-net for lumbar vertebral body segmentation and then the segmented image was fed into DenseNet-121 for calculation of BMD score and osteoporosis classification. In 13 , CT scans were used to train U-Net segmentation models to segment and label all vertebral bodies and a linear function would be built using the segmentation masks to diagnose osteoporosis. More traditional methods of machine learning have also been used, such as in 14 , where a hierarchical approach for feature selection of clinical data and CT images of spines has been used to train algorithms such as State Vector Machines (SVMs), Random Forests (RFs), eXtreme Gradient Boosting and Logistic Regression (LR) for osteoporosis detection. In 15 , Dental panoramic radiographs (DPRs) were used to train a custom CNN and VGG-16 using 5-fold cross validation for osteoporosis detection to avoid over-fitting and bias. In 16 ,DPRs along with clinical covariate data were pre-processed by creating Region Of Interests (ROIs) on the dental images and then augmented to avoid overfitting due to the small dataset and then used to train EfficientNet and ResNet family of models with the covariate data added to feature vector before Fully Connected (FC) layer to detect osteoporosis. Jang et al. 17 cropped ROIs onto hip radiographs dataset, used image augmentation techniques and then used the dataset to train a VGG-16 network for osteoporosis classification. In 18 , hip radiographs along with clinical data were pre-processed by adding ROIs and then used to train multiple CNN models such as ResNet-18 and 34, GoogleNet, EfficientNet-b3 and b4 of which EfficientNet-b3 gave the best performance. In 19 , X-ray images of the pelvis was used to train Back Propagation (BP) network, SVM and a modified U-net, of which, the U-net performed the best. X-ray images of knee, hand, leg, hip, and spine were used in 20 to perform binary classification for osteoporosis detection with a dataset size of 853 samples using vision transformer variant ViT-B/16 giving 96.01% accuracy by first segmenting the images using Pix2Pix architecture. Similarly, BAE-Vit was used to estimate bone age

Incremental Learning 

Incremental Learning has been used to ensure that the information obtained from training on original datasets is not lost when training on new datasets. Learning without Forgetting (LwF) has been used to train deep CNN models for detecting covid-19 while still mostly retaining its performance on original dataset in the paper 24 where VGG-16, ResNet and wide ResNet were fine-tuned on a dataset compiled from multiple public datasets and wide ResNet performed the best. LwF has also been used to retain performance between two different datasets both containing different classes of cancer images in 25 , where multiple different cancer datasets were collected and used to train VGG-16 and 19, DenseNet-201, MobileNetV3 small and large and MobileNetV3 small performed the best. Another example of incremental learning is training on each individual task and then combining into a unified model to avoid the computational overhead incurred by other incremental learning techniques in 26 ,where CIFAR100, PATH16 and SKIN8 datasets were used to train ResNet-18 backbones for a unified DynaMMo model. A hierarchical approach has also been proposed for diagnostic fetal echocardiography analysis where ultrasound videos are first broadly classified into super classes and then further classified into fine classes, this means that new classes can be added in parallel, this is achieved in the work 27 by using a super-model which is a CNN to extract features from each frame and then using an LSTM followed by classifier to select the CNN sub-model. Incremental learning has also been used to train a multi-organ segmentation model using different partially labelled datasets and prove that catastrophic forgetting is due to different distributions of the datasets in the paper 28 , where five open-sourced datasets were used to train U-Net. New loss functions have also been developed to counteract imbalanced datasets, partially-labeled data and introduction of new classes which can be frequent in the medical domain 29 , where a U-Net was trained in multiple different experiments to determine efficacy of proposed loss function. In 30 , a Class Incremental Learning (CIL) balanced classification loss which puts more emphasis on classes with less samples and a distribution margin loss which avoids feature embedding overlap between old and new classes were proposed and used to train on CCH5000, HAM10000 and EyePACS datasets, where the proposed loss function is compared with classical incremental learning approaches. In 31 , VGG-16 and MobileNetV2 was trained on multiple different dermatological modalities using EWC (Elastic Weight Consolidation), Averaged-Gradient Episodic Memory (A-GEM) and experience replay as incremental learning strategies for use in classification of dermatological images from the Portuguese National Health System according to their modality. In 32 , a CNN with an Encoder and Decoder utilizing Discrete Wavelet transform to incrementally learn the features was trained on 3D brain and cardiac MRI images for use in medical image registration. A classifier-free framework has also been proposed that utilizes a CLIP model with the ViT-B/32 architecture and only trains the feature extractor part of the model and uses contrastive representation learning along with knowledge distillation-based incremental learning to perform semantic segmentation. In 33 , a Multiple Instance Class-Incremental Learning (MICIL) algorithm is proposed which utilizes knowledge distillation and data rehearsal which provides partial access to past classes when training on current classes. The loss function is a combination of Cross-Entropy loss, knowledge distillation loss and a embedding matching loss which encourages similar embeddings as previous training. X. Wu et. al. in their paper 34 

highlighted the significance of incremental learning by addressing challenges like catastrophic forgetting. The authors further discussed the categories of continued learning within the context of medical image analysis. Intense research has been done on the topic of incremental learning, as it does not require the old dataset, which may be due to hardware limitations or security reasons, and does not need to retrain it from scratch. However, incremental learning has not been tested on the classification of osteoporosis. Further, Predictive modeling is being used more and more in modern data-driven medical research to improve clinical outcome analysis beyond radiographic evaluation. For example, K-Nearest Neighbor (K-NN) algorithms have shown success in predicting osteoporosis to recommend physical activity for older women in 35 , and AI has been used to diagnose osteoporosis from clinical risk factors to suggest particular sports programs in 36 .Additionally, in 37 associated comorbidities like coronary heart disease in active individuals are predicted using machine learning approaches like Support Vector Machines (SVM). The proposed work is an essential first step toward these integrated clinical decision support systems by offering a lightweight and accurate diagnostic engine.

Datasets 

Two different Osteoporosis datasets are used in this work; Osteoporosis Knee X-ray Dataset (OKD) and Knee X-ray Osteoporosis Dataset (KOD). OKD is an open-source dataset available on Kaggle 23 ˙It contains 372 X-ray images of knees belonging to two classes that are labelled as either normal or osteoporosis. There is no class imbalance in this dataset. Both the output categories have equal number of images. KOD is available on Mendeley 38 . It contains 239 X-ray images of knees (with a few containing both knees in a single image) belonging to three classes that are labelled as normal, osteopenia or osteoporosis. Osteopenia is the intermediate stage before progressing to osteoporosis and can be treated at this level. KOD is imbalanced with 36 normal images, 154 images belonging to osteopenia class and 49 images belonging to osteoporosis. First, pre-processing was applied to KOD to separate the two knees into two separate images which resulted in 57 normal images, 192 osteopenia images and 75 osteoporosis images giving an increased dataset size of 324 images. For images containing both knees, the left and right knee crops derived from the same original radiograph were assigned to the same data partition to avoid leakage between training, validation, and test sets. Augmentation was applied only after this partitioning step. From these images, three variants of KOD were created: 1. 2 class KOD for performing binary classification containing normal and osteoporosis cases with a dataset size of 132 images. 2. 3 class KOD for performing multiclass classification containing normal, osteopenia and osteoporosis cases. The dataset imbalance issue was resolved by only taking 84 samples of osteopenia class for this particular dataset, giving a dataset size of 216 images. 3. Osteopenia KOD for performing incremental learning containing only osteopenia cases with a dataset size of 192 images. All the images were resized to 224 x 224, 0.1 of each dataset was set aside as test set and 10-fold cross-validation was performed on the remaining 0.9. Image augmentation was then performed on the training set of each fold which included random affine and random zoom out and was used to increase the number of training images to around 1400. Methodology 

The pre-trained models used in the osteoporosis detection are EfficientNet-B0, DenseNet-121, EdgeNeXt-small and MobileNetV3-Small. The KONet proposed in 39 is created as an ensemble of EfficientNet-B0 and DenseNet-121 by taking the best reported ratio of 0.6 and 0.4 for EfficientNet-B0 and DenseNet-121 predictions respectively. The performance of the models on the two datasets are measured using accuracy, F1-score and Area Under Curve (AUC). The overall workflow of the proposed methodology and each of the models used are described in the sections detailed below.

Hyper-parameters 

The models were trained online on kaggle with a GPU P100. For all three steps, each of the models are trained with a batch size of 32 for 20 epochs using AdamW optimizer with learning rate of 0.0000625 for most of the tasks and Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) with learning rate of 0.00002, momentum of 0.9 and weight decay of 0.0005 is utilized for CIL as stated in 42 .The weights ( λ ) for Incremental Learning were tuned empirically to attain the best accuracy across both datasets and when introducing new class. The weight for EWC was made to scale with the number of epochs in CIL to avoid overfitting on new class. Key Contributions 

The approach used in this study makes several distinct contributions to the field of automated screening for knee osteoporosis: • Opportunistic Screening Framework: Using the existing clinical data, a novel knee X-ray imaging framework was proposed for the secondary purpose of osteoporosis detection, identifying at-risk individuals without the need for further radiation or expense. • Enhanced Knowledge Transfer: By employing knowledge distillation, the advanced feature extraction abilities of a sophisticated teacher network were compressed into a streamlined student model, enabling deployment in resource-limited clinical settings. • Adaptive Learning Strategy: The model’s resilience against "catastrophic forgetting" is guaranteed by the integration of incremental learning, which offers a scalable solution for long-term clinical use as new clinical datasets or imaging protocols are introduced. • Potential Screening Utility: By concentrating on the trabecular architecture of knee, this method explores radiographic patterns in knee X-rays that may support osteoporosis risk screening, although direct correlation with DXA-based BMD requires further validation. In real world settings, DXA scan is used for osteoporosis screening but it is expensive, involves radiation exposure and not readily available to majority of the population as far as under developed countries like Pakistan are concerned. So the proposed system provides a low cost and easily accessible tool for opportunistic screening and diagnostic precision.

As shown in tables 6 and 7, none of the p-values, except MobileNetV3-Small for 2 class KOD, were less than 0.05 thus failing the null hypothesis. This means that, aside from MobileNetV3-Small for 2 class KOD, knowledge distillation did not provide any significant improvement, this may be attributed to the small size of the dataset and the similar accuracies. Accordingly, the fine-tuned version of the models was used to perform incremental learning. Although Knowledge Distillation (KD) does not exhibit statistical significance in terms of increasing accuracy (p > 0.05), we still used it in order to reduce the size of the model. The main goal of KD is to help small and lightweight models learn from big, complex models. Pruning and quantization are also promising compression strategies; however, they were not experimentally evaluated in the present study. KD was selected because it enables knowledge transfer from a larger teacher network to compact student models without explicitly removing network weights or reducing numerical precision. Future work will compare KD with pruning, quantization, and hybrid compression approaches.

In order to achieve reliable results and to rule out any chance of data leakage, a rigorous patient-level data audit was carried out. The data set was properly divided into training and test sets before carrying out any data augmentations. This assures us that there is no overlap between the training and validation data sets, and no patches or iterations of one image were used in both sets. This separation ensures that the high accuracies obtained, such as the 87.9% accuracy obtained in 2-class KOD task, represent genuine generalization rather than artifact replication or leakage. 

Qualitative analysis 

Shown in figures 9a and 9b are the images and heatmaps of normal patients and patients with osteoporosis respectively for EfficientNet-B0, DenseNet-121, EdgeNeXt-Small and MobileNetV3-Small. These heatmaps have been obtained using Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM) 43 technique which captures the gradients of the target class with respect to the feature maps from selected convolution layer (typically from last layer of feature extractor) of the model and overlays it with original image. The heat maps for EfficientNet-B0, DenseNet-121 and MobileNetV3-Small are clearly interpretable and show that they focus on specific areas of the knee joint connecting the top and bottom bones which is most at risk of fracture. The plots obtained by applying t-distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE) reduction on the features learned by the models are shown in figure 10. The plots are difficult to interpret due to the small size of the datasets but show that the features extracted are mostly separable and discriminatory according to labels. Some of the misclassifications by DenseNet-121 when testing on 2 class KOD and Osteopenia KOD after performing Class Incremental Learning are shown alongside the heatmaps for the predicted classes in figure 11. When analyzing the first osteoporosis image and its heatmap, the model does not seem to be paying attention to the knee joint and the top, only focusing on the bottom half of the image, this could be the reason it misclassifies it. When analyzing the second osteopenia image and the normal image, they only focus on the knee joint while ignoring the top and bottom, not looking at the top and bottom of the knee joint together along with the information loss at the edges of an image that is inherent in CNN models must have resulted in its misclassification.

Statistical Significance of Results 

We have used 10-fold Cross validation technique to support the statistical significance of our findings as cross validation provides a robust measure of performance across all available samples. Furthermore, the datasets we used are also limited in terms of diversity. To mitigate this, we have employed knowledge distillation to populate the student model with a more generalized feature space derived from a complex teacher model, thereby reducing the risk of overfitting. Additionally, the incremental learning architecture is specifically incorporated to support future generalizability, as it provides a scalable framework to include larger and more diverse multi-center datasets as they become available, thus continuously improving the model’s diagnostic accuracy.

Limitations and Future Work 

While the proposed Knowledge Distillation and Incremental Learning framework shows encouraging results, there are several limitations to this approach. Firstly, the relatively small dataset size of the current study may limit the model’s capacity to fully represent the osteoporotic variations present in a larger population. However, this limitation is addressed by incorporating incremental learning. The system is intended to develop beyond its initial training set, thereby minimizing the restrictions of the initial sample size over time, permitting the model to be updated continually as new data becomes available. However, data augmentation and sequential training techniques seem to be better suited for resolving computing problems rather than differences in populations. With regard to the datasets used in this research, while it may be enough for illustrating the concept, it may be not enough to capture the full diversity of bone shapes and microarchitecture structures in different populations. Single-center datasets can limit the immediate application of findings to other populations due to various demographic factors such as age, sex, ethnic background, and Body Mass Index (BMI), which affect structural variations and, consequently, radiographic appearances. For example, post-menopausal aging led to faster bone mineral density loss, and different BMI levels affect mechanical loading of the joint. To ensure robust generalizability and mitigate potential biases inherent to single-source datasets, extensive multi-center trials and rigorous external validation across heterogeneous imaging hardware are absolute prerequisites before any real-world clinical implementation can be considered. Secondly, an overly small sample set increases the probability of overfitting, especially when tuning hyperparameters for large-capacity machine learning models. Though cross-validation has been performed to guard against overfitting, it remains imperative that the model’s generalization ability be validated using larger sample sets. Additionally, this model is specifically fine-tuned for knee radiographs; since the structural features of the knee bone are different from hip or lumbar spine, it is not advisable to apply this model to other anatomical regions without further training. Also, this work focused only on utilizing knee X-rays without including patients demographic information such as age, gender, body mass index, etc. Since we lack demographic metadata for the current cohort, it is unclear whether the diagnosis is equally equitable and resilient to demographic-based clinical bias for other highly heterogeneous populations. Despite that, the modularity of the knowledge distillation system suggests that the system could be adapted to other joints in subsequent versions. Future research will focus on multi-modal and multi-center validation and the inclusion of diverse anatomical sites to enhance the clinical robustness of the system. We plan to transition to a multimodal paradigm by incorporating patient’s meta-data (age, gender, BMI, and fracture history) into the radiographic pipeline through feature fusion models. Thus, it is suggested that future works create and share better and larger open source datasets. 

Conclusion 

This study highlights the potential of an opportunistic screening framework to leverage existing knee X-ray imaging for secondary osteoporosis assessment. Various DL models were trained on two open source knee X-ray datasets for classification of osteoporosis and tested for checking the efficacy of Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC) and Learning without Forgetting (LwF) to counteract catastrophic forgetting when performing class incremental learning. EWC and LwF have retained the performance of the models on previous dataset while allowing them to fit on the new dataset. Our results indicate that while the suggested framework is efficient at conducting binary classifications – obtaining 91.8% accuracy in OKD and 90.7% in 2-class KOD cases – when extended to conduct classification tasks on 3-class level by means of class-incremental learning (CIL), the performance of the model suffers severely: specifically, the 3-class KOD CIL version gave only 62.6% accuracy – far below the baseline accuracy of 92.9% obtained from the direct three-class training. The degradation in performance is a clear example of catastrophic forgetting when the model is not able to retain any historical data. On the other hand, although joint training is essential for reaching maximum accuracy, CIL can be used as a good baseline in situations where complete retraining is impossible for certain reasons. Further improvements in incremental learning algorithms should aim at closing this gap in performance to ensure stable accuracy across both datasets.
