# Abstract 

# Purpose: 

To develop and validate a fully-automated, Arti fi cial Intelligence (AI) tool, PSMASegmentator, for the segmentation of Prostate Speci fi c Membrane Antigen (PSMA) PET/CT scans and extraction of imaging biomarkers. 

# Materials and Methods: 

This retrospective study included 1035 PSMA PET/CT scans for training (Germany, n = 597; Australia, n = 438), with multicentre Australian testing datasets (internal, n = 199; external, n = 100). An nnU-Net–based deep learning model with PSMA-speci fi c extensions was trained to segment PSMA-expressing lesions and derive biomarkers aligned with current consensus guidelines (total lesion count [TLC], total tumour volume [TTV], SUVmean, and SUVmax), plus total lesion uptake (TLU) and quotient (TLQ). Segmentation performance was evaluated primarily using F1 and Dice scores and the prognostic power of extracted biomarkers. The software was then executed on an external cohort (n = 1,282; 2015– 2024) with linked mortality data. Biomarker value for survival prognosis was assessed in this cohort using Cox regression and Kaplan–Meier analysis. 

# Results: 

Segmentation performance (n = 299 multicentre scans) was robust (lesion-level F1: 0.800; median Dice: 0.716) and generalisable (p > 0.05 between internal and external sets for F1 and Dice). Agreement between AI- and physician-derived biomarkers was high (Spearman’s ρ = 0.906–0.964; p < 0.0005). All biomarkers were signi fi cantly associated with overall survival in univariable analysis (p < 0.0005) and strati fi ed survival on Kaplan–Meier curves. Notably, each 100 mL increase in AI-derived TTV was associated with a 52.5% increase in mortality risk. 

# Conclusion: 

PSMASegmentator, a freely available software (available at:  https://github.com/UWA-Medical-Physics-Research-Group/PSMASegmentator ), enables reproducible extraction of prognostically signi fi cant PSMA PET/CT biomarkers, facilitating standardised reporting aligned with consensus guidelines and hypothesis generation in research, clinical trials, and collaborative initiatives. 

# 1. Introduction 

Prostate cancer (PCa) is the second most frequent cancer in men, comprising approximately 7.3% of global cancer incidence  1. While  fi ve-year survival in localised disease exceeds 90%, for metastatic disease this decreases to around 30%, highlighting the importance of accurate staging  2. Given its high sensitivity and speci fi city, Prostate Speci fi cMembrane Antigen (PSMA) PET/CT has been adopted as the primary modality for the staging of clinically signi fi cant PCa  3. The Standardised PSMA PET/CT Analysis and Reporting Consensus (SPARC) framework was developed to unify the approach to the interpretation and communication of scan  fi ndings  4. Within this, the biomarkers Total Lesion Count (TLC), Total Tumour Volume (TTV), SUVmean and SUVmax were proposed for reporting metastatic and recurrent disease, given their prognostic signi fi cance across multiple PCa settings  5,6. However, the clinical burden imposed by the need to manually segment these images to facilitate the derivation of these biomarkers makes routine clinical use di ffi cult  7. Several studies have demonstrated semi- and fully-automated approaches to PSMA PET/CT segmentation, 

> Page 3/20

reporting moderate-to-high Dice Similarity Coe ffi cient (DSC) scores, generally strong correlations with manually derived measurements, and reproducible, timely segmentations  7–12 .

No study to date has investigated the prognostic value of AI-derived, SPARC-recommended biomarkers across a broader PCa population and provided a freely available means of automatically deriving them. Thus, this study presents PSMASegmentator: an open-source, AI-based software which facilitates automatic PSMA PET/CT segmentation and biomarker derivation. To demonstrate its prognostic potential, we conduct a large-scale survival analysis using a PCa dataset from South Australia. 

# 2. Materials and Methods 

# 2.1 Imaging Datasets 

Model training for this retrospective study used 1,035 PSMA PET/CTs from two sources: the AutoPET-III Challenge dataset (LMU, Munich; n = 597, including 537 PSMA-expressing scans) comprising pre- and post-therapeutic PCa settings  13 , and a prospective multicentre Australian trial (ANZCTR: ACTRN12615000608561; date registered: 11th June 2015; n = 438 scans) enrolling post-prostatectomy or radiotherapy BCR patients, predominantly with negative or oligometastatic disease (≤ 3 lesions)  14  .

Internal and external testing datasets were acquired from different sources with reference annotations, presenting diverse radiotracer, scanner type, date range, and disease characteristics. 

Internal testing data comprised 126 patients (n = 199 scans) obtained from previous, single-centre studies at Sir Charles Gairdner Hospital. Metastatic PCa patients were imaged between November 2019 and October 2025 using [ 68 Ga]Ga-PSMA-11 (n = 170) or [ 18 F]PSMA-1007 ( n = 29). Additionally, 18 patients undertook a test-retest scan as part of a prospective trial ( n = 36)  15 .

External testing data of 100 patients (n = 100 scans) were obtained from the open-source DEEP-PSMA dataset comprising mCRPC patients undergoing [ 177 Lu]Lu-PSMA-617 therapy and imaged using [ 68 Ga]Ga-PSMA-11 between August 2015 and June 2017  16 .

# 2.2 Model Training 

The segmentation network integrated into PSMASegmentator was trained using a variant of the nnU-Net V2 framework 

> 17

, which predicts organ segmentations alongside lesions to help reduce the inclusion of physiological tracer uptake. The large 3D fullres con fi guration was used, with all images resampled to 2×4.07×4.07 mm 3, z-score normalized, and percentile clipped at 0.5% and 99.5% to ensure stable, consistent training. The 1,035 training cases were split into  fi ve Cross-Validation (CV) folds at the patient-level to enable robust model experimentation and ensemble prediction  18 . Each fold was trained for 1500 epochs, with a batch size of 2 and patch size of 128x288x128, the latter parameter maximising anatomical context, hence, semantic segmentation ability  17 . Training used four RTX 4090 GPUs with a PyTorch implementation  19 . Model initialization details are provided in Section S1.2. 

# 2.3 PSMASegmentator Pipeline 

Figure  1 provides an overview of the PSMASegmentator tool ( https://github.com/UWA-Medical-Physics-Research-Group/PSMASegmentator ). The software accepts PET/CT inputs in DICOM or NIfTI formats, with PET components converted to SUVbw, and CT components resampled to PET resolution. See Sections S1.3-4 for complete pre-processing and inference information. Post-processing of the output segmentations comprises SUV thresholding, Radiotherapy Structure (RTSTRUCT) conversion (via Plastimatch  20 ), organ segmentation generation (via TotalSegmentator  21 ), segmentation expansion, lesion and liver disease classi fi cation, and biomarker extraction. 

> Page 5/20

The segmentation expansion algorithm implements Connected Component Analysis (CCA)-based  22  region growing using adaptive SUV thresholds and liver reference uptake (according to aPROMISE  23 , via a Gaussian Mixture Model) or uptake in the ascending aorta (c.f. coPERCIST  24 , via the IQR mean). A triple-constraint algorithm guides the expansion according to: (i) no crossing of organ boundaries; (ii) maximum volume increase by a factor of 3.5; (iii) overlap with contiguous uptake areas de fi ned by watershed  fi ltering using the 99.2nd percentile. These constraints interact to prevent runaway expansion along physiological uptake paths, particularly in the vicinity of the liver, kidneys, and small bowel. The use of watershed  fi ltering was inspired by Jackson et al.  8 to stabilize expansion along connected, anatomically relevant uptake. A grid search was performed to tune the local threshold ratio, expansion factor, and watershed percentile values, with evaluation performed on the CV folds. 

The lesion classi fi cation step assigns each segmented lesion to one of the 117 organ labels provided by TotalSegmentator, or as a nodal lesion. These classi fi cations are further grouped into whole body, bone, prostate, visceral, and, for nodal lesions, above/below the common iliac bifurcation. In lieu of a robust, open-source tool for nodal segmentation, a lesion is classi fi ed as nodal if < 50% of its volume overlaps with any single organ. This threshold accounts for both the diffuse morphology of nodal lesions and uncertainties in organ segmentation. It was empirically determined using the locally curated, lesion-labelled subset of the training data (n = 438)  14 . A threshold of 50% yielded the highest classi fi cation accuracy (89.2%) when evaluated across thresholds ranging from 0% to 80%. 

This classi fi cation algorithm was utilised when assessing the AI model’s ability to detect liver disease, given its signi fi cance as a biomarker of poor prognosis  25 . The patient-level detection of liver metastases in the AI segmentations was compared with reference cases using sensitivity, precision, and F1-score. 

After post-processing, the following biomarkers are extracted per-scan, informed primarily by SPARC guidelines and existing de fi nitions  10 ,26 –28 : TLC, TTV, SUVmean, SUVmax, Total Lesion Uptake ( TLU = TTV×SUVmean ), Total Lesion Quotient ( TLQ = TTV/SUVmean ), and the presence of bone, visceral, or liver metastases. This information is provided in CSV summaries for reporting and survival analysis. 

A fast version of the model has also been developed, using a shallower architecture with three stages compared to six in the full model, where each stage represents a resolution level in the encoder-decoder hierarchy, progressively downsampling spatial resolution while increasing feature channels. The fast model further reduces complexity through fewer feature channels per stage (16–64 vs 32–320) and fewer residual blocks, resulting in an approximately 150-fold reduction in model size. Inference is also performed with a smaller patch size of 64×128×64 (vs 128×288×128). As a result, this model produces segmentations approximately eight times faster than the standard model and can run on personal devices (benchmarked on a 2020 M1 MacBook). This model version is accessible via a ‘fast’  fl ag passed by the user, which further speeds up tool usage by disabling Test-Time Augmentation (TTA) and using the fast version of TotalSegmentator. 

# 2.4 Assessment of Segmentation Performance 

Using physician-derived segmentations as the reference standard, agreement with the AI segmentations was categorised as True Positives (TPs), False Positives (FPs), and False Negatives (FNs) at the voxel and lesion levels. Sensitivity, precision, F1 score, and DSC were calculated per-case and are reported using mean and standard deviation (SD). Median and inter-quartile range (IQR) are reported for DSC to account for its skewed distribution. Performance was strati fi ed by tracer type ( 68 Ga or  18 F), median physician-derived TTV, and data source, with the Mann-Whitney U test used to assess group differences. At the lesion level, metrics were calculated both per case (averaged across cases) and, for sensitivity and precision, also computed globally (i.e., once using all lesions pooled across cases).

For the AI- and physician-derived biomarkers, agreement plots were constructed, and correlation was assessed via Pearson and Spearman coe ffi cients. Intraclass correlation coe ffi cients (ICC) are reported as ICC(2,1) (two-way random-effects, single-measure) and interpreted according to established guidelines (poor < 0.50, moderate 0.50–0.75, good 0.75–0.90, excellent > 0.90)  29 .

Compared with analysis performed without segmentation expansion, the expanded test set showed substantially higher per-case voxel-level DSC (0.681 vs. 0.570), driven primarily by increased sensitivity (0.615 vs. 0.453) with a slight reduction in precision (0.853 vs. 0.894). Signi fi cant intra-group differences (p < 0.05) were observed for lesion-level sensitivity and precision, but not for F1 score. Signi fi cant differences in DSC were not observed between internal and external data sources. 

Cases acquired using  68 Ga and higher TTV displayed better segmentation performance, while greater variability was observed for  18 F and lower TTV cases, though estimates for  18 F-based cases are less stable due to the smaller sample size (n = 29). Figure  2 provides examples of excellent and poor agreement between AI and physician segmentations. 

Average times for segmentation varied from 0.5-2 min per-scan depending on the model (full or fast) and computing resources (GPU or CPU), excluding use of the full model on non-GPU setups, given its untenability on such devices. 

Comparing the fast model’s performance, at the lesion level, mean F1-score was 0.731 versus 0.800 for the full model, with precision slightly improved (0.894 vs 0.864) but reduced sensitivity (0.660 vs 0.790). At the voxel level, DSC was modestly lower (0.634 vs 0.681), with similar precision (0.867 vs 0.853) but reduced sensitivity (0.546 vs 0.615). These 

fi ndings indicate that the fast model preserves high precision with a moderate reduction in sensitivity, providing a reasonable trade-off between performance and computational e ffi ciency. Table S2 provides complete metrics for the fast model. 

Regarding patient-level liver disease detection across the testing data, the AI model demonstrated moderate sensitivity (0.59) and high precision (0.89), resulting in an overall F1-score of 0.71. Speci fi city was near perfect (0.99), with a negative predictive value of 0.96, indicating very low false-positive rates and strong ability to exclude liver disease. Performance on external data showed comparatively higher sensitivity (0.75) and F1-score (0.86) with perfect precision. 

# 3.2 Biomarker Correlation and Agreement 

Figure  3 shows strong correlation between AI- and physician-derived biomarkers (Spearman ρ > 0.9), with TLU demonstrating the highest correlation (ρ = 0.963). SUVmean showed evidence of proportional bias, with increasing overestimation by the AI at higher values. ICC values ranged from 0.769 (SUVmean) to 0.971 (SUVmax), showing good to excellent agreement. Figures S2 and S3 provide boxplots comparing TLC count differences and Bland-Altman plots for each biomarker, respectively. 

# 3.3. Survival Analysis 

From Table  4, all AI-derived biomarkers were signi fi cantly associated with outcome in univariable Cox analyses. Notably, patients with ≥ 20 lesions had a markedly increased risk of death (HR 5.95, 95% CI 3.95–8.98) compared to those with < 20 lesions. Tumour burden metrics also displayed strong prognostic potential, with TTV associated with a 52% increase in hazard per 100 mL (HR 1.53, 95% CI 1.41–1.65). SUV-based and composite biomarkers (SUVmax, SUVmean, TLU, TLQ) likewise demonstrated consistent associations with OS. # 4. Discussion 

This work demonstrates that openly available, AI-driven auto-segmentation can support the implementation of SPARC-recommended PSMA PET/CT biomarkers at scale, facilitating reproducible prognostic assessment across varying PCa settings. PSMASegmentator, an end-to-end software pipeline, can receive heterogeneous PSMA PET/CT inputs, provide automatic segmentations, and compute consensus biomarkers, including TTV, TLC, SUV metrics, and derivatives. Importantly, we show that these biomarkers were not only concordant with physician annotations, but also validated for prognostic signi fi cance in a large, multi-centre cohort. This addresses SPARC’s call for standardised, accessible tools that support outcome-linked reporting. 

Building upon the work of Rokuss et al.  17 , our study expanded the training task to segment 14 additional organs relevant to physiological tracer uptake and leveraged a larger, PSMA-speci fi c training dataset. Although cross-validation DSC was not improved relative to previous AutoPET-III challenge submissions  17 ,31 ,32 , evaluation on a large heterogeneous test cohort (n = 299), including independent external validation, demonstrated robust generalisation. 

While our testing DSC was better or comparable to previous studies  9,11 , it remained lower than the 0.95 reported by Jackson et al.  8, who utilized a dataset drawn from trial-based cohorts, with standardised acquisition protocols and a threshold-based annotation framework for the physician segmentations. In contrast, our testing cohort encompassed substantial heterogeneity in acquisition protocols, disease burden, tracers, and scanner types, likely increasing the di ffi culty of the segmentation task. Future prospective validation across additional institutions, disease states, and balanced radiotracer distributions is warranted to further establish the robustness of PSMASegmentator in routine clinical deployment. 

Our reported lesion-level sensitivity remains comparable in the context of heterogeneous data, exceeding the collated averages of previous studies by approximately 2%  9,12 . The observed disparity relative to lesion‑level precision, also reported by Kendrick et al.  9, is largely attributable to clinical segmentation conventions, and motivated the post‑processing expansion step to better align AI and physician methodologies. As all physician annotations used threshold‑based methods with manual re fi nement, they tended to merge contiguous uptake regions. In contrast, our 

> Page 11/20

model often subdivides such regions into multiple delineations, generally resulting in higher TLC. This re fl ects the inherent instability of discrete lesion enumeration, and aligns with SPARC, which recognises TLC as a setting-dependent, strati fi ed variable, particularly outside the BCR setting. 

The high correlations and ICCs demonstrate that AI-derived biomarkers can be derived automatically with close agreement to physician-derived measurements, despite systematic under-segmentation. Although voxel-level overlap impacts biomarker estimates, these results indicate that robust biomarker quanti fi cation can be achieved without perfect spatial agreement, which is su ffi cient for standardised reporting. AI-derived biomarkers were strongly associated with survival, corroborating their clinical relevance  26 ,27  and utility in standardised reporting. Established SPARC-recommended metrics demonstrated consistent prognostic value, reinforcing their signi fi cance in risk strati fi cation. 

In addition, the composite biomarkers of TLU and TLQ demonstrated promising prognostic associations, suggesting that integrating volumetric and uptake-based information may enhance risk strati fi cation  5,10 . The utility of these measures likely arises from capturing complementary aspects of disease burden and PSMA expression not re fl ected by either parameter alone  6. While higher PSMA expression is generally associated with more aggressive disease, exceptions exist in dedifferentiated states  10 ,14 ,16 . Although not currently included in SPARC recommendations, these  fi ndings highlight composite biomarkers as candidates for further validation. In this context, PSMASegmentator provides a scalable framework for hypothesis generation, enabling systematic evaluation of emerging biomarkers across diverse disease settings. 

Key limitations of this study include its retrospective design, inter‑dataset heterogeneity, instability of lesion‑level metrics arising from annotation conventions, and limited evaluation of rarer metastatic sites. We also note that survival validation was limited to univariable analyses for assessing prognostic association rather than establishing independent prognostic value. Future work should quantify test–retest repeatability and longitudinal biomarker stability, including lesion-level consistency assessed via lesion matching, particularly for applications in treatment response assessment and RECIP-based response criteria  33 . Further investigation of adaptive post-processing strategies, including organ- and lesion-speci fi c segmentation expansion, may improve voxel-level agreement while maintaining lesion-level performance. Notwithstanding these limitations, PSMASegmentator provides a robust foundation for AI‑enabled standardisation of biomarker extraction, quantitative reporting, and risk strati fi cation, with potential for future integration into prospective trials and collaborative initiatives  34 .

In summary, this work demonstrates that AI‑based PSMA‑PET auto‑segmentation can reliably support the standardised extraction of prognostically signi fi cant biomarkers. Strong agreement with physician‑derived metrics and effective prognostic performance, despite heterogeneous data, demonstrates the clinical utility of the extracted biomarker information. Both established and composite biomarkers displayed meaningful survival associations, supporting the broader utility of automated biomarker extraction for standardised, quantitative risk strati fi cation in large cohorts. Our broader contribution is the release of an openly available, generalisable tool for analysis across diverse research and clinical settings. By generating reproducible segmentations from heterogeneous PSMA PET/CT inputs, PSMASegmentator enables derivation of a range of voxel- and lesion-level biomarkers beyond the SPARC-endorsed metrics validated here. This provides a foundation for exploratory biomarker discovery and hypothesis generation. As such, the value of the tool lies not only in enabling consensus biomarker extraction, but also in lowering the barrier to scalable analysis across registries and in future clinical trials. 

# 5. Conclusion 

This study presents and validates PSMASegmentator, an open-source software for automated PSMA PET/CT segmentation, enabling reproducible derivation of prognostically signi fi cant imaging biomarkers with close agreement to 

> Page 12/20

physician-derived values. By supporting standardised quantitative reporting, the tool directly aligns with SPARC recommendations, demonstrating strong biomarker agreement with expert reference standards and robust prognostic performance in a large, multi‑centre cohort. The open‑source availability of the software (available at: 

https://github.com/UWA-Medical-Physics-Research-Group/PSMASegmentator ) facilitates its use in prospective studies and collaboration in future biomarker studies, providing a scalable foundation for harmonised biomarker implementation in the advancement of precision medicine initiatives.
