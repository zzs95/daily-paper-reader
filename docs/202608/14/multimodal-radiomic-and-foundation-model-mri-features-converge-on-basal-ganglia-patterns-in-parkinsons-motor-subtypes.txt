ABSTRACT 

Parkinson’s disease (PD) comprises heterogeneous motor phenotypes, most prominently tremor-dominant (TD) and postural instability/gait difficulty (PIGD), which differ in progression, disability burden, and therapeutic response. Despite standardized Movement Disorder Society–Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (MDS-UPDRS) criteria, subtype classification remains partly subjective and lacks clear biological anchoring. In this pilot study, we investigated whether multimodal MRI-derived representations can discriminate TD and PIGD phenotypes by directly comparing conventional whole-brain radiomic features with latent embeddings extracted from BrainIAC, a transformer-based MRI foundation model. Sixty-two age- and sex-matched PD patients (31 TD, 31 PIGD) underwent 3T MRI including T1-weighted, FLAIR, SWI, and diffusion imaging (FA and MD). For each modality, 1046 radiomic features and 2048-dimensional embeddings were extracted. Within a nested stratified five-fold cross-validation framework, a group of machine learning models were trained using imaging (radiomics/embeddings) features alone, clinical scores alone, and their combination, with fold-wise standardization and principal component analysis (80% variance retained) for a TD vs PIGD classification task. Radiomic features consistently outperformed foundation-model embeddings (mean AUC: 0.664 vs 0.592), with the best performance achieved by combining FLAIR and SWI radiomics with clinical scores (AUC = 0.79 ± 0.10). Interpretability analyses indicated that discriminative components were driven mainly by SWI-derived intensity and texture features, with saliency maps highlighting striatal and pallidal regions within basal ganglia circuitry. 

Keywords : tremor dominant PD, PIGD PD, multimodal MRI, foundation model, radiomics 

1. INTRODUCTION 

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder affecting over 10 million people worldwide, with prevalence rising and incidence increasing with age 1. Beyond its cardinal motor manifestations, including tremor, rigidity, bradykinesia, gait impairment, and postural instability, PD encompasses a broad spectrum of non-motor symptoms that contribute substantially to disability and reduced quality of life 2. Clinically, PD is commonly stratified into motor subtypes, most notably tremor-dominant (TD) and postural instability/gait difficulty (PIGD). These phenotypes differ in disease trajectory, therapeutic responsiveness, and overall prognosis, with PIGD patients typically experiencing faster progression and greater functional impairment 3. Despite the use of standardized Movement Disorder Society–Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (MDS-UPDRS) criteria, subtype classification remains partially subjective and may not fully reflect underlying biological heterogeneity 4. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) provides a non-invasive window into structural, microstructural, and susceptibility-related brain alterations in PD. Prior neuroimaging studies have reported subtype-related differences in cortical atrophy, white-matter integrity, and functional connectivity, particularly involving motor and cognitive networks 5–7 . Diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) studies have revealed microstructural alterations associated with motor severity 8, while susceptibility-weighted imaging (SWI) has demonstrated abnormal iron accumulation within basal ganglia structures, a hallmark of PD pathophysiology 9,10 . Such findings suggest that subtype-specific differences may be embedded in multimodal MRI contrasts reflecting tissue integrity, iron deposition, and microstructural heterogeneity. Traditional neuroimaging analyses often rely on region-of-interest–based volumetry or low-dimensional handcrafted features, which may overlook richer signal patterns distributed across the whole brain. Radiomics offers a complementary, high-throughput framework

By converting images into high-dimensional feature spaces, radiomics enables data-driven characterization of subtle disease phenotypes. Recent studies have shown that radiomic features derived from structural and diffusion MRI can discriminate PD motor subtypes 8, highlighting the potential of quantitative imaging biomarkers. In parallel, foundation models based on transformer architecture have emerged as powerful tools for representation learning in medical imaging. Pretrained on large-scale datasets, these models aim to capture generalized, semantically meaningful latent representations that can be transferred to downstream clinical tasks 13 . In neuroimaging, transformer-based models have demonstrated promising performance in structural analysis and disease classification tasks. BrainIAC 14 , a transformer-based MRI foundation model, generates high-dimensional embeddings encoding multi-scale spatial dependencies across the whole brain without predefined regions of interest. However, whether such generalized embeddings can capture fine-grained phenotypic distinctions, such as PD motor subtypes, remains unknown. To date, no study has directly compared whole-brain radiomic features and foundation-model embeddings for the classification of PD motor phenotypes. Moreover, the relative contribution of multimodal MRI sequences, particularly susceptibility-sensitive SWI and tissue-sensitive FLAIR, within these representation paradigms remains unclear. In this preliminary study, we systematically evaluate whether latent representations derived from BrainIAC can discriminate TD and PIGD phenotypes and directly compare their performance against whole-brain radiomics across multiple MRI modalities (T1, FLAIR, SWI, FA, MD). Using a nested cross-validation framework and multimodal feature ablation analysis, we further assess the added value of clinical variables and dimensionality reduction. By integrating interpretable radiomic descriptors with emerging foundation-model embeddings, this work aims to clarify which MRI-derived representations most effectively capture subtype-specific neurobiological signatures in Parkinson’s disease. 2. RESULTS 2.1 Participants 

Eighty-eight consecutive patients with idiopathic Parkinson’s disease (36 TD, 52 PIGD) were recruited at the Institute of Neurology, University “Magna Graecia” of Catanzaro, Italy and included to the study. To minimize demographic confounding, an age- and sex-matched subset was constructed using nearest-neighbor matching without replacement, based exclusively on age and sex. The final matched cohort included N = 31 patients for each group (balanced TD and PIGD groups). The TD and PIGD groups were comparable in terms of age, age at disease onset, disease duration, Hoehn and Yahr stage, and MDS-UPDRS III scores, with no statistically significant between-group differences observed for demographic or clinical severity measures (Table 1). Matching was performed prior to machine learning analysis to ensure balanced group representation. Subtype assignment followed established clinical ratios of tremor to PIGD symptoms, ensuring phenotypic consistency 15 . Clinical variables included Hoehn & Yahr stage, MDS-UPDRS III, disease onset, age, and disease duration. All procedures involving human participants were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional research committee and with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The average AUC values across all configurations were 0.664 and 0.592 for radiomics and embeddings, respectively, corresponding to an average performance gain of approximately +0.07 AUC units in favor of radiomics. Configuration-wise comparison further confirmed this trend. Radiomics achieved higher AUC values in 83% of modality combinations, indicating systematic superiority rather than isolated best-case performance. Despite quantitative differences, both methods converged toward the same most informative MRI modality combinations. For both pipelines, the highest-performing configuration involved the combination of FLAIR and SWI with clinical scores: BrainIAC (FLAIR+SWI+scores): 0.734 ± 0.159 and Radiomics (FLAIR+SWI+scores): 0.792 ± 0.101. FLAIR consistently emerged as the strongest single modality in radiomics and was part of the top-performing combinations in BrainIAC. Similarly, SWI contributed most strongly when combined with other modalities, particularly in the FLAIR + SWI configuration. This convergence suggests that the discriminative information was partly modality-dependent rather than specific to a single feature representation. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously, as they do not establish that the identified MRI contrasts are definitive biological markers of TD and PIGD subtypes. Rather, they indicate that FLAIR- and SWI-derived representations may capture complementary imaging patterns associated with clinically defined motor phenotypes. 3. DISCUSSION 

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder affecting over 10 million people worldwide, presenting with both motor and non-motor symptoms. Clinically, PD is commonly divided into tremor-dominant and postural instability/gait difficulty subtypes, the latter showing faster progression and greater disability. Despite standardized scales, subtype classification still relies on expert-based MDS-UPDRS scoring, which is subjective and often inconsistent 4. In this study, we directly compared two distinct MRI representation paradigms, whole-brain radiomics and transformer-based foundation-model embeddings, to determine their ability to discriminate TD and PIGD phenotypes and to assess whether they converge on shared neurobiological patterns. Across modalities, radiomic features consistently outperformed BrainIAC latent embeddings in classification performance. Using only clinical scores yielded an AUC of 0.647 ± 0.105, whereas radiomic features alone achieved 0.712 ± 0.193. The highest performance was observed for the FLAIR + SWI + clinical scores configuration after PCA (AUC = 0.79 ±

These findings suggest that handcrafted radiomic descriptors, which explicitly quantify intensity distribution, texture heterogeneity, and shape characteristics, may better capture subtle subtype-specific signal variations than generalized foundation-model embeddings. Moreover, these findings align with the conceptual foundations of radiomics, which explicitly quantify intensity distribution, spatial heterogeneity, and morphological patterns 11,12 . In small-to-moderate sample clinical datasets, such engineered descriptors may retain sensitivity to fine-grained phenotypic variance that generalized foundation embeddings, trained for broad representational robustness, may attenuate 16 . Although transformer-based foundation models have demonstrated strong performance in large-scale medical imaging tasks 13 , their effectiveness for subtle subtype discrimination without task-specific fine-tuning remains under investigation 17 . Importantly, dimensionality reduction played a critical role: PCA-enhanced models were frequently among the top performers, indicating that isolating orthogonal signal components helped suppress noise and redundancy inherent in high-dimensional radiomic spaces. Within the best-performing FLAIR + SWI + scores model, the third principal component (PC3) emerged as the dominant discriminator between TD and PIGD. Notably, PC3 loadings were driven almost exclusively by SWI-derived radiomic features, including first-order intensity, texture, and shape descriptors, before FLAIR-derived ones. The relevance of FLAIR in the best-performing models may reflect sensitivity to tissue and white-matter abnormalities that are not fully captured by T1-weighted structural imaging. FLAIR suppresses cerebrospinal fluid signal and enhances the visibility of periventricular and deep white-matter hyperintensities, gliotic changes, and other nonspecific tissue abnormalities 18 . In Parkinson’s disease, such changes may be clinically relevant because white-matter burden and vascular-related alterations can contribute to gait impairment, postural instability, executive dysfunction, and reduced cognitive-motor integration 19 . Therefore, the contribution of FLAIR-derived radiomic features may reflect not only local signal abnormalities but also broader network vulnerability relevant to the PIGD phenotype 20 . The contribution of SWI is biologically complementary. SWI is sensitive to magnetic susceptibility effects, including iron-related contrast, which is highly relevant to Parkinsonian neurodegeneration 21 . In the present study, the most discriminative PCA component in the FLAIR + SWI + clinical scores model was driven predominantly by SWI-derived first-order intensity and texture features. These features may reflect susceptibility-related signal differences and microstructural heterogeneity within nigrostriatal and basal ganglia pathways. Linking these findings to nigrostriatal degeneration, SWI-derived radiomic patterns may capture downstream effects of dopaminergic neuronal loss, iron accumulation, and altered striato-pallidal circuitry that differ across TD and PIGD phenotypes 22,23 . Taken together, the combined relevance of FLAIR and SWI suggests that TD versus PIGD discrimination may depend on complementary MRI contrasts: FLAIR-sensitive white-matter and vascular-related tissue burden, and SWI-sensitive susceptibility changes related to nigrostriatal and basal ganglia degeneration. More broadly, the biological interpretation of multimodal radiomic features requires caution because radiomics quantify mathematical properties of image intensity, texture, and shape rather than direct histopathological processes. Nevertheless, the modality-specific origin of these features may provide indirect information about complementary aspects of PD-related tissue heterogeneity. FLAIR-derived radiomic features may reflect variation in tissue signal related to white-matter hyperintensities, periventricular or deep white-matter abnormalities, gliosis, vascular-related burden, or other nonspecific tissue changes. T1-weighted radiomic features, although not dominant in the best-performing model, may capture structural tissue contrast, local morphology, cortical or subcortical atrophy, and gray–white matter organization, thereby reflecting more global neurodegenerative or morphometric differences between TD and PIGD phenotypes. SWI-derived features may provide complementary susceptibility-sensitive information, potentially related to iron-sensitive contrast or microstructural heterogeneity within basal ganglia circuits. Thus, multimodal radiomics should be interpreted as capturing complementary MRI-visible tissue properties rather than a single PD-specific mechanism. These associations remain indirect and exploratory, and future studies combining radiomics with quantitative MRI, regional volumetry, diffusion metrics, connectivity analyses, or histopathological validation will be required to define their biological specificity. Although ROI-based saliency quantification did not yield statistically significant differences after FDR correction, consistent directional trends emerged across SWI and FLAIR analyses. Both modalities highlighted striatal and

For SWI, PIGD patients showed higher mean saliency within caudate/accumbens and thalamic regions, whereas TD patients exhibited relatively more spatially distributed high-intensity saliency within the globus pallidus externa. In FLAIR, TD patients demonstrated greater focal saliency in the caudate/accumbens, while PIGD patients showed broader spatial saliency within the putamen. Although these regional effects did not reach statistical significance, effect sizes were coherent across complementary metrics (mean intensity, top 5% mass, and spatial extent), and importantly, converged on basal ganglia circuitry. This anatomical consistency across radiomics and embedding-derived saliency suggests that the discriminative signal is biologically grounded rather than model-specific. This regional emphasis is consistent with prior subtype-differentiation studies demonstrating structural, diffusion, and functional connectivity differences within basal ganglia–thalamo-cortical networks 5,24 . More recent diffusion and resting-state connectivity studies have similarly reported altered network segregation and motor circuit integration across PD subtypes, particularly within striatal and pallidal pathways 25–27 . The convergence between radiomic discriminative features and embedding-derived saliency localization strengthens the biological plausibility of basal ganglia involvement rather than suggesting method-specific artifacts. A key contribution of this work lies in integrating two interpretability frameworks. Radiomics provides quantifiable, statistically testable feature descriptors that facilitate structured inference, whereas saliency maps preserve voxel-level spatial localization derived from deep representations. While whole-brain radiomic aggregation sacrifices explicit spatial mapping, saliency overlays enable anatomical visualization of subtype-relevant regions. The convergence between radiomics-driven performance and saliency-driven regional emphasis strengthens confidence in the robustness of the observed basal ganglia patterns. Rather than competing approaches, radiomics and embedding-based saliency offer complementary perspectives on MRI-derived subtype discrimination. However, rather than indicating basal ganglia involvement in a generic sense, the present findings may be interpreted within the framework of partially segregated cortico-striato-pallido-thalamo-cortical loops. The motor circuit, including sensorimotor striatal territories such as the posterior putamen, pallidal output nuclei, thalamus, supplementary motor area, and primary motor regions, is directly relevant to gait impairment, postural instability, and axial motor dysfunction. Accordingly, the broader putaminal and pallidal saliency trends observed in PIGD patients may be consistent with greater involvement of sensorimotor basal ganglia circuitry 28 . In contrast, saliency trends involving the caudate/accumbens region may point toward associative or limbic-associative striatal territories, which interact with prefrontal, cingulate, and limbic networks 29 . These circuits are increasingly recognized as relevant to the cognitive-motor integration and network-level dysfunction that may distinguish PD motor phenotypes. From this perspective, the observed distribution of discriminative signal across putaminal, pallidal, thalamic, and caudate/accumbens regions may reflect not only basal ganglia involvement per se, but differential weighting of sensorimotor versus associative basal ganglia loops across TD and PIGD phenotypes. However, this interpretation remains exploratory. Future studies should therefore combine radiomic or foundation-model representations with connectivity-based basal ganglia parcellation, diffusion tractography, or resting-state functional connectivity to directly test whether TD and PIGD phenotypes differ in sensorimotor versus associative loop involvement. 

3.1 Study limitations 

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, this study should be interpreted as a pilot and exploratory analysis. Although the cohort was balanced and nested cross-validation was used to reduce overfitting, the limited sample size and absence of an independent external validation cohort restrict statistical power and generalizability. Therefore, the reported AUC values should be considered internal cross-validation estimates rather than externally validated measures of clinical performance. Second, TD and PIGD subtypes are clinically useful but symptom-based constructs that may vary with medication state, disease stage, symptom fluctuation, and longitudinal progression. Thus, the MRI-derived patterns reported here should be interpreted as preliminary imaging correlates of clinically defined phenotypes rather than definitive biological anchors of fixed motor subtypes. Third, the comparison between radiomics and BrainIAC reflects engineered radiomic descriptors versus off-the-shelf foundation-model embeddings, not intrinsic superiority of radiomics over foundation models. BrainIAC was not specifically trained or fine-tuned for 

ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT ARTICLE IN PRESS ARTICLE IN PRESS Parkinson’s disease motor subtype classification, and its lower performance may reflect limited task adaptation or domain mismatch. Finally, saliency analyses should be interpreted cautiously, as ROI-based comparisons did not survive correction for multiple comparisons and effect sizes were small to moderate. We acknowledge that whole-brain radiomic extraction may dilute region-specific effects, particularly in PD, where subtype-relevant pathology is expected to involve nigrostriatal and basal ganglia circuits. However, the use of a whole-brain mask was motivated by the comparative design of the study: BrainIAC embeddings are generated as global whole-brain representations, and applying radiomics to selected ROIs would have introduced a different spatial input definition, limiting the interpretability of direct AUC comparisons between radiomics and foundation-model embeddings. Therefore, the whole-brain radiomic analysis should be interpreted as a global representation-level comparison. Anatomical specificity was instead explored through saliency-based localization and ROI-wise saliency quantification, which highlighted striatal, pallidal, and thalamic regions in an exploratory manner. We finally acknowledge the inclusion of MDS-UPDRS III scores within the ablation study which may introduce partial circularity given their relationship to motor subtype classification. 

3.2 Conclusions 

This pilot study provides encouraging evidence that multimodal MRI contains subtype-relevant information capable of partially separating clinically defined TD and PIGD phenotypes. Among the evaluated representations, engineered radiomic features derived from FLAIR and SWI showed the strongest classification performance, suggesting that tissue-sensitive and susceptibility-sensitive MRI contrasts may capture complementary aspects of Parkinson’s disease motor heterogeneity. PCA further highlighted SWI-driven components as important contributors to discrimination, supporting the relevance of susceptibility-related signal variation in this context. Although off-the-shelf BrainIAC embeddings performed less strongly than radiomic features in the present setting, their convergence with radiomics toward similar informative MRI contrasts suggests that foundation-model representations may still provide useful complementary information. Future task-specific fine-tuning or domain adaptation may further enhance their sensitivity to subtle PD motor subtype differences. Similarly, while saliency analyses did not reveal statistically significant regional differences after correction, the observed spatial trends across SWI and FLAIR were consistent with involvement of striatal and pallidal circuitry and provide a biologically plausible direction for future validation. Overall, these findings support the feasibility and potential value of multimodal MRI-based phenotyping in Parkinson’s disease. By combining interpretable engineered radiomic descriptors with emerging foundation-model representations, this work provides a promising framework for future studies aiming to develop more robust, biologically informed imaging markers of PD motor heterogeneity. 

4. METHODS 4.1 MRI Acquisition and Preprocessing 

All participants underwent 3T MRI on a 3T scanner. The acquisition protocol included: (i) three-dimensional T1-weighted MPRAGE imaging; (ii) T2-fluid attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR); (iii) echo-planar diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI); and (iv) multi-echo susceptibility-weighted imaging (SWI). The T1-weighted MPRAGE sequence was acquired with repetition time (TR) = 2300 ms, echo time (TE) = 2.34 ms, inversion time (TI) = 900 ms, acquisition time = 5:12 min, and voxel size = 1 × 1 × 1 mm. The FLAIR sequence was acquired with TR = 8000 ms, TE = 94 ms, TI = 2370 ms, acquisition time = 4:02 min, and voxel size = 0.4 × 0.4 × 0.3 mm. The DWI sequence was acquired with TR = 8900 ms, TE = 88 ms, acquisition time = 11:36 min, and voxel size = 2.5 × 2.5 × 2.3 mm. The multi-echo SWI sequence was acquired with TR = 50 ms and five echo times: TE1 = 5.95 ms, TE2 = 13.62 ms, TE3 = 21.62 ms, TE4 = 29.62 ms, and TE5 = 37.96 ms. SWI acquisition time was 6:23 min, with voxel size = 0.7 × 0.7 × 1.2 mm. The total scan duration was approximately 45 minutes. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Regional Ethics Committee of Calabria (protocol code 358/2020, date of approval 10/11/2020 and 107/2025, date of approval: 17/04/2025). Data preprocessing was performed using FSL (FMRIB Software Library). After preprocessing, whole-brain masks were generated from skull-stripped images using FSL-BET and visually inspected for gross segmentation errors. These masks were used for whole-brain radiomic feature extraction across modalities. All images were 

ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT ARTICLE IN PRESS ARTICLE IN PRESS rigidly registered to MNI152 space and resampled to 1 × 1 × 1 mm isotropic resolution before feature extraction. Fractional anisotropy (FA) and mean diffusivity (MD) maps were computed using standard diffusion tensor modeling. 

4.2 Radiomic Features and BrainIAC Embeddings 

Whole-brain radiomic features were extracted independently from each modality using the PyRadiomics library. A total of 1046 features per modality were computed, including first order, shape (2D), shape (3D), gray level cooccurrence matrix (GLCM), gray level size zone matrix (GLSZM), gray level run length matrix (GLRLM), neighboring gray tone difference matrix (NGTDM), and gray level dependence matrix (GLDM). Feature extraction was performed within the whole-brain mask to ensure methodological comparability with the global foundation-model embeddings. BrainIAC generates whole-brain latent representations without predefined anatomical regions of interest; therefore, radiomic features were also extracted from whole-brain masks so that both representation strategies were based on the same spatial input definition. This choice was made to enable a fair comparison between radiomics and foundation-model embeddings, rather than to imply that PD motor-subtype pathology is uniformly distributed across the brain. The radiomics workflow is summarized in Supplementary Figure S2. In parallel, each MRI modality was independently processed using BrainIAC. BrainIAC was used as an off-the-shelf pretrained foundation-model feature extractor. It is a self-supervised brain MRI foundation model designed to learn general-purpose representations from unlabeled structural MRI data 14 . In the present study, pretrained BrainIAC weights were used without task-specific fine-tuning for Parkinson’s disease motor subtype classification. Each modality was encoded into a 2048-dimensional latent representation capturing multi-scale spatial dependencies across the whole brain volume without predefined regions of interest. For multimodal experiments, features from the selected modalities were concatenated, and clinical variables were added when applicable. 

4.3 Feature Standardization and Dimensionality reduction 

Within each training fold features were z-scored using mean and standard deviation computed exclusively on training data, the same parameters were then applied to the corresponding held-out test fold. Because the number of imaging features exceeded the number of subjects, dimensionality reduction was incorporated to reduce the risk of overfitting. PCA was evaluated as an experimental preprocessing factor rather than applied as a fixed step to all models. For each imaging modality or modality combination, classification was performed under two conditions: (i) without PCA, in which z-scored features were entered directly into the classifier, and (ii) with PCA, in which principal components retaining 80% of the explained variance were used as classifier input. In the PCA condition, PCA was fitted exclusively on the training data within each cross-validation fold, and the learned projection was then applied to the corresponding held-out test fold. Thus, the held-out outer test data were never used for feature standardization, PCA fitting, dimensionality reduction, hyperparameter optimization, or model selection. In the best-performing FLAIR + SWI + clinical scores radiomics model, PCA reduced the input space to a mean of six components across outer folds, substantially lowering the effective dimensionality relative to the original feature set. In the non-PCA condition, only fold-wise z-score standardization was applied before classification. This design allowed direct assessment of whether dimensionality reduction improved model performance for each feature configuration while preserving strict separation between training and testing data. To further assess whether classification performance exceeded chance expectations, we performed a permutation-based null analysis. Class labels were randomly permuted 1000 times while preserving the complete nested cross-validation pipeline, including fold-wise standardization, PCA fitting, hyperparameter optimization, and outer-fold testing. Observed AUC values were compared against the empirical null distribution generated from the permuted-label analyses. 

4.4 Machine Learning Framework 

All supervised classification analyses were performed exclusively on the final matched cohort of 62 patients, including 31 TD and 31 PIGD patients. No additional subjects or external datasets were used for classifier training or testing. Model performance was estimated using nested stratified 5-fold cross-validation. In the outer loop, the 62 patients were split into five stratified folds. At each iteration, four folds were used for training/model selection and one fold was held out for testing. Because 62 is not exactly 

ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT ARTICLE IN PRESS ARTICLE IN PRESS divisible by five, the outer test folds contained either 12 or 13 patients, with corresponding training sets of 50 or 49 patients. Stratification was used to preserve the TD/PIGD class balance as closely as possible in each fold. Within each outer training set, an inner stratified cross-validation loop was used for hyperparameter tuning. Feature standardization, PCA fitting, and model selection were performed only within the training data of each fold, and the learned transformations and optimized models were then applied to the held-out outer test fold. Four supervised classifiers were evaluated: i) linear Support Vector Machine (SVM), ii) Random Forest (RF), iii) XGBoost (XGB), iv) Elastic-net Logistic Regression (LR), v) gradient boost classifier (GBC) and vi) K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN). Hyperparameter optimization was performed within a fully nested cross-validation framework. For each outer stratified fold, model selection and hyperparameter tuning were performed only on the corresponding training data using an inner stratified cross-validation loop. The optimized model was then evaluated on the held-out outer test fold. This procedure was applied consistently to all classifiers. To systematically evaluate modality contributions, an ablation study was conducted including single MRI modalities, all possible multimodal MRI combinations, imaging features alone, clinical features alone, combined imaging + clinical features, and configurations with and without PCA. The main focus of the study was the evaluation of MRI-derived radiomic and foundation-model representations. Clinical variables, including Hoehn & Yahr stage, MDS-UPDRS III, disease onset, and disease duration, were included as auxiliary contextual features in the multimodal models and as a clinical-only comparator, rather than as the primary focus of classification. This design allowed us to assess imaging-derived subtype-discriminative signal while also evaluating whether clinical information provided complementary support in a clinically informed multimodal setting. Because MDS-UPDRS III is related to the clinical motor features used to define TD and PIGD subtypes, combined imaging + clinical models were interpreted cautiously. Performance metrics, including AUC, accuracy, precision, and recall, were computed exclusively on outer test folds and averaged across folds. The overall experimental workflow is summarized in Figure 1. 

4.5 Interpretability analysis 

To investigate the contribution of individual features and spatial patterns to subtype discrimination, interpretability analyses were conducted separately for radiomic features and foundation-model embeddings. For radiomics-based models which included PCA, PCA loadings were examined for the best-performing multimodal configuration. The principal component most strongly associated with classifier decision weights was identified based on its contribution to the trained model. Feature loadings within this component were ranked to determine the dominant radiomic descriptors driving discrimination between TD and PIGD phenotypes. For BrainIAC-based models, gradient-based saliency maps were computed to identify spatial regions contributing most strongly to classification decisions. Individual saliency maps were visually inspected and qualitatively compared across subjects to identify consistent spatial patterns associated with TD and PIGD phenotypes. This dual interpretability approach allowed complementary assessment of (i) feature-level importance in radiomics-based models and (ii) spatial relevance patterns underlying foundation-model embeddings. In particular, to quantitatively assess spatial patterns underlying BrainIAC-based classification, saliency maps were analyzed within predefined subcortical regions of interest (ROIs) including amygdala, hippocampus, caudate/accumbens, putamen, pallidum, globus pallidus, and thalamus. For each subject and ROI, three complementary metrics were computed: (i) mean absolute saliency intensity, reflecting overall regional contribution; (ii) top 5% saliency mass, quantifying the cumulative intensity of the most influential voxels within the region; and (iii) the fraction of voxels exceeding the 95th percentile of the global saliency distribution across all ROIs, capturing the spatial extent of highly salient activations. Group differences between tremor-dominant and postural instability/gait difficulty patients were assessed using two-sided permutation testing (10,000 permutations) to accommodate the modest sample size. Effect sizes were quantified using Cohen’s d, and multiple comparisons across ROIs were controlled using false discovery rate (FDR) correction. This analysis enabled objective regional comparison of subtype-specific saliency patterns beyond qualitative visualization. To complement ROI-wise saliency averages, we also performed a spatial-consistency analysis of high-saliency voxels. For each subject, absolute saliency maps were thresholded at the 95th 

ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT ARTICLE IN PRESS ARTICLE IN PRESS percentile within the union of predefined subcortical ROIs, yielding subject-specific binary high-saliency maps. Voxel-wise frequency maps were then computed separately for TD and PIGD patients by averaging binary masks within each group. Spatial reproducibility was quantified using pairwise Dice and Jaccard overlap of high-saliency maps within each phenotype, as well as between-group overlap and soft overlap of group-level frequency maps. A permutation test (N = 5000) was used to assess whether within-group spatial consistency exceeded between-group overlap. Together, these analyses provided complementary ROI-level and spatial-overlap measures of saliency-map reproducibility beyond qualitative visualization.
