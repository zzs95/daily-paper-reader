Abstract 16 

Gastrointestinal (GI) diseases remain a significant global health burden due to elevated morbidity and mortal-17 

ity. Accurate and timely diagnosis, especially for small-bowel conditions, remains challenging because capsule 18 

endoscopy (CE) videos last 6–12 hours and require prolonged manual review. While artificial intelligence has 19 

advanced CE analysis, integrated video-to-report generation remains largely unexplored. We introduce CE Re-20 

porter, an explainable system transforming full-length CE videos into evidence-supported diagnostic reports 21 

through multi-task keyframe detection and time-aware vision-language alignment. CE Reporter was trained on 22 

MICS-CE, a multicenter, multi-device dataset comprising 1,002 video-report pairs, 109 million frames, 11,028 23 

video hours, and 29,338 expert-annotated keyframe-text pairs. The model performed strongly across three di-24 

mensions: (1) Report quality: exceeding existing multimodal large language models in linguistic coherence 25 

and diagnostic agreement (BLEU-ave: 0.573 vs. 0.523; small-intestine lesion F1-score: 0.79 vs. 0.58); (2) 26 

Clinician evaluation: achieving high correctness, completeness, and conciseness ratings, outperforming res-27 

ident clinicians while reducing reporting time by 91.9%; (3) Clinical utility: generating explainable reports 28 

supported by visual keyframes and maintaining competitive performance across centers and capsule devices 29 

(F1-score: 0.72–0.81). These findings establish a foundation for automated CE video-to-report generation and 30 

highlight its potential for efficient and transparent clinical workflows. 31

Introduction 32 

Gastrointestinal (GI) diseases remain a major global health challenge, affecting 2.86 billion people and caus-33 

ing approximately 8 million deaths annually 1. To address this challenge, capsule endoscopy (CE) offers a 34 

transformative approach for effective GI diagnostics, enabling non-invasive visualization of the entire diges-35 

tive tract, particularly the small intestine. It is highly effective in detecting pathologies such as obscure GI 36 

bleeding, Crohn’s disease, and small intestinal tumors 2, 3 . Despite its clinical value, manually reviewing CE 37 

videos exceeding 6 hours per examination requires approximately 1.5 hours, creating a time-consuming and 38 

labor-intensive workload for clinicians 4–6 . This workflow challenge also contributes to a miss rate of 17.4% 39 

in disease detection and an overall diagnostic miss rate of 10.8% 7. Consequently, integrating artificial intelli-40 

gence (AI) into the CE video analysis pipeline has become increasingly vital to enhance diagnostic efficiency 41 

and accuracy 8.42 

Current AI systems for CE video analysis primarily focus on vision-only tasks such as video summa-43 

rization (keyframe extraction) 9–11 , lesion classification 12–14 , polyp detection 15–17 , and lesion segmentation 44 

> 18–20

. However, automated report generation for CE videos, a routine clinical practice that provides compre-45 

hensive diagnostic summaries, remains largely unexplored. Unlike vision-only tasks, report generation offers 46 

a holistic diagnostic narrative that combines visual findings with contextual insights. This allows clinicians 47 

to convey nuanced observations and tailor recommendations to specific clinical needs 21 . In recent years, 48 

commercial multimodal large language models (MLLMs) (e.g., GPT-5, o3, Gemini-2.5-Pro, Grok4) 22–25 and 49 

open-source medical MLLMs (e.g., LLaVA-Med, BiomedGPT) 26–29 have revolutionized healthcare AI through 50 

vision-language alignment techniques, demonstrating exceptional capabilities in medical question answering, 51 

medical report generation, and patient dialogue systems 30–33 . Despite their strengths, these models are pri-52 

marily image-based and are therefore limited to keyframe-based report generation and unable to process long 53 

CE videos. To overcome this limitation, video MLLMs 34–36 have extended vision–language alignment to the 54 

temporal domain and demonstrated promising abilities in long video understanding. However, adapting these 55 

video MLLMs to CE report generation remains technically challenging. Firstly, current models struggle to 56 

process the lengthy duration of CE videos (approximately 60,000 frames) due to architectural limitations and 57 

computational constraints. For instance, models like MiniGPT4-Video 36 and LongVLM 35 can only handle 58 

segments up to 45 and 100 frames, respectively. Secondly, these models typically generate text-based reports 59 

without providing supporting visual evidence, such as key lesion images. These visual associations are crit-60 

ical for clinicians to validate diagnostic conclusions and enhance trust in automated systems 37, 38 . Beyond 61 

these limitations, the absence of comprehensive public datasets further limits progress in this field. Existing 62 

datasets, such as Kvasir Capsule 39 (117 videos) and KID 40 (47 videos), are limited in scale, do not cover 63 

the full GI tract, and contain mostly short videos (typically under 30 minutes). Furthermore, these datasets 64 

typically contain annotations such as lesion types or segmentation masks without diagnostic reports, which 65 

limit their application in automated report generation. 66 

In this study, to tackle the aforementioned challenges, we have established the first multi-source, compre-67 

hensive, and standardized CE video dataset, named MICS-CE , alongside a novel explainable video-to-report 68 

framework for CE, termed CE Reporter . Specifically, MICS-CE comprises 109 million frames across 11,028 69 

hours of CE videos, derived from 1,002 video-report pairs collected at four major clinical centers between 70 

August 2015 and October 2024. These videos span three commonly used capsule devices (MiroCam, OMOM, 71 

PillCam SB3), with individual video durations ranging from 1 hour 55 minutes to 14 hours 39 minutes (Fig. 1a). 72 

It features 29,338 gastroenterologist-annotated keyframe-text pairs across five GI regions with frame-level tem-73 

poral alignment. Additionally, this dataset features broad lesion diversity with 16 disease categories, ranging 74 

from common conditions (e.g., ulcers, erosions) to rare entities (e.g., parasites, lymphangiectasia). Each video 75 

is paired with a de-identified structured diagnostic report, featuring temporal segmentation of anatomical re-76

gions, key lesion frames, and a diagnostic summary. Building on this foundation, we have introduced CE 77 

Reporter, the first explainable system that transforms an entire CE video into an evidence-supported diagnos-78 

tic report (Fig. 1b). Our system integrated temporal continuity-constrained redundancy reduction, guided by 79 

feature similarity, to eliminate redundancy. Concurrently, a transformer-based multi-task framework detected 80 

diagnostic keyframes. By leveraging a time-aware vision-language model, CE Reporter generated context-rich 81 

descriptions for keyframes, which were then structured into a clinically coherent report through knowledge-82 

aware language synthesis. Distinct from prior methods that lack visual support, our approach directly embed-83 

ded keyframes into reports, thereby enhancing interpretability and facilitating clinical verification of findings. 84 

To showcase the potential of our automated CE reporting, we conducted a comprehensive evaluation 85 

focusing on three key aspects: report quality , clinician evaluation , and clinical utility . Our model was sys-86 

tematically compared against nine state-of-the-art (SOTA) methods 22–25, 27, 28, 34–36 in multimodal medical report 87 

generation. For report quality , our model significantly outperformed existing benchmarks, achieving higher 88 

scores in standard metrics and GPT-4-based assessments (Fig. 1c). This demonstrated its ability to generate 89 

linguistically coherent and clinically relevant reports 41, 42 . To quantitatively assess diagnostic agreement, we 90 

employed DeepSeek-V3 43 to transform clinical reports into structured condition matrices, serving as a prag-91 

matic proxy. Our model exhibited superior precision and recall in multi-lesion scenarios (Fig. 1c), highlighting 92 

the model’s potential to enhance clinical decision-making and diagnostic precision. In clinician evaluation ,93 

three clinicians with varying expertise rated reports across correctness, completeness, and conciseness in a 94 

blinded manner, reflecting reference-report alignment and perceived report quality. Moreover, senior experts 95 

evaluated CE Reporter-generated reports against those written by resident clinicians for diagnostic quality and 96 

efficiency. This assessment demonstrated that the AI-generated reports were superior in quality and notably 97 

more efficient, suggesting a practical advantage in clinical workflows. Regarding clinical utility , we evaluated 98 

the model’s explainability and generalization. To illustrate the explainability of our model, we demonstrated 99 

the strong connection between the detected keyframes and the generated reports. This connection allowed 100 

clinicians to trace the supporting visual evidence for the conclusions and to assess report credibility. Finally, 101 

we evaluated external applicability across 223 patient cases from one cross-center cohort and two cross-device 102 

cohorts, assessing CE Reporter’s performance under institutional and device-related variations. The ablation 103 

study (Supplementary Figure 1) demonstrates that the proposed core modules are essential for achieving robust 104 

report generation and diagnostic agreement. 105 

In summary, we have established MICS-CE, the first multi-center, multi-device, large-scale dataset de-106 

signed for CE reporting, serving as a critical resource for advancing gastrointestinal diagnostic research. 107 

Building on this resource, we have developed CE Reporter, an explainable, automated CE reporting system 108 

that streamlines the clinical workflow and completes the diagnostic loop. In real-world clinical settings, CE 109 

Reporter is intended to rapidly generate structured draft reports for clinicians, including keyframes with times-110 

tamps, frame-level descriptions, and a concise overall summary for reference. This allows clinicians to priori-111 

tize AI-annotated keyframes to guide their video review and then leverage the AI-generated text as an editable 112 

baseline for refinement, thereby enhancing diagnostic efficiency. Moreover, CE Reporter’s performance in 113 

correctness and completeness compared with resident clinicians suggests its potential as a drafting and review-114 

assistance tool in CE reporting workflows. By introducing the first dedicated dataset and the first practical 115 

framework for automated CE report generation, this work addresses a critical gap in GI diagnostics and lays 116 

the foundation for scalable, AI-assisted CE workflows. 117

Results 118 

The effectiveness of the proposed framework for CE report generation is demonstrated by benchmarking its 119 

inference performance against nine SOTA models, including four commercial models (GPT-5 22 , o3 23 , Gemini-120 

2.5-Pro 24 , and Grok4 25 ), two medical open-source MLLMs (LLaVA-Med-v1.5-7B 27 and BiomedGPT 28 ), and 121 

three video MLLMs (Video-LLaVA-7B 34 , LongVLM 35 , and MiniGPT4-Video 36 ). Training and internal val-122 

idation (4:1 ratio) were conducted using a diverse cohort from the Department of Gastrointestinal Endoscopy 123 

at the Sixth Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (SSH). External validation was performed using sep-124 

arate cohorts from The Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (TSH), Yuebei People’s Hospital 125 

(YPH), and Ruijin Hospital, Shanghai Jiao tong University School of Medicine (RJH) to assess generaliza-126 

tion. For model comparison, we fine-tuned Video-LLaVA-7B 34 , LongVLM 35 , and MiniGPT4-Video 36 on the 127 

SSH training dataset using the same video-to-report generation protocol as CE Reporter, while GPT-5 22 , o3 128 

> 23

, Gemini-2.5-Pro 24 , Grok4 25 , LLaVA-Med-v1.5-7B 27 , and BiomedGPT 28 were evaluated directly without 129 

fine-tuning due to their pre-trained configurations, using ground truth keyframes as input for keyframe-to-report 130 

generation. The following sections highlight our results across these aspects. 131 

CE Reporter Achieves Strong Linguistic Coherence in Report Quality Our method consistently outper-132 

formed SOTA models across multiple metrics, demonstrating superior performance in both linguistic coherence 133 

and clinical relevance (Fig. 2a; complete 95% CIs, effect sizes (rank-biserial correlation, rrb ), and false dis-134 

covery rate (FDR)-adjusted q-values are detailed in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2). For linguistic coherence, 135 

CE Reporter improved BLEU-ave and METEOR over the strongest baseline by 9.3% and 8.6%, respectively 136 

(rrb ∈ [0 .370 , 0.454] , q < 1 × 10 −4). These gains suggest better capture of diagnostic details and fewer 137 

vocabulary mismatches than baseline models. ROUGE-L showed only a marginal, non-significant difference 138 

(rrb = 0 .110 , q = 0 .239 ), consistent with its sensitivity to minor variations in phrasing and report ordering. 139 

For clinical relevance, we assessed model performance using GPT4-score, DocLens scores, and MEDCON 140 

scores. CE Reporter outperformed the strongest baseline, Video-LLaVA-7B, in GPT4-score (3.6471, 95% 141 

CI: 3.5229–3.7647 vs. 3.2484; rrb = 0 .446 ; q = 5 .4 × 10 −5), DocLens precision and recall (0.6706 and 142 

0.6416, respectively), and MEDCON (0.8055, 95% CI: 0.7890–0.8217 vs. 0.7751; rrb = 0 .307 ; q = 0 .0014 ). 143 

These results highlight the CE Reporter’s robust capability to capture critical diagnostic findings and represent 144 

medical concepts accurately. Furthermore, representative generated reports are illustrated in Fig. 2b. In small 145 

intestine lesion detection, our model correctly identified key lesions consistent with the reference report, while 146 

Video-LLaVA-7B produced false negatives and misclassifications. For normal cases, our model avoided over-147 

diagnosis artifacts causing hallucinated findings in Video-LLaVA-7B. This reliability stems from prioritizing 148 

diagnostically significant frames and linking textual findings to visual evidence, enhancing interpretability and 149 

reducing omission risks. More examples of generated reports are available in Supplementary Figures 2–6. 150 

CE Reporter Demonstrates High Diagnostic Agreement To evaluate the diagnostic capability of our model, 151 

we employed DeepSeek-V3 43 to convert summary reports into structured condition matrices as pragmatic 152 

proxies. To validate the reliability of this proxy pipeline, an expert physician manually audited matrices for 153 

50 randomly sampled reports, yielding a highly consistent patient-level F1-score of 0.998 (49/50 full-matrix 154 

exact matches; Supplementary Figure 7 for detailed results). Subsequently, we calculated precision, recall, and 155 

F1-score based on this verified proxy to evaluate the diagnostic agreement (see Supplementary Table 3 and 156 

Supplementary Figure 8 for details). 157 

As shown in Fig. 3a, our method demonstrated substantial improvements over nine SOTA models in small 158 

intestine lesion classification (two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with FDR-adjusted q < 0.001 across all 159 

metrics, see Supplementary Tables 2 and 4 for complete statistics). Specifically, compared to the commercial 160

in clinical practice 44–46 ). Consequently, rather than serving as a standalone diagnostic system, CE Reporter 179 

is best positioned as an adjunctive drafting and review-assistance tool embedded within clinician-supervised 180 

workflows, providing structured report drafts and visual evidence to help clinicians efficiently review potential 181 

abnormalities. Finally, to investigate the underlying causes of misclassifications, we explored the model’s clas-182 

sification patterns across 16 conditions (Fig. 3d). The diagnostic analysis matrix reveals that errors primarily 183 

occur between visually similar conditions. For instance, 10 out of 45 congestion cases were misclassified as 184 

ulcers, as both share the visual characteristic of mucosal erythema. In summary, compared to existing baseline 185 

models, CE Reporter shows promising performance across a wide range of small intestine lesions, support-186 

ing its potential utility as an adjunct tool in clinical settings. More details about diagnostic results for all 187 

gastrointestinal structures are provided in Supplementary Table 5. 188 

CE Reporter Receives High Ratings in Clinician Evaluation To evaluate the clinical applicability of AI-189 

generated diagnostic reports, we conducted a blind assessment involving three clinicians with varying exper-190 

tise: a resident clinician with 5 years of experience, an attending clinician with 10 years of experience, and 191 

a department chief with 15 years of experience. CE Reporter, together with three SOTA fine-tuned models, 192 

Video-LLaVA-7B, LongVLM, and MiniGPT4-Video, were used for report generation. Reports were generated 193

reference reports and available visual evidence, reflecting factual alignment and perceived report quality rather 199

CE Reporter Outperforms Resident Clinicians in Report Quality and Efficiency To assess the practical 227 

effectiveness of CE Reporter, we compared its performance with five resident clinicians using twenty CE videos 228 

of small intestine examinations. These included thirteen videos with lesions (8% of our internal validation 229 

set) and seven normal videos (4% of the set), ensuring a balanced evaluation. Resident clinicians and CE 230 

Reporter independently generated diagnostic reports for each case, with report generation times recorded to 231 

assess efficiency. For the evaluation, two attending clinicians and a department chief performed a blinded 232 

comparison of reports generated by the CE Reporter and resident clinicians, assessing preference and scoring 233 

correctness, completeness, and conciseness on a 1–5 scale. 234

clinicians, with a 58.06% preference rate for the AI-generated reports compared to 41.94% for the clinicians’ 236 

reports. In addition, CE Reporter demonstrated higher correctness and completeness while maintaining com-237 

parable conciseness (Fig. 5b). For correctness, CE Reporter achieved a higher average score than resident clin-238

which is critical for clinical adoption, we examined the relationship between keyframe detection, description 263 

accuracy, and report quality. Keyframe detection completeness was defined as the ratio of detected keyframes 264 

to total reference keyframes, while description accuracy was calculated as the proportion of correctly described 265 

keyframes among all detected keyframes. A department chief assessed the validity of the detected keyframes 266 

and the correctness of their captions using an interactive clinician interface. The interactive interface for 267 

keyframe evaluation is depicted in Supplementary Figure 12. To further illustrate the final output format, a 268 

complete CE Reporter-generated structured report, including detected keyframes, timestamps, keyframe de-269 

scriptions, and the final diagnostic summary, is shown in Supplementary Figure 13. 270 

Fig. 6a demonstrates a positive association between keyframe detection completeness and report quality. 271 

High-quality reports (a score of 5) consistently achieve higher keyframe detection rates (0.9715 for correctness 272 

and 0.9494 for completeness) than low-quality reports (scores of 1–4), highlighting the role of comprehensive 273 

keyframe capture in report quality. Fig. 6b demonstrates that high-quality reports feature a greater propor-274 

tion of accurately described keyframes (0.8895 for correctness and 0.8745 for completeness), highlighting the 275 

importance of the context-aware captioning model. This precise alignment between visual content and tex-276 

tual descriptions is associated with improved report correctness and may facilitate clinician verification of the 277 

findings. Moreover, we present a case study (Fig. 6c) to visualize the linkage between detected keyframes, 278 

their descriptions, and the AI-generated report. The case shows precise detection of lesions, such as ulcers and 279 

polyps, with robust alignment between visual keyframes and their textual descriptions. This visualization illus-280 

trates how keyframe-guided AI enhances transparency by linking textual findings to visual content, enabling 281 

clinicians to trace diagnostic conclusions to specific visual cues. 282

Discussion 318 

In this work, we have introduced the MICS-CE dataset, the first large-scale resource for automated CE report 319 

generation, and CE Reporter, an explainable framework for generating evidence-supported diagnostic reports. 320 

This system achieved higher clinician-rated report quality than resident clinicians, particularly in correctness 321 

(4.70 vs. 3.74), while reducing report generation time to 2.44 minutes versus 30.25 minutes for small bowel 322 

videos (91.9% reduction). These findings demonstrate that CE Reporter delivers a scalable solution with 323 

potential workflow value to enhance diagnostic efficiency and expand global healthcare accessibility. 324 

The MICS-CE dataset advances CE research through its scale, temporal coverage, and detailed annota-325 

tions. Unlike prior datasets like Kvasir or KID, which are limited in size ( <150 videos, each video <30 minutes 326 

in length), anatomical scope (e.g., small bowel only), and annotation type (e.g., lesion segmentation), MICS-327 

CE spans the entire gastrointestinal tract and provides structured reports. This comprehensive resource lays a 328 

foundation for robust modeling for clinically relevant report generation. Notably, CE Reporter is the first AI 329 

system to process full-length CE videos, delivering structured reports with embedded visual evidence. Its two-330 

stage architecture first employs temporal continuity-constrained clustering and a multi-task learning framework 331 

to reduce redundancy by 77.73% and select diagnostically relevant keyframes. A vision-language model with 332 

time-aware prompts then generates precise keyframe descriptions, followed by report summarization. This 333 

design addresses limitations of models like LongVLM and Video-LLaVA, which struggle with long sequences 334 

and lack interpretability, achieving a BLEU-ave of 0.5721, CIDEr of 0.6939, and multi-lesion precision/recall 335 

of 0.82/0.83, outperforming current SOTA models. In the clinician comparison, CE Reporter showed favorable 336 

report quality compared with resident clinicians. It achieved a higher average correctness score than resident 337 

clinicians (4.70 vs. 3.74), while reducing report generation time to an average of 2.44 minutes, compared with 338 

30.25 minutes for resident clinicians reviewing small bowel videos (91.9% reduction) and approximately 90 339

minutes for complete gastrointestinal videos (97.3% reduction). In a real-world clinical workflow, CE Reporter 340 

is designed to generate a structured draft report that includes keyframes with timestamps, frame-level descrip-341 

tions, and a narrative summary. This capability supports a streamlined review process where clinicians can first 342 

direct their attention to AI-annotated key timestamps to guide their video assessment, and subsequently use the 343 

AI-generated text as a baseline for efficient refinement, thereby enhancing diagnostic efficiency. Moreover, the 344 

demonstrated correctness and completeness of its automated reports position CE Reporter as a drafting and 345 

review-assistance tool, with potential to support diagnostic consistency and help clinicians identify possible 346 

omissions during report refinement. 347 

Despite these advancements, limitations remain at the dataset, model, and evaluation levels. At the 348 

dataset level, the MICS-CE dataset has three main constraints. First, it exhibits demographic imbalance, with 349 

a bias toward male patients (67.8%) and a China-centric recruitment strategy. Second, it contains very few in-350 

stances of rare lesions. This underrepresentation is partly attributable to the low occurrence of such findings in 351 

routine CE practice and their dependence on regional epidemiology and referral patterns 47, 48 . As quantified in 352 

Fig. 3b, conditions such as foreign bodies, parasites, and pigmentation each comprise fewer than 10 instances, 353 

which is insufficient for the model to learn discriminative features. This data scarcity directly leads to poor 354 

recall scores for rare lesions (e.g., parasite: 0/1; pigmentation: 0/4; detailed in Fig. 3d and Supplementary 355 

Figure 14). This limitation is clinically relevant because missed rare findings may increase the risk of delayed 356 

recognition if CE Reporter is used without careful clinician supervision. For example, undetected parasitic 357 

infections may lead to chronic gastrointestinal complications, while certain missed pigmentations may warrant 358 

further clinical assessment. Third, the current system generates a structured summary report rather than a com-359 

prehensive clinical report. Specifically, it lacks quantitative lesion size estimation, because the unpredictable 360 

perspective and distance of the capsule make physical scale calibration highly unreliable 49 . Therefore, auto-361 

mated accurate size estimation remains challenging without external depth sensors or physical calibrators. At 362 

the model level, external validation showed variability in lesion-level sensitivity. Although CE Reporter main-363 

tained competitive cohort-level performance across the external cohorts, category-specific sensitivity varied 364 

more noticeably in cross-device settings. Sensitivity for high-frequency and visually distinctive findings such 365 

as congestion remained relatively stable across cohorts (YPH: 92.0%, TSH: 90.0%, RJH: 82.2%). In contrast, 366 

sensitivity for ulceration decreased from 78.9% in the TSH cross-center cohort to 37.5% and 32.6% in the YPH 367 

and RJH cross-device cohorts, respectively, and bleeding sensitivity also varied across cohorts (TSH: 58.3%, 368 

YPH: 20.0%, RJH: 36.4%). This pattern may be related to differences in capsule optics, illumination, and 369 

resolution, which can influence the appearance of subtle or visually overlapping lesions. Finally, the model 370 

also has difficulty distinguishing between visually similar common conditions, such as ulcers and erosions, 371 

which is also a challenge for clinicians. At the evaluation level, our clinical metrics rely on agreement with 372 

reference reports rather than independently adjudicated diagnostic truth. Although physician-report agreement 373 

analysis demonstrated strong concordance between independently drafted and reference reports (mean Cohen’s 374 

κ = 0.929; range, 0.914–0.938), some disagreement remained. Most discrepancies reflected differences in le-375 

sion characterization and reporting granularity. Such variability is inherent to CE interpretation and highlights 376 

the need for more rigorous diagnostic reference standards in future studies. In addition, our comparison with 377 

resident clinicians does not establish equivalence to experienced CE readers. Consistent with prior endoscopy 378 

AI studies, performance advantages over less-experienced clinicians may not fully extend to senior specialists 379

critical gastrointestinal transitions, such as the esophagus, pylorus, and ileocecal valve. Lesion frames (55.9%) 409 

document pathological findings, including erosion, ulceration, and bleeding. Normal frames (29.8%) serve as 410 

a comparative baseline. 411 

Capsule endoscopy videos were collected from four centers, each using specific capsule endoscopy tech-412

779 videos using MiroCam endoscopes, while The Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (TSH) 414

types, video parameters, total video hours, and usage, is provided in Supplementary Table 11. 420

terologists from the participating institutions. For the SSH cohort, annotations were performed by a senior 422 

gastroenterologist with over 25 years of expertise in digestive endoscopy, having conducted over 1,000 double-423 

balloon enteroscopies and interpreted more than 1,200 capsule endoscopy videos. For other cohorts, senior 424 

physicians with over 15 years of experience in capsule endoscopy provided annotations. A rigorous three-stage 425

Data Preprocessing and Standardization To ensure data usability while prioritizing privacy and integrity, 432 

the dataset underwent comprehensive preprocessing and standardization. As shown in Fig. 8c, the process 433 

began with de-identification, where PyPDF extracted clinical data from raw PDFs, followed by the system-434 

atic removal of patient-identifiable text to create de-identified JSON files. Simultaneously, endoscopic videos 435 

were decomposed into image sequences using OpenCV, and PaddleOCR synchronized frame timestamps with 436 

their temporal positions in the original procedures to maintain chronological consistency. A specialized fa-437 

cial recognition classifier was then applied to detect and exclude video frames containing human facial fea-438 

tures, completing the sanitization process. Subsequently, as shown in Fig. 8c, the standardization phase uti-439 

lized Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct to transform free-text diagnostic reports into a hierarchical structure organized by 440 

anatomical regions, including esophageal, gastric, duodenal, small intestinal, and large intestinal segments. 441 

This model enforced uniform reporting conventions through carefully crafted prompts, with templates and ex-442 

amples detailed in Supplementary Table 12. Specifically, the standardized reports were designed strictly as 443 

structured summaries of visible CE findings. During standardization, patient history and prior examinations 444 

were removed because they cannot be directly inferred from a single independent CE video 30, 52 . In addition, 445 

quantitative lesion-size descriptions were excluded because the continuously changing capsule position and 446 

viewing distance make retrospective size estimation highly unreliable, a known challenge even for human ex-447 

perts 49 . Finally, narrative time expressions were removed to avoid alignment noise, as identical lesions often 448 

appear repeatedly at different timestamps in lengthy videos. Following standardization, we used keyframe re-449 

finement with Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct to integrate lesion classification results, standardized report content, and 450 

frame metadata to generate detailed descriptions of pathological conditions for each keyframe (Fig. 8c). These 451 

descriptions were rigorously validated by clinical experts to ensure diagnostic accuracy. Moreover, temporal 452 

segmentation was manually refined from minute-level to second-level precision, enhancing temporal alignment 453 

and supporting robust AI-driven analysis. The external validation datasets underwent de-identification and re-454 

port standardization to align with the primary dataset, but no keyframe-related processing was performed due 455 

to the lack of corresponding textual annotations in these cohorts. 456

Model Framework We propose a framework for automated CE report generation, structured into two key 484 

stages: (i) Temporal Continuity-Constrained Redundancy Reduction and Multi-Task Keyframe Extraction, 485 

which leverages a novel clustering algorithm and a multi-task learning framework to eliminate redundant 486 

frames and identify diagnostically relevant keyframes, and (ii) Time-Aware Multimodal Captioning and Re-487 

port Synthesis, which employs a fine-tuned LLaVA-v1.5-7B with time-aware prompts to generate precise, 488 

contextually informed captions for keyframes and a fine-tuned Llama 3.1-8B to synthesize these captions into 489 

structured, clinically coherent diagnostic reports. The complete pipeline is illustrated in Figure 9. 490 

The first stage performs temporal continuity-constrained redundancy reduction and multi-task keyframe 491 

extraction. CE videos often contain extensive redundancy, with many frames offering limited diagnostic value 492 

due to visual similarity. The first stage addresses this redundancy by distilling the video into a concise set of 493 

keyframes while preserving clinical content. For an input video of resolution H × W × 3 with T frames, this 494 

stage yields a manageable keyframe subset for subsequent processing. 495

Implementation Details Our framework for automated CE report generation operates through two stages. 546 

Input video frames are uniformly resized to 320 ×320 pixels and processed by a ResNet50 backbone pretrained 547 

on our pre-constructed CE classification dataset. The statistics of the CE classification dataset used for encoder 548 

pretraining are provided in Supplementary Table 16. Redundancy reduction employs a temporal continuity-549 

constrained clustering algorithm with a cosine similarity threshold of θr = 0 .94 , dynamically grouping adjacent 550 

frames. Keyframe detection integrates a Swin Transformer (configuration: swin-base-patch4-window7-224-551 

in22k) for semantic feature extraction at 224 × 224 resolution, followed by a lightweight Vision Transformer (4 552 

layers, 8 attention heads, 256-dimensional embeddings) to model spatiotemporal dependencies. The multi-task 553 

objective combines keyframe detection and anatomical classification losses, weighted by λ1 = 0 .3 for expert-554 

annotated frames, λ2 = 0 .3 for similarity-augmented keyframes ( θe = 0 .9), and λ3 = 0 .4 for anatomical 555

3 for the stomach, 10 for the small intestine, and 2 for the large intestine. Training spans 500 epochs using 558 

the AdamW optimizer with a learning rate of 2 × 10 −5 and a batch size of 16. In the second stage, keyframes 559

Evaluation Metrics We evaluate the quality of generated endoscopic reports using a comprehensive frame-585 

work that combines automated metrics and semantic analysis to ensure both linguistic coherence and clinical 586 

relevance. Lexical similarity is measured with BLEU 61 , which assesses n-gram precision, and ROUGE-L 62 ,587 

which emphasizes longest common subsequence recall, providing a foundation for comparing surface-level 588 

text alignment with reference reports. To capture semantic coherence, we employ syntactic parsing and deep 589 

learning-based similarity measures from the METEOR framework 63 , ensuring the generated reports maintain 590 

logical structure. Additionally, CIDEr scores 64 are computed to evaluate contextual relevance by weighting 591 

n-grams with TF-IDF across multiple reference reports, prioritizing clinically significant terms. 592 

For factual correctness, we used three complementary metrics. A GPT-4-based structured prompt eval-593 

uation 65 scores reports on a 5-point scale for factual accuracy, completeness, and clinical utility (see Supple-594 

mentary Table 18 for detailed prompts). We also applied MEDCON 66 to assess medical concept consistency, 595 

using QuickUMLS 67 for concept extraction via string-matching algorithms 68 , focusing on UMLS semantic 596 

groups. In addition, DocLens scores 69 decompose reports into subclaims for fine-grained precision and re-597 

call analysis at the claim level (see Supplementary Table 18 for detailed prompts). To specifically evaluate 598 

diagnostic agreement for abnormality identification, we used DeepSeek-V3 43 to convert each generated or 599 

reference report into a structured substructure-condition matrix. A fixed prompt mapped report findings to 14 600 

gastrointestinal substructures and 16 predefined condition categories. Multiple lesion categories could be as-601 

signed to the same substructure, while substructures without any reported abnormality were treated as normal. 602 

Precision, recall, and F1-scores were then calculated from the resulting matrices. Each metric is first calculated 603 

per condition at the substructure level, then aggregated by averaging across all substructures belonging to the 604 

same major anatomical region (e.g., esophagus, stomach, duodenum, small bowel, large bowel). Patient-level 605

ARTICLE IN PRESS 

scores are derived by averaging these region-level metrics across all patients. This hierarchical, region-aware 606 

evaluation ensures precise localization and classification of critical lesions. The complete prompt text and an 607

Clinician Evaluation To ensure clinical validity, three gastroenterologists with 15, 10, and 5 years of expe-609 

rience independently evaluate the generated reports using a custom-designed interface. Each expert scores 610 

the reports on a 5-point Likert scale across three dimensions: correctness, conciseness, and completeness. 611 

Correctness measures factual alignment with the reference report and available visual evidence. Conciseness 612 

evaluates the report’s brevity, penalizing redundancy while rewarding clarity. Completeness assesses the inclu-613 

sion of critical findings, ensuring no major abnormalities are omitted. Moreover, to assess inter-rater reliability 614 

of the clinician evaluations, we calculated the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) using the ICC(2,k) model, 615 

where k represents the number of raters (k=3). 616 

Furthermore, a senior gastroenterologist with 15 years of experience performs a dedicated keyframe 617 

analysis. This expert categorizes keyframes into anatomical landmarks, lesion findings, non-keyframes, or 618
