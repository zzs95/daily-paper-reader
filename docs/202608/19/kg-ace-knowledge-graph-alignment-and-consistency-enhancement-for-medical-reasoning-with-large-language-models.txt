## Abstract

Large language models (LLMs) can answer complex medical questions, but their intermediate reasoning may contain unsupported relations, omitted clinical constraints, or contradictions with established medical knowledge. We present Knowledge Graph Alignment and Consistency Enhancement (KG-ACE), a post-generation framework for verifying and revising medical reasoning produced by LLMs KG-ACE contains three components. First, a dual-layer medical knowledge graph separates standardized medical concepts in the Ontology Knowledge Graph from clinical rules in the Consistency Knowledge Graph. Second, a three-level verification mechanism evaluates concept validity, relation validity, and logical coherence. Third, a benefit-driven correction engine accepts a revision only when the recomputed alignment score improves by a specified margin. This design verifies the reasoning process without constraining the initial generation. Experiments on MedQA, MedMCQA, GenMedGPT-5k, and DDXPlus show that KG-ACE outperforms prompting-based, knowledge-graph-based, retrieval-based, and domain-pretrained baselines across all reported metrics. The results indicate that explicit process-level verification can complement knowledge augmentation by improving answer accuracy, reasoning stability, and the traceability of intermediate decisions.

## Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) have expanded the range of natural language tasks that can be addressed with limited task-specific supervision (Brown et al., 2020, Ouyang et al., 2022). In medicine, models such as Med-PaLM have achieved strong performance on medical question-answering benchmarks, and LLMs are increasingly studied for clinical decision support, medical education, and patient communication (Singhal et al., 2023a, Thirunavukarasu et al., 2023). These capabilities are valuable in clinical problems that require information to be combined across symptoms, diseases, medications, and temporal events.

Strong benchmark performance does not, however, guarantee a sound reasoning process. Medical LLMs can produce fluent explanations that contain unsupported inferential steps, conflicting conclusions, or recommendations that violate clinical constraints. Existing work has primarily improved the knowledge available to the model through retrieval, data augmentation, or domain-specific training. These methods do not necessarily verify how that knowledge is used after generation. As a result, an answer may be correct while its intermediate reasoning remains incomplete or unsafe, and the supporting evidence may not be traceable to a specific medical source (Ji et al., 2023, Lee et al., 2023, Singhal et al., 2023b).

**Motivation.** Fig. 1 illustrates a process-level error. The model recommends warfarin for a patient with atrial fibrillation but fails to examine the concurrent use of aspirin, which creates a clinically important bleeding-risk interaction. The isolated recommendation may appear plausible; the problem arises in the omitted verification step. This example motivates three observations:

**(M1) Final-answer evaluation can miss errors in intermediate reasoning.** Drug interactions, conflicting clinical states, and incorrect temporal orderings may appear within a reasoning chain even when the final answer is superficially acceptable.

**(M2) Linguistic fluency does not ensure logical completeness.** A model may rely on statistical association and omit the intermediate conditions needed to justify a clinical inference.

**(M3) General hallucination detection provides limited diagnostic detail.** A single answer-level judgment does not identify whether an error originates from an unrecognized concept, an invalid relation, or a global logical conflict, and it does not indicate which part of the reasoning should be revised.

These observations motivate a post-generation framework that verifies the reasoning process at multiple levels and uses the verification result to guide targeted revision. The experimental evaluation is organized around four research questions (RQ1–RQ4) in Section 4; computational cost is examined in Appendix A.

**Challenges.** Three technical challenges follow from this motivation.

**(C1) Reasoning consistency: no logic-level validation.** Input-stage knowledge injection and answer-level checking do not determine whether each intermediate step conforms to the relevant medical knowledge structure. Internal contradictions can therefore remain undetected (Lee et al., 2023).

**(C2) Reasoning completeness: association can replace clinical inference.** Models may compress a multi-step pathophysiological process into an unsupported leap, omitting the conditions needed to connect evidence to a conclusion (Ji et al., 2023).

**(C3) Reasoning traceability: evidence is not linked to individual steps.** Clinical users need to know which knowledge source supports a recommendation. Current systems often provide no explicit mapping from a generated step to the entities, relations, or rules on which it depends (Singhal et al., 2023b).

**Approach.** KG-ACE addresses these challenges with a combined verification and correction pipeline. The Ontology Knowledge Graph ($\mathcal{G}_{O}$) standardizes medical concepts and semantic relations. The Consistency Knowledge Graph ($\mathcal{G}_{C}$) represents clinical constraints, including interactions, contraindications, causal relations, and temporal orderings. A three-level mechanism then evaluates concept validity, relation validity, and logical coherence. Finally, the correction engine revises only the affected reasoning content and retains a candidate revision only when its alignment score improves.

**Contributions.** KG-ACE shifts verification from the final answer to the generated reasoning process. It differs from existing work in three linked ways: it verifies reasoning after generation rather than only supplying knowledge before generation; it localizes errors at the concept, relation, and logic levels rather than returning a single answer-level verdict; and it accepts a correction only after measuring its effect on alignment. The knowledge graph therefore functions as an explicit verification specification rather than only as a retrieval source.

Table 1 compares KG-ACE with closely related methods that combine knowledge graphs and LLMs. ToG (Sun et al., 2024) searches a knowledge graph before answer generation, and MedGraphRAG (Wu et al., 2024) retrieves graph-grounded evidence. Neither method verifies the internal consistency of a completed reasoning chain. KG-ACE instead combines post-generation, step-level verification; alignment at the concept, relation, and logic levels; and benefit-driven correction. The framework is intended to complement, rather than replace, retrieval, prompting, and domain-specific training.

Specifically, this study makes three contributions:

*   •
We develop a dual-layer medical knowledge graph that separates standardized medical concepts from clinical constraints, enabling the system to distinguish terminology errors from rule violations.

*   •
We introduce a three-level verification mechanism that evaluates generated reasoning at the concept, relation, and logic levels and returns both a continuous alignment score and a structured diagnostic report.

*   •
We design a benefit-driven correction engine that applies local, structural, or global revisions and retains a change only when the alignment improvement exceeds a specified threshold.

### Conclusion

We introduced KG-ACE, a post-generation framework for verifying and correcting medical reasoning produced by LLMs. By separating concept standardization from clinical constraint checking through a dual-layer knowledge graph, KG-ACE evaluates each reasoning chain at the concept, relation, and logic levels and retains a correction only when the revised chain achieves a higher alignment score. This design makes the verification target explicit while preserving the original generation process.
