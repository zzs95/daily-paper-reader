Title: A deep learning model for opportunistic screening of chronic kidney disease using chest radiographs: a multicentre validation study in the USA

URL Source: https://www.thelancet.com/journals/eclinm/article/PIIS2589-5370(26)00331-7/fulltext

Markdown Content:
## Summary

### Background

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) affects 843 million people worldwide yet remains underdiagnosed. Current risk stratification, including the CKD Prognosis Consortium (CKD-PC) score, relies on laboratory data rarely evaluated outside nephrology settings. Chest radiographs (CXR) are among the most frequently performed imaging procedures globally, offering an opportunistic screening pathway. CXR-CKD5, a CXR-derived AI score for predicting 5-year incident CKD without laboratory data, was developed and externally validated.

### Methods

In this retrospective two-centre study conducted at two sites in the USA, 97,553 adults (≥18 years) without baseline CKD who underwent routine CXR were included: Emory University (development cohort, 2008–2021, n = 75,683) and University of Illinois Chicago (external validation cohort, 2010–2020, n = 21,870). Patients with a CKD diagnosis within 90 days of the index CXR were excluded. A convolutional neural network extracted 10 cardiopulmonary and metabolic risk features from routine CXRs, which were used to train an XGBoost accelerated failure time model. The primary outcome was 5-year incident CKD. Discrimination (C-index) and calibration were evaluated against a clinical base model and the CKD-PC score.

### Findings

CXR-CKD5 achieved apparent C-indices of 0.774 (95% CI: 0.768–0.780) in development (optimism-corrected: 0.737) and 0.713 (95% CI: 0.697–0.729) at external validation. A combined imaging-clinical model achieved the highest external validation discrimination (C-index 0.734, 95% CI: 0.719–0.751). Performance was strongest in non-diabetic patients (development C-index 0.783, 95% CI: 0.775–0.790; external validation 0.714, 95% CI: 0.689–0.739) and attenuated in diabetic patients across both cohorts (development C-index 0.658, 95% CI: 0.645–0.671; external validation 0.607, 95% CI: 0.580–0.634). Absolute risk was overestimated at external validation, indicating that calibration would be required before deployment at new institutions. A threshold of ≥15% predicted risk identified the top 20% at risk.

### Interpretation

CXR-CKD5 demonstrates that routine chest radiographs can identify patients at elevated CKD risk without laboratory data, offering a scalable opportunistic screening pathway. Recalibration and prospective evaluation assessing clinical outcomes and workflow integration are needed before deployment.

Research in context

## Evidence before this study

We conducted a systematic search of PubMed and Embase from their inception to 30 June 2025, with no language restrictions. The search included terms related to “chronic kidney disease”, “screening”, “undiagnosed”, “deep learning”, and “chest radiography”. We included original research articles and systematic reviews that assessed CKD prevalence, current diagnostic limitations, and the application of artificial intelligence to medical imaging for systemic disease prediction. The evidence from large epidemiological studies and health organizations is of high quality and consistently shows that CKD is a highly prevalent global health issue that remains severely underdiagnosed. The quality of evidence for AI applications in chest radiography is growing, though many studies are retrospective and thus carry an inherent risk of bias. Our search identified prior work demonstrating that a deep learning model could predict key CKD risk factors, such as diabetes, from chest X-rays. Separately, other research showed the feasibility of using a different deep learning approach to predict the future risk of moderate to severe kidney function loss. No pooled estimate was derived as a formal meta-analysis was not performed.

## Added value of this study

This study adds to the existing evidence by providing the first validated model specifically designed to predict the initial onset (incident) of CKD by repurposing clinically interpretable risk scores from chest X-rays. Unlike prior work focused on progression to severe disease or using abstract image features, our model offers a transparent basis for its predictions, a critical factor for clinical adoption. A key finding is the model’s significantly higher performance in non-diabetic patients, which adds value by identifying a novel and effective application for opportunistic screening in a large population not currently targeted for routine kidney function testing.

## Implications of all the available evidence

The combined evidence suggests that AI can transform routine medical images into powerful tools for proactive health screening. For clinical practice and policy, this approach could be integrated into standard radiology workflows to automatically flag patients with high-risk chest X-rays for follow-up kidney function testing, creating a scalable cost-effective screening pathway. For future research, it is imperative to prospectively validate this model in diverse, real-world clinical settings to confirm its effectiveness and assess its impact on patient outcomes. Further studies are also needed to address performance disparities in underrepresented ethnic groups and to refine the model for predicting specific CKD stages.

## Methods

### Ethics

This retrospective analysis was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and all relevant ethical standards, with institutional review board (IRB) approval at two clinically distinct sites (Emory University IRB Study Number STUDY00002276 and University of Illinois at Chicago IRB #1 protocol number 2024-0187). This report adheres to the Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis Or Diagnosis + Artificial Intelligence (TRIPOD + AI) and the Strengthening the Reporting of OBservational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines. The requirement for written informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study.

### Study cohort

This study was conducted on two different datasets from two geographically distinct institutions in the USA: Emory University (Emory), Atlanta, and University of Illinois, Chicago (UIC). The Emory dataset was used as the development cohort, while the UIC dataset was used as the external validation cohort. At both institutions, the primary inclusion criteria were adult patients (≥18 years) who had at least one ambulatory, upright, posteroanterior (PA) chest X-ray (CXR).

Exclusion criteria were applied at the patient and image level ([Supplementary Fig. S1](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/eclinm/article/PIIS2589-5370(26)00331-7/fulltext#supplementary-material)). To create a cohort of individuals without significant kidney disease at baseline, we excluded patients with a prior diagnosis of CKD, end-stage kidney disease (ESKD), or renal replacement therapy (RRT). Additionally, patients with a baseline eGFR below 60 mL/min/1.73 m 2 were excluded. The 2021 CKD-Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) Creatinine Equation, which does not include a race variable, was utilised for all eGFR calculations. We further excluded all CXRs obtained in inpatient or portable settings, lateral views, and any images that had undergone Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalisation (CLAHE) processing. Lateral views were excluded as the Pyrros et al. convolutional neural-network (CNN)[12](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/eclinm/article/PIIS2589-5370(26)00331-7/fulltext#)

12.

Pyrros, A. ∙ Borstelmann, S.M. ∙ Mantravadi, R. ∙ et al.

**Opportunistic detection of type 2 diabetes using deep learning from frontal chest radiographs**

_Nat Commun._ 2023; **14**:4039

 was trained exclusively on posteroanterior (PA) views; applying it to lateral projections would constitute an out-of-distribution use case with unreliable feature extraction.

### Outcomes definition

The primary outcome of this study was a diagnosis of CKD. The outcome was defined by the presence of one or more relevant international classification of diseases 9 (ICD-9) or ICD-10 diagnosis codes in a patient’s electronic health record. A complete list of the codes used for case identification is available in [Supplementary Table S1](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/eclinm/article/PIIS2589-5370(26)00331-7/fulltext#supplementary-material). ICD-based outcome ascertainment was chosen to enable uniform case identification across both institutions, where quantitative eGFR trajectories were not consistently available.

Patients free of CKD at baseline were followed from the date of the index CXR. To reduce the likelihood of capturing pre-existing but undiagnosed CKD, a 90-day landmark period was applied. Patients who received a CKD diagnosis within 90 days of the index CXR were excluded. Follow-up therefore commenced at 90 days post-CXR, and the follow-up period for each patient extended until one of the following events occurred: a new CKD diagnosis (the outcome), death, or the last qualifying clinical encounter (censoring). For this analysis, a qualifying clinical encounter was defined as a face-to-face visit to primary care, hospitalisation, or a visit to the emergency department or a subspecialty clinic. A CKD diagnosis was only considered a valid outcome event if it was recorded on one of these encounters.

### CXR-CKD5 model development

We sought to determine whether the prognostic signals captured by a previously validated CXR-based CNN[12](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/eclinm/article/PIIS2589-5370(26)00331-7/fulltext#)

12.

Pyrros, A. ∙ Borstelmann, S.M. ∙ Mantravadi, R. ∙ et al.

**Opportunistic detection of type 2 diabetes using deep learning from frontal chest radiographs**

_Nat Commun._ 2023; **14**:4039

 could be extended to predict incident CKD. The upstream CNN (Pyrros et al.) was trained on approximately 271,000 frontal chest radiographs from Duly Health and Care using a ResNet34 architecture with 10 multi-task outputs: six disease probability scores (type 2 diabetes, congestive heart failure [CHF], cardiac arrhythmias, morbid obesity, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [COPD], vascular disease) and four continuous health metrics (age, BMI, HbA1c, risk adjustment factor [RAF]); model is publicly available (see [Supplementary Methods](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/eclinm/article/PIIS2589-5370(26)00331-7/fulltext#supplementary-material)). We used these features rather than training an end-to-end model to maximise reproducibility, clinical face validity (outputs correspond to recognisable risk constructs), and accessibility (no GPU infrastructure needed for model development). To test this, we engineered an XGBoost accelerated failure time (AFT) model, trained to predict time-to-event for reduced kidney function, and 5-year CKD probabilities were derived by evaluating the normal cumulative density function (CDF) at t = 1825 days (see [Supplementary Methods, Fig. S2, and Tables S2 and S3](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/eclinm/article/PIIS2589-5370(26)00331-7/fulltext#supplementary-material)).

### Internal validation

The primary predictive task was framed as a fixed-horizon binary classification, specifically predicting incident CKD within a 5-year follow-up period. To evaluate the prognostic value of the CXR-CKD5 score for CKD prediction, we compared the following models on the development cohort:

1.

CXR-CKD5: Standalone predictions from the XGBoost AFT model.

2.

Base model: Cox proportional hazards model with age (per 5 years, centred at 55), sex, BMI (per 5 units), and diabetes.

3.

Base model + CXR-CKD5: A combined model developed by fitting a Cox proportional hazards model on the development cohort, integrating the base clinical covariates with the continuous CXR-CKD5 score (scaled per 5% absolute risk increase) to evaluate additive prognostic value.

4.

CKD-PC score: For the subset of patients with available laboratory values to calculate the 5-year kidney function decline risk score.

The CKD-PC comprises two diabetes-stratified equations (12 variables including age, sex, race, eGFR, uACR, BMI, cardiovascular disease (CVD) history, smoking, and hypertension; the diabetes version additionally includes HbA1c and medication use).[7](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/eclinm/article/PIIS2589-5370(26)00331-7/fulltext#)

### External validation

Discrimination and calibration performance of all three models were evaluated on the UIC dataset for external validation. The CKD-PC comparison was restricted to the development cohort because uACR data, a required input for both CKD-PC equations, were not available in the UIC validation cohort. For the main analysis, all models were evaluated both on the full cohort and stratified by diabetes status to assess performance consistency across these patient subgroups. Secondary subgroup analyses were also done on different age groups (20–40, 40–60, and >60 years), sex, and race. No recalibration was performed on the external dataset.

### Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were computed for categorical and continuous variables. Continuous variables are reported as median [Q1, Q3] (Kruskal–Wallis test) and categorical variables as n (%) (chi-squared test) and all comparisons were Bonferroni-corrected (α = 0.05). Kaplan–Meier analyses with stratification by CXR-CKD5 score risk categories (low or high) based on the clinical threshold were performed. Statistical significance was determined using the log-rank test.

Patients who died without prior CKD were censored at death (development cohort: 861 patients, 1.1%) and a post-hoc sensitivity analysis excluding these patients confirmed minimal impact on discrimination. Missing laboratory data are unlikely to be missing completely at random, as patients with greater comorbidity burden are more likely to undergo testing. The CKD-PC-eligible subset therefore represents a sicker subpopulation, and comparisons may not generalise to the full cohort. Imputation was not performed given the high proportion of missing uACR (>70%).

To assess the foundational validity of the Pyrros feature representations, we evaluated the model’s baseline predictive performance for intermediate traits with available ground truth (age, BMI, and diabetes) in both the development and validation cohorts. Performance was quantified using mean absolute error (MAE) for continuous traits and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) for binary disease probability, with 95% confidence intervals derived via non-parametric bootstrapping (1000 iterations). Additionally, population-level occlusion maps were generated for each of the ten Pyrros feature outputs by aggregating patients from the development cohort with positive incident CKD per feature to illustrate the spatial distribution of radiographic signal driving each feature.

We estimated both the apparent and optimism-corrected performance of all models on the development cohort, and validated performance on the external cohort.[18](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/eclinm/article/PIIS2589-5370(26)00331-7/fulltext#)

Paired comparisons between models were performed using the bootstrap difference of the C-index. The null hypothesis of no statistically significant difference between models was rejected if the 95% confidence interval of the C-index difference did not contain zero. Calibration was assessed using calibration plots (10 bins), calibration slope, and expected-to-observed (E/O) ratio (95% CI via bootstrapping, 1000 iterations) for the full cohort and stratified by diabetes status.

## Results

A total of 97,553 patients met inclusion criteria across both institutions: 75,683 in the Emory development cohort and 21,870 in the UIC external validation cohort. Baseline characteristics are presented in [Table 1](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/eclinm/article/PIIS2589-5370(26)00331-7/fulltext#tbl1). Cohorts differed significantly across all variables (all p < 0.001). Patients in the development cohort were older (median age 54 vs. 44 years) and more likely to be male (42.1%, n = 31,843 vs. 34.8%, n = 7618) and White (48.3%, n = 36,530 vs. 13.8%, n = 3025). The validation cohort had a higher proportion of Hispanic or Latino patients (22.4%, n = 4900 vs. 2.5%, n = 1921) and Black patients (54.6%, n = 11,945 vs. 40.9%, n = 30,917). Diabetes prevalence was higher in the validation cohort (22.2%, n = 4854 vs. 14.6%, n = 10,933). Median follow-up duration was longer in the development cohort (1825 vs. 871 days), reflecting the 5-year observation window and greater follow-up maturity at Emory. The overall 5-year incidence of CKD was 6.3% (n = 6145), with a higher rate in the development cohort (7.0%, n = 5272) compared to the validation cohort (4.0%, n = 873).

Coefficients and adjusted hazard ratios for all Cox proportional hazards models are presented in [Supplementary Table S6](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/eclinm/article/PIIS2589-5370(26)00331-7/fulltext#supplementary-material). Briefly, each additional 5-year increment in age (HR 1.162, 95% CI: 1.150–1.174), male sex (HR 1.517, 95% CI: 1.436–1.602), and diabetes (HR 2.242, 95% CI: 2.112–2.381) were independent predictors of incident CKD in the base model. In the combined model, the CXR-CKD5 score was the strongest predictor (HR 1.491 per 5% risk score increment, 95% CI: 1.472–1.511), with attenuation of the age, sex, and diabetes coefficients reflecting partial mediation through the imaging score.

Discrimination results for all models in the development and validation cohorts are presented in [Table 3](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/eclinm/article/PIIS2589-5370(26)00331-7/fulltext#tbl3) and the paired discrimination differences presented in [Supplementary Table S7](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/eclinm/article/PIIS2589-5370(26)00331-7/fulltext#supplementary-material). Development cohort C-indices represent apparent performance; optimism-corrected bootstrap estimates yielded corrected C-indices of 0.737 for CXR-CKD5, 0.682 for the base model, and 0.772 for the combined model, indicating negligible optimism for the Cox PH models (0.000) and modest optimism for the XGBoost AFT model (0.037). The CXR-CKD5 score demonstrated strong discrimination across the full development cohort (apparent C-index 0.774, 95% CI: 0.768–0.780), substantially outperforming the base clinical model (C-index 0.682, 95% CI: 0.675–0.689; p < 0.001) in the subset of patients with complete covariate data (n = 72,678). Addition of the CXR-CKD5 score to the base model (C-index 0.772, 95% CI: 0.766–0.778) did not materially improve discrimination over CXR-CKD5 alone (C-index 0.774, 95% CI: 0.768–0.780). As a post-hoc sensitivity analysis for competing mortality, excluding 861 patients who died without developing CKD yielded a C-index of 0.775 for CXR-CKD5, compared to 0.774 in the full cohort, indicating minimal impact of competing mortality on discrimination estimates. Among patients with diabetes (n = 10,933), the CXR-CKD5 score showed lower discrimination (C-index 0.658, 95% CI: 0.645–0.671) compared to non-diabetic patients (C-index 0.783, 95% CI: 0.775–0.790), consistent with the greater clinical complexity of CKD risk in this subgroup. In the CKD-PC-eligible subset (n = 19,082), the CXR-CKD5 score (C-index 0.741, 95% CI: 0.731–0.752) outperformed both the CKD-PC (C-index 0.707, 95% CI: 0.694–0.720) and the base model (C-index 0.647, 95% CI: 0.634–0.659). Discrimination of CXR-CKD5 across demographic subgroups in both cohorts is presented in [Supplementary Table S8](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/eclinm/article/PIIS2589-5370(26)00331-7/fulltext#supplementary-material). In the external validation cohort (N = 21,870), the combined base model + CXR-CKD5 achieved the highest overall discrimination (C-index 0.734, 95% CI: 0.719–0.751), followed by the base clinical model (C-index 0.723, 95% CI: 0.705–0.741) and CXR-CKD5 alone (C-index 0.713, 95% CI: 0.697–0.729). Paired bootstrap comparisons showed that the combined model showed a borderline non-significant improvement over the base model alone (ΔC-index −0.012, 95% CI: −0.000 to 0.023, p = 0.054). CXR-CKD5 alone did not significantly differ from the base model in the full cohort (ΔC-index −0.010, 95% CI: −0.027 to 0.006, p = 0.272) but showed significant improvement among non-diabetic patients (ΔC-index 0.041, 95% CI: 0.018–0.066, p = 0.002). Among patients with diabetes (n = 4854), discrimination was lower across all models: base model C-index 0.593 (95% CI: 0.566–0.621), CXR-CKD5 C-index 0.607 (95% CI: 0.580–0.634), and base model + CXR-CKD5 C-index 0.613 (95% CI: 0.585–0.641). Among non-diabetic patients (n = 17,016), all models performed better: base model C-index 0.673 (95% CI: 0.645–0.701), CXR-CKD5 C-index 0.714 (95% CI: 0.689–0.739), and base model + CXR-CKD5 C-index 0.718 (95% CI: 0.692–0.742).

Calibration plots are shown in [Fig. 1](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/eclinm/article/PIIS2589-5370(26)00331-7/fulltext#fig1) with calibration slopes and E/O ratios presented in [Supplementary Table S9](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/eclinm/article/PIIS2589-5370(26)00331-7/fulltext#supplementary-material). The base model, CXR-CKD5, and base model + CXR-CKD5, all demonstrated reasonable calibration in the base model-eligible subset, with E/O ratios ranging from 1.252 to 1.346 and calibration slopes near unity for the base model (0.976, 95% CI: 0.933–1.018) and combined model (0.922, 95% CI: 0.894–0.951); the CXR-CKD5 score showed mild overdispersion (slope 1.155, 95% CI: 1.118–1.191). In the CKD-PC-eligible subset, the base model, CXR-CKD5, and combined model all calibrated well (E/O ratios 0.918–1.024, slopes 0.870–1.098), whereas the CKD-PC score was severely miscalibrated (E/O ratio 2.726, slope 0.198, 95% CI: 0.188–0.209), indicating substantial overestimation of absolute risk. Calibration was markedly worse in patients with diabetes across all models, with slopes as low as 0.462 for the base model and 0.554 for the combined model, though the CXR-CKD5 score performed best in this subgroup (slope 0.782, E/O ratio 1.013). In the external validation cohort, all models overestimated absolute CKD risk consistent with the lower event rate observed at UIC (4.0% vs. 7.0% in development). The base model showed the least overestimation (E/O ratio 1.947) and the most favourable calibration slope (1.070, 95% CI: 0.981–1.160), approaching ideal calibration in terms of risk spread. The CXR-CKD5 score and combined model showed greater overestimation (E/O ratios 2.300 and 2.187, respectively) and flatter slopes (0.750 and 0.662), indicating underdispersion of predicted probabilities. Calibration was substantially worse among patients with diabetes, with slopes of 0.362 (CXR-CKD5) and 0.291 (combined model), compared to more acceptable slopes in non-diabetic patients (0.760 and 0.723, respectively). These findings indicate that recalibration of both the CXR-CKD5 score and combined model would be required prior to external deployment. Feature importance and directionality for the CXR-CKD5 model are presented in [Supplementary Fig. S7](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/eclinm/article/PIIS2589-5370(26)00331-7/fulltext#supplementary-material). The AI-derived congestive heart failure score (AI_CHF) was the dominant predictor of accelerated CKD onset across both diabetic and non-diabetic subgroups. To evaluate the clinical utility of the CXR-CKD5 score as a screening tool, we assessed performance at two decision thresholds: the Youden’s index-optimal threshold and the top 20% risk threshold. Results are presented in [Supplementary Tables S10 and S11](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/eclinm/article/PIIS2589-5370(26)00331-7/fulltext#supplementary-material). In the full development cohort (N = 75,683), the Youden’s optimal threshold (0.1102) yielded balanced sensitivity and specificity of 69.7% each, with a negative predictive value (NPV) of 96.8%, reflecting the low baseline CKD incidence. At the top 20% threshold (0.1564), specificity increased to 82.4% at the cost of sensitivity (51.7%), with PPV of 18.0% and NPV of 95.8%. In the CKD-PC-eligible subset (N = 19,082), at the Youden’s threshold (0.1100), the CXR-CKD5 score achieved higher sensitivity (68.2% vs. 60.3%) but lower specificity (66.9% vs. 75.0%) compared to the CKD-PC score at its own Youden’s threshold. The paired difference in sensitivity was +7.9% (95% CI: 5.5–10.7%) and the paired difference in specificity was −8.1% (95% CI: −8.9 to −7.2%), consistent with a more sensitive but less specific screening profile for CXR-CKD5. At the top 20% threshold, differences between scores were small and the 95% confidence intervals for the difference between the metrics crossed zero, indicating no statistically significant difference in performance. The consistently high NPV across all thresholds and subsets (93.5–96.8%) supports the potential utility of the CXR-CKD5 score as a rule-out screening instrument, where a low score could be used to defer further nephrology workup.

For subsequent binary risk stratification, high risk was defined as a CXR-CKD5 score ≥0.15 (15%), a clinically intuitive approximation of the top 20% risk threshold that avoids a population-specific percentile cut point. Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrated significantly lower CKD-free survival in high-risk patients vs. low-risk patients in both cohorts (all log-rank p < 0.0001), including in diabetes and non-diabetes subgroups ([Fig. 2](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/eclinm/article/PIIS2589-5370(26)00331-7/fulltext#fig2)). Further subgroup analyses ([Supplementary Figs. S10–S13](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/eclinm/article/PIIS2589-5370(26)00331-7/fulltext#supplementary-material)) confirmed significant risk stratification across age, sex, race, BMI, hypertension, smoking, and CVD history subgroups in both cohorts, with two exceptions in the external validation cohort: the Asian subgroup (log-rank p = 0.04) and the 60–79 age group (log-rank p < 0.01), likely reflecting smaller sample sizes.

## Discussion

In this retrospective two-site study of 97,553 adults without baseline CKD, we developed and externally validated CXR-CKD5, a chest radiograph-derived AI score for predicting 5-year incident CKD without requiring any laboratory data. The score achieved strong discrimination in development and maintained robust performance at a geographically and demographically distinct institution. While a combined model achieved the highest validation performance, CXR-CKD5 alone offers a scalable laboratory-free screening approach.

These findings address a growing global urgency. With an estimated 850 million people worldwide have kidney disease, increasing awareness and earlier identification are critical KDIGO priorities[1](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/eclinm/article/PIIS2589-5370(26)00331-7/fulltext#)

While an institution could theoretically extract hidden layer embeddings from any standard end-to-end CNN to train a downstream risk model with minimal computational overhead, such latent vectors are highly dimensional and clinically opaque. The advantage of leveraging the Pyrros representations lies in the combination of computational efficiency and clinical interpretability. By compressing the radiographic phenotype into exactly ten explicit, clinically recognisable disease constructs, such as subclinical heart failure and vascular disease ([Supplementary Fig. S3](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/eclinm/article/PIIS2589-5370(26)00331-7/fulltext#supplementary-material)), resource constrained institutions can locally validate or recalibrate the model using standard central processing unit (CPU) hardware and small datasets. This drastically lowers the barrier for local adaptation while maintaining total transparency regarding which specific radiographic phenotypes are driving the CKD risk predictions in their local population. This two-stage approach provides a degree of face validity and interpretability often missing from end-to-end “black box” algorithms.

The concentration of early events among high-risk patients suggests CXR-CKD5 is highly sensitive to radiographically visible, subclinical kidney dysfunction (e.g., early fluid overload) present at the time of the index CXR. By identifying a high-risk radiographic phenotype that rapidly progresses to a formal diagnosis past the 90-day landmark period, CXR-CKD5 functions as an effective early-warning prognostic tool. Furthermore, the score achieved significant risk stratification across diverse subgroups, including age, sex, race, BMI, hypertension, and history of cardiovascular disease ([Supplementary Figs. S3–S5, S10–S13, and Table S8](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/eclinm/article/PIIS2589-5370(26)00331-7/fulltext#supplementary-material)).

The consistent attenuation of discrimination in patients with diabetes across all models and both cohorts is clinically informative. Our feature importance analysis provides a clear mechanistic explanation for this performance gap ([Supplementary Fig. S7](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/eclinm/article/PIIS2589-5370(26)00331-7/fulltext#supplementary-material)). In patients without diabetes, those who did not develop CKD generally had lower CNN-derived disease probabilities ([Supplementary Fig. S9](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/eclinm/article/PIIS2589-5370(26)00331-7/fulltext#supplementary-material)), producing positive SHAP values that appropriately pushed predictions toward longer CKD-free survival. Those who developed CKD had higher feature values, producing negative SHAP contributions. This pattern reflects the model’s ability to leverage the variance in radiographic disease burden to separate progressors from non-progressors. However, within the diabetic cohort, this discriminative separation collapses ([Supplementary Fig. S9](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/eclinm/article/PIIS2589-5370(26)00331-7/fulltext#supplementary-material)). Both progressors and non-progressors exhibit elevated AI_Diabetes and AI_CHF scores, leaving the model with insufficient variance to discriminate between groups. The high baseline radiographic disease burden associated with diabetes overwhelms the model, producing uniformly negative SHAP values regardless of the patient’s true clinical trajectory. Notably, the state-of-the-art laboratory model (CKD-PC), which features a dedicated diabetes equation, also showed attenuated discrimination in this subgroup. This suggests the challenge lies not in the choice of modality, but in the inherently multifactorial nature of diabetic kidney disease, which likely requires longitudinal monitoring of glycaemic and albuminuria trends rather than single-timepoint screening.[21](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/eclinm/article/PIIS2589-5370(26)00331-7/fulltext#)

 Conversely, the consistently high negative predictive value of CXR-CKD5 supports its role as a rule-out instrument, safely deferring intensive workups in low-scoring patients. We deliberately chose a top-quintile operating threshold over a Youden-optimal cut point to generate practicable referral volumes, and we encourage future implementers to adapt thresholds to their local resources.

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, the retrospective, hospital-based design and reliance on ICD codes for outcome ascertainment may limit generalizability and underestimate the true incidence of early-stage disease. Future work incorporating confirmed eGFR decline would strengthen case identification. Second, while externally validated at a distinct institution, the underlying Pyrros CNN was trained on data from Duly Health, and because both UIC and Duly serve the greater Chicago area, the feature extraction may have benefited from regional demographic similarities. Third, patients who died before a CKD diagnosis were treated as censored without a formal competing risks framework, though sensitivity analyses showed minimal impact on discrimination. Fourth, our comparison to the CKD-PC score was restricted by the aforementioned lack of uACR data, preventing benchmarking at the validation site. Finally, interpretability is assessed at the intermediate feature level, while full image-level attribution remains an important direction for future work.

In summary, CKD remains a leading cause of global mortality, yet the majority of affected individuals are unaware of their condition. CXR-CKD5 demonstrates that a routine chest radiograph can be repurposed to identify patients at elevated CKD risk without requiring laboratory data. CXR-CKD5 may be most valuable as an opportunistic screening tool for non-diabetic patients, a large population not currently targeted for routine kidney function testing, rather than as a replacement for structured monitoring in patients with diabetes, who should continue to receive guideline-recommended laboratory surveillance. Discrimination was meaningful in external validation, though absolute risk estimates require recalibration before deployment at any new institution. We view this work as an early step toward embedding CKD risk awareness into existing imaging workflows, with prospective evaluation needed to assess whether earlier identification translates into improved patient outcomes.
