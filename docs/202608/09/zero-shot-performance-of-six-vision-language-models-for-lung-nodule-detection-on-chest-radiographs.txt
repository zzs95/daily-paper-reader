Radiology-specific vision-language models (VLMs) have shown the ability to generate free-text interpretations for chest radiographs [1-4]. However, the performance of VLMs for detection of individual radiographic findings remains poorly characterized. Lung nodules are clinically important but commonly missed on radiographs, particularly when small or obscured by overlapping anatomy. This study aimed to evaluate the performance of VLMs for lung nodule detection on chest radiographs. This retrospective study using a publicly available deidentified dataset did not constitute human subjects research and thus did not require institutional review board approval. The study used the Japanese Society of Radiological Technology (JSRT) chest radiograph database [5], which includes posteroanterior chest radiographs from 14 institutions [6]. The current investigation included all 247 radiographs in the database, without exclusions. Using corresponding patient information extracted from the database, mean age was 58.2±14.0 years; 119 patients were male, and 128 were female. The database includes text files providing for each radiograph a binary nodule label (presence of a single nodule [n=154] vs absence of a nodule [n=93]; both based on a CT reference standard). For cases with a nodule, the file also indicates nodule size (mean, 17.3 mm) and classification (benign [n=54; based on a composite reference standard including histology, clinical information, and imaging follow-up] vs malignant [n=100; requiring pathologic confirmation]) [6]. The database does not include information regarding nodule type (e.g., ground-glass, part-solid, or solid). Six VLMs were evaluated: RadVLM, gpt-4o-mini, Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct, MedGemma-4b-it, LLaVA-Rad, and CheXpert Plus Model. Because of incomplete public documentation of the VLMs’ original training data, it could not be determined with certainty whether the VLMs

had been exposed during development to the JSRT radiographs or to other resources developed using the JSRT radiographs. The radiographs were provided as input to the VLMs in a zero-shot manner. Specifically, no training, fine-tuning, calibration, cross-validation, or prompt optimization for nodule detection using the database was performed. The radiographs were initially resized according to each VLM’s input requirements. When provided to the models, the radiograph were accompanied by a text prompt instructing the VLMs to generate a “concise report” for the radiograph (aside from the CheXpert Plus Model, for which no text prompt was used). The VLMs’ free-text outputs were saved in CSV or JSON format. Two board-certified general radiologists (MN and HM, with 20 and 9 years of posttraining experience, respectively), blinded to model identity, jointly reviewed the VLM-generated reports, classifying each as positive or negative for nodule presence. For each VLM, sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy were calculated using the database labels as the reference standard. Sensitivity was also computed for benign nodules, malignant nodules, nodules measuring ≥11 mm, and nodules measuring ≥21 mm. The Supplemental Methods provide further details, including the VLM prompts. Tables 1 and 2 summarize VLM performance. Accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity for RadVLM were 44.5%, 11.0%, and 100.0%; for gpt-4o-mini were 39.3%, 6.5%, and 93.5%; for Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct were 38.1%, 7.8%, and 88.2%; for MedGemma-4b-it were 40.9%, 5.2%, and 100.0%; for LLaVA-Rad were 43.7%, 27.3%, and 71.0%; and for CheXpert Plus Model were 36.4%, 0.6%, and 95.7%, respectively. Sensitivity ranged from 0.8% to 30.9% for nodules measuring ≥11 mm, from 0.0% to 37.1% for nodules measuring ≥21 mm, from 0.0% to 18.5% for benign nodules, and from 1.0% to 32.0% for malignant nodules.

This study’s findings indicate poor zero-shot performance of six VLMs for lung nodule detection on chest radiographs. Although the VLMs had moderate or even excellent specificity, they all had very low sensitivity and poor overall accuracy. The findings therefore do not support use of the tested VLMs for lung nodule detection on radiographs and highlight the need for caution in their application for chest radiography interpretation despite potentially promising findings in early studies [1,2,4]. Future studies could evaluate whether task-specific prompt engineering improves nodule detection performance. Limitations include small sample size; use of a single dataset; lack of information regarding nodule type; incomplete public information regarding potential overlap between JSRT radiographs and VLM training data; binary assessment of model outputs without ROC analysis; lack of a human reader study; and lack of comparison with dedicated computer-aided detection systems. 

In conclusion, six VLMs showed poor nodule detection accuracy on radiographs from the JSRT database, with very low sensitivity despite moderate-to-excellent specificity.
