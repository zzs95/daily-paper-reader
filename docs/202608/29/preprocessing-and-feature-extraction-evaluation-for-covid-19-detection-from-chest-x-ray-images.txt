ABSTRACT 

Timely and accurate COVID-19 diagnosis remains a pressing clinical challenge, particularly in settings where RT-PCR testing is not available or delayed. Chest X-ray imaging offers a widely accessible alternative; yet a fundamental question persists in the literature: does the choice of image preprocessing determine diagnostic success more than the classifier itself? To answer this, a controlled factorial experiment was used to evaluate 108 pipeline configurations on a publicly available X-ray dataset of 251 training and 66 test images spanning three classes: COVID-19, Normal, and Viral Pneumonia. Each configuration combined one of four preprocessing techniques (Grayscale conversion, Histogram equalization, Adaptive histogram equalization, Gaussian filtering), one of three feature extractors (HOG, LBP, GLCM), one of three dimensionality reduction strategies (None, PCA, LDA), and one of three classifiers (SVM, KNN, DT). What emerged from this experiment was striking; accuracy ranged from 39.39% to 96.97% across configurations that used identical classifiers and feature extractors, with preprocessing selection alone accounting for the bulk of this 57.58 percentage point spread. Beyond this finding, the experiment revealed that preprocessing and feature extraction do not operate independently; Gaussian filtering actively enhanced the gradient signal that HOG depends on, while grayscale conversion preserved the intensity patterns that LBP encodes, demonstrating that alignment between preprocessing and feature descriptor is what separates high-performing pipelines from poor ones. The best-performing configuration—Gaussian filtering paired with HOG and SVM—reached 96.97% accuracy with macro-averaged precision, recall, and F1-score of 0.97, running entirely on standard CPU hardware with no deep learning infrastructure required. These findings reframe preprocessing from a routine preparatory step into the central design decision of any classical ML-based COVID-19 screening pipeline.

Although RT-PCR testing is the standard diagnostic approach, limitations such as testing availability and delayed results necessitate supplementary diagnostic aids. Chest X-rays (CXR) provide a rapid, widely accessible imaging modality that reveals the pulmonary manifestations associated with COVID-19 infection. Recent advances in Artificial Intelligence (AI) have enabled the automated analysis of these images to assist clinicians by enhancing diagnostic efficiency and consistency [1-3]. Delayed diagnosis of COVID-19 is particularly dangerous for patients with pre-existing comorbidities, such as cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and mental health conditions, where rapid intervention is critical to prevent severe outcomes [4]. A. Dataset Acquisition and Organization 

CXR images were acquired from [26]. The dataset comprises three classes: COVID-19, Normal, and Viral Pneumonia. The methodology begins with loading the images from predefined directories containing training and testing data organized into subfolders representing the three classes. The system utilizes image datastore functions to automatically assign labels based on folder names, enabling systematic data management and class distribution analysis [1]. 

B. Image Preprocessing Pipeline 

All images underwent standardized preprocessing to ensure consistency and optimal feature extraction. A multi-step preprocessing approach was implemented, where images were first resized to a uniform dimension of 128×128 pixels to reduce computational complexity while maintaining essential diagnostic information. Four distinct preprocessing techniques were systematically evaluated: 

 Grayscale Conversion: Images were converted from RGB to grayscale and normalized using the mat2gray function, followed by conversion to uint8 format for standardized pixel intensity representation. 

 Histogram Equalization: The histeq function enhanced image contrast by redistributing pixel intensities across the entire dynamic range, improving visibility of subtle pathological features. 

 Adaptive Histogram Equalization: Using adapthisteq ,localized contrast enhancement was performed, which is particularly beneficial for medical images where different regions may have varying contrast requirements. 

 Gaussian Filtering: A Gaussian filter with a sigma value of 2 was applied to reduce noise while preserving important structural information in the lung parenchyma regions. 

C. Feature Extraction Strategies 

Three complementary feature extraction approaches were employed to capture different aspects of the medical imagery: 

 Histogram of Oriented Gradients (HOG): This technique extracts texture and shape information by analyzing local gradient orientations using 16×16 cell sizes, providing discriminative features for distinguishing between different pathological patterns. 

 Local Binary Patterns (LBP): LBP features capture local texture characteristics by comparing pixel intensities within neighborhoods, utilizing 16×16 cell configurations to maintain spatial resolution while reducing dimensionality. 

 Gray-Level Co-occurrence Matrix (GLCM): Includes statistical texture features, such as contrast, correlation, energy, and homogeneity, computed from GLCM analysis, providing quantitative measures of texture properties with offset parameters of [0, 1]. 

D. Dimensionality Reduction Implementation 

To address the curse of dimensionality and improve classification efficiency, two dimensionality reduction strategies were employed: 

 Principal Component Analysis (PCA): This method retains the first 50 principal components (or fewer if the feature space is smaller) to capture maximum variance while reducing computational burden. Features are standardized by subtracting the mean before applying the PCA transformation. 

 Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA): For multiclass discrimination, this approach projects features into a lower-dimensional space that maximizes class separability. The implementation uses a pseudo-linear discriminant type to handle potential zero variance issues automatically. 

E. Classification Framework 

The following classifiers were evaluated across different pipeline configurations: 

 SVM: Implemented using Error-Correcting Output Codes (ECOC) for multiclass classification via the fitcecoc 

function. The default parameters were used, employing a linear kernel, one-versus-one coding design, and automatic standardization of predictors. For a three-class problem, this requires 3 × (3 − 1)/2 = 3 binary SVM classifiers. This configuration provides robust performance through margin maximization principles. 

 KNN: Configured with = 5 neighbors (selected via 20% validation split), Euclidean distance metric, and equal weight voting. Smaller values ( = 1,3 ) increased overfitting, while larger values ( = 7,10 ) degraded decision boundaries. RESULTS AND ANALYSIS This study introduces an evaluation of COVID-19 CXR classification methodologies to identify optimal algorithmic configurations (within the evaluated dataset) and understand the complex interactions between preprocessing techniques, feature extraction methods, dimensionality reduction approaches, and classification algorithms. This section presents a detailed examination of the 108 unique experimental configurations derived from the factorial combination of four preprocessing methods (Grayscale conversion, Histogram equalization, Adaptive histogram equalization, and Gaussian filtering), three feature extraction techniques (HOG, LBP, and GLCM), three dimensionality reduction strategies (None, PCA, and LDA), and three classification algorithms (SVM, KNN, and DT). The analytical framework focuses on multiple performance metrics to provide comprehensive insight into algorithmic behavior across diverse parameter combinations. Primary evaluation focuses on classification accuracy as the fundamental metric for diagnostic effectiveness, while secondary analysis examines performance consistency, method robustness, and feature-classifier compatibility patterns. The dataset characteristics, including class imbalance in the training set, provide essential context for interpreting classification performance variations. Particular attention is directed toward identifying statistically significant performance differences between methodological approaches, understanding the impact of preprocessing techniques on feature discriminability, and evaluating the effectiveness of dimensionality reduction strategies across different feature types. The analysis addresses critical questions regarding optimal parameter selection for automated COVID-19 diagnosis systems, including the trade-offs between computational efficiency and classification accuracy, the robustness of different algorithmic combinations under varying image quality conditions, and the clinical viability of high-performing configurations. The systematic evaluation reveals notable performance variations ranging from 39.39% to 96.97% accuracy, indicating substantial methodological impact on diagnostic capability. This wide performance spectrum underscores the critical importance of appropriate technique selection and parameter optimization in medical imaging applications, where diagnostic accuracy directly influences patient care outcomes and clinical decision-making processes.

The dataset consists of CXR images collected from Montreal University's affiliated hospitals. A total of 317 images were used, split into training and test sets following the original dataset's predefined partition. F. Statistical Significance and Reliability Assessment 

The systematic evaluation identified PCA warnings indicating linear dependency in feature matrices, suggesting potential overfitting concerns. The consistent appearance of these warnings across multiple configurations indicates the need for feature selection or regularization techniques to improve model generalization capability. 

G. Clinical Implications and Optimization Recommendations 

The optimal configuration (Gaussian+HOG+SVM), within the bounds of the evaluated dataset, achieved 96.97% accuracy, representing a clinically viable diagnostic tool for COVID-19 screening. The high performance of this combination suggests that gradient-based features effectively capture pathological patterns in CXR images when enhanced through appropriate noise reduction techniques. The superior performance of SVM classifiers indicates their suitability for medical imaging applications requiring high precision and reliability. This evaluation framework successfully identified optimal parameter combinations (within the bounds of the evaluated dataset) while revealing the complex interactions between preprocessing, feature extraction, and classification components in automated COVID-19 diagnosis systems. The experimental results show that high accuracy was achieved with traditional ML. 

H. Statistical Analysis of the Full Experimental Space 

To provide a comprehensive understanding of performance distribution across all 108 experimental configurations, a full statistical analysis was conducted. The accuracy distribution exhibited a mean of 72.6%, standard deviation of 14.3%, median of 74.2%, minimum of 39.39%, and maximum of 96.97%. The wide standard deviation of 14.3 percentage points is a significant finding that confirms that method selection, rather than random variation or dataset noise, is the dominant driver of performance differences across configurations. This wide spread validates the study's central premise that systematic pipeline evaluation is necessary and consequential. Further breakdown of the distribution by classifier shows interesting behavioral differences between the algorithms. SVM has an average accuracy of 76.82% with a standard deviation of 18.94%, while KNN has an average accuracy of 72.35% with a standard deviation of 9.87%, and DT has an average accuracy of 66.52% with a standard deviation of 8.43%. The high standard deviation of SVM compared to those of KNN and DT is an important observation. This shows that the performance of SVM is highly dependent on the upstream pipeline configurations. While SVM can attain the global maximum accuracy of 96.97% when combined with the right upstream configurations, it can attain some of the lowest accuracy values when combined with inappropriate configurations. This makes SVM both the highest-rewarded and highest-risk classifier in the evaluation. When examining the results based on feature extractors, similar observations can be noted. For HOG features, a mean accuracy of 78.4% and a standard deviation of 16.2% were observed. For LBP features, a mean accuracy of 74.8% and a standard deviation of 12.6% were observed. Finally, for GLCM features, a mean accuracy of 63.1% and a standard deviation of 9.8% were observed. GLCM features demonstrate the worst mean accuracy (63.1%) compared to HOG (78.4%) and LBP (74.8%). Notably, GLCM exhibits the lowest standard deviation (9.8%), indicating consistent underperformance across configurations rather than high variability. In contrast, HOG features, despite achieving the highest mean accuracy (78.4%), exhibit a higher standard deviation (16.2%), revealing greater sensitivity to preprocessing method selection. This pattern—where superior mean performance is coupled with higher variance—demonstrates that optimal feature extractors require careful preprocessing alignment to achieve their best performance, while less effective extractors, such as GLCM, fail to benefit substantially from preprocessing optimization. In comparison, SVM classifiers, evaluated across all feature and preprocessing combinations, achieved a mean accuracy of 76.82% with a standard deviation of 18.94%, demonstrating even greater variance than any single feature extractor. This suggests that classifier selection and feature extraction interact significantly in determining overall pipeline performance. HOG features attain their peak accuracy under Gaussian filter preprocessing, but are affected by other filters. As for the evaluation robustness, the fixed train/test split approach, predetermined by the structure of the dataset, does not allow for cross-validation. With a test set comprising 66 images, a difference in correct classification of one image equates to 1.52 percent in terms of accuracy. Therefore, the 1.52 percent difference between the highest and second-highest performing configurations must be viewed appropriately. Nevertheless, the significant difference in performance between broader methodological approaches, e.g., SVM vs. DT (difference of 10.3 percent on average) and HOG vs. GLCM (difference of 15.3 percent on average), is sufficiently high as to still hold even under this constraint. Future research will seek to verify the consistency of these rankings across different dataset splits, utilizing stratified k-fold cross-validation on a larger dataset. 

I. Limitation and Future Work 1) Limitations 

 The dataset exhibited class imbalance, potentially biasing the classifiers toward majority classes and limiting generalizability across varying real-world prevalence scenarios. The training set exhibited class imbalance, with COVID-19 comprising 44.2% of samples. No resampling (e.g., SMOTE) or cost-sensitive learning was applied. Future studies should incorporate imbalance-handling strategies and report class-stratified metrics. 

 Persistent linear dependency warnings during PCA suggest feature redundancy and potential overfitting, impacting model robustness on unseen data. 

 Empirical validation of overfitting via training/validation loss curves or learning curves was not performed due to the fixed split evaluation design. 

 This study is limited to a single publicly available dataset.

CONCLUSION This study conducted a controlled factorial experiment that isolates the causal contribution of each pipeline component and quantifies interaction effects—a distinction that yields generalizable design rules rather than dataset-specific rankings. The identified interaction effects—particularly the synergy between Gaussian filtering and HOG features—provide theoretically grounded and immediately actionable guidance for pipeline design in resource-constrained clinical environments. Preprocessing pipeline selection is a primary determinant of COVID-19 classification performance, with accuracy spanning 57.58 percentage points across the 108 systematically evaluated configurations. Gradient-based features (HOG with SVM) emerged as the most discriminative representations for detecting pulmonary pathology, corroborating findings from the broader medical image analysis literature. Furthermore, PCA dimensionality reduction benefits LBP features, providing theoretically grounded insights into how pipeline components interact within classical ML frameworks for medical imaging. The optimal configuration—Gaussian filtering combined with HOG feature extraction and SVM classification— achieved 96.97% accuracy with macro-averaged precision, recall, and F1-score of 0.97 across three classes (mean across all configurations: 72.6%, SD: 14.3%). The 57.58 percentage-point spread in overall performance validates the necessity of this systematic evaluation and demonstrates that configuration selection fundamentally determines diagnostic capability. Critically, this pipeline requires no GPUs, minimal training data, and no DL infrastructure—making it immediately deployable in rural and resource-limited healthcare settings. The interpretability of handcrafted features provides clinician trust in diagnostic decisions, a practical advantage absent in black-box deep learning systems. Future work should validate these findings across multi-institutional datasets to assess generalizability and explore lightweight ensemble methods that enhance performance while maintaining clinical accessibility.
