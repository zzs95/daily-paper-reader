Abstract 

Fluorescein fundus angiography (FFA) is essential for diagnosing retinal vascular diseases, yet 

its interpretation is expertise -intensive. Here, we present Clin -FFA -VLM , a multimodal vision –

language framework that mirrors retina specialists ’ cognitive workflow by decomposing FFA 

interpretation into three stages: lesion -aware visual perception, clinical report generation, and 

diagnostic decision support. Trained and tested on a multi -center dataset of 13,178 FFA images 

with expert label , 21,717 FFA images with 1,790 clinical reports and diagnosis across 7 retinal 

disease s, Clin -FFA -VLM achieves an F1 of 0.834 for lesion detection, an entity -level F1 of 0.73 

for report genera tion, and a diagnostic F1 of 0.77 by jointly reasoning over images and self -

generated reports . External validation across two independent hospitals confirmed its 

generalizability (F1 of 0.78 and 0.70). In a prospective reader study with 20 0 FFA cases, Clin -

FFA -VL M significantly improved diagnostic accuracy for medical students and residents 

(p<0.05), bridging the gap between automated systems and clinical practice. ARTICLE IN PRESS

Introduction 

Fluorescein fundus angiography (FFA) remains the clinical gold standard for diagnosing and 

assessing broad range of retinal vascular disease ,1,2 providing functional hemodynamic 

information such as vascular leakage and hemorrhage that non -in vasive alternatives like OCT -A

cannot yet capture. 3,4 Interpreting FFA relies on expert knowledge and follow s the clinical 

workflow : clinicians must review a sequence to track phase -dependent changes in fluorescence, 

identify and localize lesion s, and integrate them into a concise structured report that can be used 

to support diagnostic decision -making . In routine practice, this process remains a significant 

challenge due to high case volume, variable image quality, and uneven access to experienced 

retina specialists. 5

Recent progress in deep learning has improved performance on individua l ophthalmic 

imaging task, including lesion detection 6,7 , report generation 8,9 and disease classification 10 .

However, most system s we re designed as single -purpose predictors, which d id not match how 

clinicians actually work with FFA. Lesion detection models often operate in isolation. Yet, 

detecting lesions is rarely the final goal of clinical management. Report -generation pipelines are 

typically evaluated purely as text -production systems. They prioritize linguistic fluency but 

rarely demonstrate that their reports can reliably support downstream diagnosis .11 Diagnostic 

models are often trained directly end -to -end from images, resulting in limited interpretability and 

posing challenges for clinical adoption .12 This creates a key translational gap for FFA: clinicians 

interpret angiography through an evidence -to -report -to -decision workflow, yet current methods 

rarely integrate these steps into a deployment -consistent pipeline where t he generated report is 

explicitly leveraged to support diagnostic inference and clinical decision -making. 

Vision –language models (VLMs) jointly learn visual understanding and natural -language 

generation, enabling them to map medical images to clinically interpretable text .13 –15 While 

general -purpose VLMs often suffer from scarcity of domain knowledge 16 , vision -language 

models in ophthalmology like EyeCLIP 17 and VisionUnite 18 show remarkable performance 

across multi -task, multi -modal abilities. However, these architectures predominantly operate as ARTICLE IN PRESS 

ARTICLE IN PRESS 

end -to -end predictors that bypass the structured reasoning process inherent to clini cal 

interpretation. By mapping images directly to diagnostic outputs , they fail to provide the 

auditable intermediate findings necessary for clinician verification . This may cause 

hallucinations , generating medically inaccurate information , that limit s their transparency and 

trustworthiness in high -stakes diagnostic settings. 19,20 Recent study has shifted towards clinically 

aligned workflows to increase interpretability and robustness. 21,22 By integrating domain -specific 

priors into the model ing process, these approaches move beyond opaque prediction to 

transparent reasoning . However, such clinically grounded frameworks have yet to be explored in 

FFA, which is inherently a multi -modal diagnostic tool that relies on the integration of dynamic 

imaging and structured clinical reports. 

To address these challenges, we present Clin -FFA -VLM, a clinician -aligned vision -language 

framework built for FFA that mimics the clinician’s cognitive workflow for dynamic 

angiographic interpretation . Clin -FFA -VL M is trained via a progressive three -stage curriculum 

that mirrors professional education: (1) a lesion -aware backbone is first established to capture 

fine -grained angiographic features; (2) the model is fine -tuned to synthesize these findings into 

concise , clinical -style reports; and (3) the model is further trained to infer diagnoses by 

conditioning on both raw FFA sequences and the self -generated reports . We empirically validate 

that this training strategy yields robust performance gains over single -moda lity baselines and 

generic Large Vision -Language Models. Through comprehensive validation across multi -center 

retrospective cohorts and a perspective clinical reader study, we demonstrate that Clin -FFA -

VLM achieves diagnostic accuracy comparable to residen ts, and measurably elevates the 

performance of junior ophthalmologists towards senior -level proficiency. .

Clin -FFA -VLM development and validation using multicenter datasets 

We developed Clin -FFA -VLM to emulate routine FFA interpretation (Figure 1A). Rather than 

assembling independent modules, we built Clin -FFA -VLM on Qwen2.5 -VL -7B and applied a 

three -stage progressive training curriculum (Figure 1B), where each stage initializes from the 

weights of the previous one, sequentially expanding the model's capabilities : (i) recognize 

angiographic image features , (ii) translate findings into coherent real -world style reports, and (iii) 

integrate raw FFA sequences with its self -generated report to support diagnosis. Clin -FFA -VLM 

was trained on 12,666 images wi th expert annotation and 12 ,574 images in 1241 sequences with 

clinical report and diagnosis. As summarized in Table 1 , 2, we retrospectively curated internal 

datasets from the Eye Center at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University (ZJU2), 

including ZJU2 -annotation for lesion detection and ZJU2 -report for report generation and 

diagnosis. We further assessed cross -institution generalizability on two independent external 

cohorts : TZ -report from Taizhou First People’s Hospital and XJU2 -report f rom the Second 

Affiliated Hospital of Xi’an Jiaotong University . And we finally conducted prospective diagnosis

Diagnostic performance with FFA and self -generated report 

In the final training stage (Stage 3), the model was further trained for diagnostic reasoning, 

building on top of the Stage 2, yielding the final Clin -FFA -VLM. During inference, the model 

autonomously generates a clinical report and t hen infers the disease category by jointly 

processing the raw FFA image sequences and its self -generated report. To rigorously evaluate 

this capability, we examined four inference configurations on the internal test set: the full 

multimodal setting (Image + PredReport), unimodal baselines using either images alone (Image 

only) or self -generated reports alone (PredReport only), and an oracle setting using images with 

ground -truth clinician reports (Image + GTReport). Additionally, we benchmarked our 

framewor k against pipelines based on classical image classification baseline (ResNet -50) and 

generic VLMs, specifically GPT -4o and Hulu -Med .

The proposed multimodal Clin -FFA -VLM (Image + PredReport) demonstrated robust 

diagnostic capability, achieving a weighted F1 -score of 0.768 ± 0.031 . Crucially, the system 

outperformed generic large models by a substantial margin: the GPT -4o pipeline achieved an F1 

of only 0.506 ± 0.03 9, and the Hulu -Med pipeline reached 0. 232 ± 0.0 34 , consistent with the 

expected limitations of models not trained on domain -specific angiographic data . Additinally, 

our framework significantly outperformed ResNet -50 in F1 (0.768 ± 0.032 vs. 0.662 ± 0.037, p = 

0.007), further supporting the advantage of report -based multimodal reasoning over direc t image 

classification. 

Ablation studies further highlighted the value of the multimodal strategy. The full 

framework yielded a statistically significant improvement over the Image -only baseline (F1 = 

0.682 , p < 0.05 ), confirming that the generated report s serve as a valuable intermediate reasoning 

step that complements visual data. Furthermore, the multimodal model outperformed the 

PredReport -only baseline (F1 = 0.727 ), achieving the highest overall performance. The oracle 

setting (Image + GTReport) achie ved an F1 of 0.937 , indicating a high performance ceiling for ARTICLE IN PRESS

Prospective reader study and clinical validation 

We evaluated the clinical utility of Clin -FFA -VLM in a comprehensive prospective reader study 

involving 20 0 de -identified FFA examinations. The study comprised five readers spanning 

distinct experience levels, including two medical students, two residents, and a senior 

ophthalmologist who served as the expert benchmark. The students and residents performed 

diag noses in two separate rounds: an initial unaided Round 1, followed by an AI -assisted Round 

2 where they referenced the model -generated report and diagnosis. 

The introduction of AI assistance yielded improvements in diagnostic performance across 

both train ee groups . Medical students demonstrated the most substantial gain, with their F1 score 

surging from a baseline of 0.566 in the unaided round to 0.755 in the assisted round. This 

significant increase (+0.189, p < 0.05) enabl es novice readers to successfull y surpass the 

standalone AI baseline (0.730). Residents , who started with a high unaided baseline (0.783) that 

already outperformed the AI model, achieved F1 score improvement to 0.817 (+0.034, p < 0.05) .

Notably, the senior ophthalmologist set a high expe rt benchmark of 0.849. 

Further stratification by disease etiology reveals that AI assistance was particularly 

impactful for challenging diagnoses.(Figure 5C) For medical students, the framework drove 

substantial F1 -score gains in identifying inflammatory and vascular pathologies, most notably 

Vogt –Koyanagi –Harada disease (+49%) and BRVO (+ 23 %). Case -level analysis (Figure 5D) 

further confirmed that AI assistance successfully corrected initially erroneous diagnoses in 

23.5% of student cases and 8.0% of resi dent cases. Moreover, correct initial decisions that were 

overturned by the AI was contained to 4.5% across both groups

Discussion 

In this study, we introduced Clin -FFA -VLM, a clinician -aligned framework that mi rrors the 

ophthalmologist’s cognitive workflow —progressing systematically from perceiving lesion 

features to synthesizing structured reports, and finally deriving a diagnosis.. Our extensive 

validation confirms that this transparent, stepwise approach yiel ds superior performance 

compared to traditional methods. The multimodal fusion model (Image + Pred Report) achieved a 

robust weighted F1 -score of 0.725. Moreover , this accuracy is not confined to internal datasets; 

the model demonstrated robust generalizability, maintaining an F1 -score in external set. Our 

prospective reader study validated the model’s real -world utility as improving residents’ and 

students’ diagnostic ability and even out -performed the model’s baseline. These results 

demonstrate that aligning AI architecture with the clinical reasoning pathway of FFA 

interpretation can yield a system that is not only accurate but also clinically auditable and useful. ARTICLE IN PRESS

Our findings resonate with emerging evidence suggesting that clinically aligne d reasoning 

frameworks are prerequisites for robust medical AI. Some of the recent study have shown that 

clinically aligned reasoning framework improves diagnosis ability and report quality. Holland et 

al. 21 intr oduced a specialized training curriculum for retinal analysis that mirrors the 

ophthalmologist’s learning path, progressing from fundamental biomarker recognition to 

complex disease staging and referral. Zhong et al. 22 proposed an agent -based framework for CT 

pulmonary angiography that decomposes interpretation into fine -grained abnormality detection 

followed by structured reporting and prognosis. These advances, together with our results, 

suggest that aligning AI architectures with clinical reasoning workflows is a possible direction 

for interpretable medical imaging analysis in FFA , though further validation across diverse 

modalities is needed to establish its generalizability .

A practical barrier to applying generic foundation models to FFA is their lack of domain -

specific clinical perception .27 While generic VLMs (e.g., GPT -4o) , and e ven those with FFA as 

pretrained data (e.g., Hulu -Med) excel at des cribing natural images and even color fundus 

photography 28,29 , our results reveal they fail to grasp the nuanced details of FFA (phase, 

location). Interpreting angiography requires more than just object detection, it demands expert 

knowledge to distinguis h physiological states, such as differentiating "active leakage" (a 

pathological sign) from "staining" (a static artifact). This limited perception directly compounds 

the hallucination risk inherent to generative AI. 19,30 Generic VLMs may not only missed critical 

clinical details but also produced reports with low factual grounding (Entity F1 ~0.51 –0.53). 

These findings, while consistent with expectations, highlight the necessity of do main -specific 

training for reliable FFA interpretation. 

Existing ophthalmologic AI is the reliance on 'black -box' end -to -end classification, which 

outputs diagnostic labels without providing the underlying rationale. 31,32 In contrast to these 

workflows, our framework introduces an intermediate explainable reasoning layer via structured 

report generation. Our analysis confirms that this transparency enhances rather than ARTICLE IN PRESS 

ARTICLE IN PRESS 

compromi ses accuracy. The multimodal fusion model (Image + Pred Report) significantly 

outperformed the image -only baseline (F1: 0.725 vs. 0.630). This indicates that the generated 

report effectively functions as a semantic filter, distilling complex angiographic data into clear 

clinical concepts while suppressing the noise inherent in raw imaging. Our ablation study was 

specifically designed to disentangle the contributions of visual and textual modalities and to test 

whether the report -based pipeline reflects gen uine multimodal reasoning or merely reduces the 

task to text classification. The graded performance across three controlled conditions —Image -

only (0.682) < PredReport -only (0.733) < Image+PredReport (0.768) —demonstrates that neither 

modality alone matches the combined model . This indicates that visual features contribute 

diagnostic information beyond the report text alone. These findings support genuine cross -modal 

integration rather than a simple reduction to text classification. 

Generalizability across d iverse clinical settings remains a primary challenge for medical AI 

deployment. 33 We rigorously assessed this by deploying Clin -FFA -VLM on two independent 

external cohorts (TZ and XJU2). A key insight from this validation is that variations in reporting 

style do not compromise core medical accuracy. While we observed exp ected fluctuations in 

surface -level linguistic metrics (e.g., BLEU) due to heterogeneous institutional writing norms, 

the model demonstrated robustness in preserving core clinical semantics. Specifically, the Entity 

F1remained robust, showing only a modest decline from 0.73 (internal) to 0.64 –0.66 (external). 

This indicates that our lesion -aware backbone successfully anchors the model to visual evidence 

rather than overfitting to local textual patterns. Consequently, this semantic stability translated 

into reliable diagnostic performance: the model achieved a weighted F1 of 0.765 in the TZ 

cohort, matching internal baselines, and retained substantial utility (0.602) in the XJU cohort. 

Our stratified analysis demonstrated stable performance across different s equence lengths, and 

the cross -center evaluation confirmed robustness to acquisition variability. The impact of other 

factors such as image quality remains a direction for future investigation. ARTICLE IN PRESS

A central advantage of Clin -FFA -VLM is that it turns diagnosis into an auditable, human -in -

the -loop process by exposing a structured intermediate report. By presenting an intermediate 

report, we allow clinicians to audit the diagnostic process, identifying whether errors stem from 

missed lesions or interpretation flaws. This transparency acts as a rigorous safety mechanism; 

our analysis shows that when conditioned on ground -truth reports(Image+GT Report), diagnostic 

performance surged to 0.923, approaching expert equivalence. This theoretical potential was 

validated in our prospective reader study, where the collaboration yielded a clear benefit: the AI 

assistance not only significantly improved the F1 scores of both residents and medical students , 

but cruc ially, allowed them to outperform the standalone model itself. This confirms that the 

structured report functions as a verifiable reference allowing clinicians to filter out potential 

hallucinations and leverage the system as a highly reliable diagnostic c ompanion rather than a 

standalone replacement, as qualitatively demonstrated by the auditability cases in Supplementary 

Figure 5.

Our study has several limitations. First, regarding data and label quality , all participating 

centers are located within Chin a, and the model currently operates in Mandarin, which together 

limit conclusions about robustness across linguistically and demographically heterogeneous 

international settings. Additionally, inter -observer agreement for lesion annotations and clinical 

re ports was not formally assessed, v ariability in reporting style and diagnostic emphasis among 

annotators may introduce label noise. Future work will prioritize validation in multi -ethnic 

cohorts and extension to multilingual settings with formal inter -rater reliability evaluation .

Second, our empirical validation is currently confined to FFA, a n invasive imaging modality .

While FFA provides irreplaceable functional hemodynamic information that OCT -A cannot yet 

capture, 1,3,4 the stage -wise design is potentially transferable to other imaging workflows that 

follow a similar perception -to -report -to -diagnosis reasonin g process like OCTA. This represent s

a natural architectural adaptation, as it would allow for the integration of structural vascular 

information with FFA's functional hemodynamic data .34 Third, our system supports only ARTICLE IN PRESS 

ARTICLE IN PRESS 

diagnostic decision; as diagnosis is only one stage of the clinical workflow, future work will aim 

to extend the framework to assist with treatment planning and prognosis. Also, while our 

ablation studies provide evidence for cross -modal inte gration, further mechanistic analyses such 

as attention visualization or gradient -based attribution across modalities could deepen 

understanding of how visual and textual features interact during diagnostic inference. Finally, 

although our reader study val idates the effectiveness of our system, it inevitably has certain 

limitations. (1) It was conducted in a simulated environment , which does not fully replicate the 

complexities of real -world clinical workflows . C linicians may over -rely on AI suggestions and 

uncritically accept its outputs. While our case -level analysis showed that AI -induced errors were 

limited to 4.5%, this risk may be amplified in routine clinical practice where attention to AI 

outputs may be less critical. (2) The sample of five readers a nd 200 cases provides adequate 

power to detect large effect sizes (e.g., the observed F1 improvement), but may be underpowered 

to detect smaller effects among experienced clinicians. (3) The sequential crossover design , in 

which the AI -assisted diagnosis followed the unaided round , may introduce learning or 

familiarity bias despite washout period and randomized case order . Future studies should 

incorporate larger reader panels , adequately powered sample sizes determined through formal 

power analysis, and r andomized crossover designs with balanced subsets to further mitigate 

these limitations and strengthen the robustness of the findings .

Overall, we present Clin -FFA -VLM, a clinical -aligned multimodal framework that 

transforms FFA interpretation into a tran sparent, three -step workflow: observe lesions, generate 

structured reports, and then diagnose. The framework shows robust generalization across 

multiple centers and significantly improves diagnostic performance for clinicians in prospective 

reader study. This work demonstrates that aligning AI training with the clinical reasoning 

pathway of FFA interpretation can produce an auditable decision -support system, offering a 

validated framework for augmenting FFA interpretation in clinical settings.

Study Design 

This study was designed to develop and clinically evaluate Clin -FFA -VLM, a clinician -aligned, 

multi -level visual –language framework for fundus fluorescein angiography (FFA) interpretation. 

Instead of directly predicting disease labels from raw FFA images, Clin -FFA -VLM mirrors the 

cognitive workflow of retina specialists by sequentially performing lesion identification, report 

generation, and diagnostic decision support. The overall study design compris ed three key 

phases: (1) the collection and curation of a multi -source, multi -task FFA dataset; (2) stepwise 

model training and development; and (3) clinical validation via external testing and a prospective 

reader study. 

First, to establish a robust data foundation, we retrospectively collected and curated FFA 

examinations from multiple centers to construct three distinct datasets: an annotation dataset with 

image -level lesion labels, a report dataset containing paired FFA sequences and clinician -

authored reports, and a diagnosis dataset comprising FFA –report –diagnosis triplets. 

Additionally, external cohorts from independent centers were assembled for generalizability 

testing, and a temporally independent cohort was defined for prospective clinical valida tion. 

Second, we developed Clin -FFA -VLM using a hierarchical, multi -stage training strategy 

aligned with the clinical workflow. A lesion -aware backbone was first trained on the annotation 

dataset to learn FFA -specific visual representations, capturing bot h specific lesion patterns and 

global image features. This backbone was then fine -tuned for report generation using the FFA –

report dataset and subsequently adapted to jointly perform report generation and diagnostic ARTICLE IN PRESS 

ARTICLE IN PRESS 

decision support using the FFA –report –di agnosis dataset. Conventional deep -learning models 

and general -purpose Vision –Language Models (VLMs) served as baselines for lesion detection, 

report generation, and disease classification. 

Third, we conducted clinical validation at two levels. The quanti tative performance of Clin -

FFA -VLM and baseline models was evaluated on internal test sets and external cohorts to assess 

generalizability. Furthermore, we performed a prospective, multi -level reader study using a 

temporally independent FFA cohort. In this study, ophthalmologists, residents, and medical 

students independently interpreted FFA sequences under masked conditions, both with and 

without the assistance of Clin -FFA -VLM. Their diagnostic accuracy was compared to evaluate 

the framework's potential as an interpretable decision -support tool in clinical practice. 

Dataset Curation 

To ensure robust model development and rigorous clinical evaluation, we constructed a 

comprehensive, multi -source repository of FFA examinations comprising seven distinct cohort s

from three independent medical centers. The dataset curation process was designed to support 

the three -stage training curriculum and multi -level validation of Clin -FFA -VLM. A detailed 

breakdown of the datasets is provided in Table 1 , 2.

The foundationa l data were retrospectively collected from the Eye Center at the Second 

Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University (ZJU2). The annotation and report datasets were 

originally curated in our prior studies 6,8 ; here we adopted the same cohorts and applied 

additional task -specific processing for the current framework. Based on specific task 

requirements, we curated two primary subsets . ZJU2 -Annotation (Lesion Detection ) dataset 

spans from August 2016 to December 2021 and includes 12,666 images from 1,495 eyes for 

training and 512 images from 424 eyes for testing. Image -level lesion labels were established 

following the multi -rater protocol detailed in our prior work 6. Briefly, after excluding images of 

uninterpretable quality (e.g., turbid media or extensive haemorrhage obscuring the retina), each 

remaining image was independently labelled by two trained medical students, with ARTICLE IN PRESS 

ARTICLE IN PRESS 

disagreements adjudicated by a b oard -certified ophthalmologist. Annotators were qualified prior 

to formal labelling via a standardized guideline review and a benchmark test, and only students 

whose accuracy matched the ophthalmologist average were admitted to the annotation stage. 

This d ual -annotation -with -expert -adjudication design was adopted to reduce per -rater bias and 

improve label consistency across the dataset. ZJU2 -Report (Report Generation & Diagnosis )

cover s a broader timeframe from August 2016 to December 2023, this dataset con tains paired 

FFA sequences and clinical reports (Train: 1,2 41 reports; Test: 178 reports). The report dataset 

was curated following the pipeline detailed in our prior work 8. Briefly, FFA cases together with 

their corresponding free -text clinical reports were retrieved from the hospital's picture archiving 

and communication system (PACS) and were excluded if the FFA images were of poor quality 

(e.g., significant blur or high noise arising from pathological or technical factors) or if a 

correspondin g free -text report was missing or substantially incomplete. We selected a range of 5 

to 16 images during collection to ensure consistent model input. The retained reports were then 

reviewed by two trained ophthalmology residents under the supervision of a senior 

ophthalmologist, who corrected typographical errors and removed reports with incomplete 

clinical information . Crucially, to establish a clear and standardized benchmark for the diagnostic 

task, we defined it as a single -label classification problem. We therefore filtered the dataset to 

ensure each case was assigned a single primary diagnosis. Compared to our prior dataset, we 

refined the diagnostic taxonomy in two ways: (1) diabetic retinopathy was subdivided into non -

proliferative (NPDR) and prolife rative (PDR) stages to reflect their distinct angiographic patterns 

and clinical management pathways; and (2) only neovascular (wet) age -related macular 

degeneration (wAMD) was retained, as dry AMD does not typically require FFA examination 

and was therefo re excluded. The final diagnostic categories comprised seven disease types and 

normal controls, as detailed in Table 2. 

To assess the cross -center robustness of Clin -FFA -VLM against variations in imaging 

devices and population demographics, we assembled t wo independent external test cohorts: ARTICLE IN PRESS 

ARTICLE IN PRESS 

XJU2 -Report (Comprising 202 reports and 2,258 images from the Second Affiliated Hospital of 

Xi ’an Jiaotong University ) and TZ -Report (Comprising 169 reports and 3,016 images from 

Taizhou First People ’s Hospital ). Both external datasets followed the same curation standards as 

the internal set but were strictly held out during all training stages. 

To facilitate a bias -free evaluation of clinical utility, we collected a temporally prospective 

independent cohort ZJU2 -Prospective consisting of 20 0 consecutive eyes (2, 082 images) 

examined between October 2024 and October 2025. Crucially, strict temporal separation was 

enforced to ensure zero overlap between this validation set and the retrospective training data ,

thereby preventing data leakage and ensuring a rigorous assessment of the model's performance in 

a simulated real -world deployment setting. 

Model framework and training strategy 

Clin -FFA -VLM was implemented by adapting Qwen2.5 -VL -7B, a 7 -billion -parameter open -

source vision –language foundation model. 24 We employed a progressive, three -stage curriculum 

designed to mirror the clinical cognitive hierarchy of FFA interpretation by vision instruction 

tuning 23 : perception (lesion recognition), synthesis (report generation), and deduction 

(diagnosis). 

Stage 1: First, we established a lesion -aware backbone using the VQA -formatted annotation 

dataset. To con struct a diverse and instruction -rich training corpus, we leveraged GPT -4o to 

synthesize 92,120 visual question –answering (VQA) pairs based on the ground -truth lesion 

annotations (detailed prompts are provided in Supplementary Materials “Lesion detection V QA 

generation prompt” ). This synthesis process transformed static labels into varied natural 

language interactions. Consequently, each training instance consisted of a single FFA image 

paired with a structured question –answer sequence , transforming the generic visual encoder into 

a specialized feature extractor capable of capturing FFA -specific semantics. 

Stage 2: Second, the model was initialized with the Stage -1 weights and fine -tuned to 

synthesize coherent, clinician -style narratives using the FFA -Report dataset. The model was ARTICLE IN PRESS 

ARTICLE IN PRESS 

instructed to generate a full expert report based on the input FFA sequence. To prevent 

catastrophic forgetting —where the model loses its lesion -recognition capability while adapting 

to linguistic patterns —we employed a continu ous training strategy: approximately 5% of the 

Stage 1 lesion -QA samples (ZJU2 -Annotation training set) were randomly interleaved into each 

training epoch. This ensured the model remained anchored to visual evidence while mastering 

professional reporting t erminology. 

Stage 3 (Clin -FFA -VLM) : Third, we adapted the model for disease classification using the 

same FFA -Report dataset. A "Teacher -Forcing" strategy was adopted during training, where the 

model was conditioned on ground -truth expert reports to learn the optimal diagnostic logic 

mapping. During deployment, the system operates in an autonomous "Report -then -Diagnose" 

workflow, utilizing its self -generated report as an intermediate reasoning context to infer the 

diagnosis. This design ensures that the fin al decision is explicitly grounded in the model's own 

descriptive findings. 

All training stages utilized the same Qwen2.5 -VL -7B backbone with sequential weight 

initialization. We employed Parameter -Efficient Fine -Tuning (PEFT) 35 via Low -Rank 

Adaptation (LoRA) 36 applied to both the vision –language projector and the language model 

attention layers (rank = 8). Training was orchestrated using the Llama -Factory 37 framework on a 

single N VIDIA RTX 3090 GPU. 

Evaluation Metrics 

To comprehensively assess Clin -FFA -VLM, we conducted a multi -level evaluation covering 

lesion recognition, report generation, diagnostic decision support, and clinical utility. We

compared our model against four categ ories of baselines. First, a supervised deep -learning 

baseline (ResNet -50) was trained on our training set and served as the reference for both lesion 

detection (Stage 1) and disease diagnosis (Stage 3), reflecting the most widely adopted 

architecture in r etinal image classification. Second, a specialized medical report -generation 

baseline (M2KT) was included for Stage 2 as a representative non -LLM approach that integrates ARTICLE IN PRESS 

ARTICLE IN PRESS 

a learned medical knowledge base with multi -modal alignment, enabling direct comparis on 

against a state -of -the -art specialized architecture for medical narrative synthesis. Third, two 

generic vision -language models (GPT -4o and Qwen2.5 -VL -7B) were evaluated as reference 

points to characterize the domain gap when applying general -purpose fou ndation models to 

specialized angiographic interpretation without task -specific adaptation. Fourth, a medical -

domain vision -language model (Hulu -Med), whose pretraining corpus explicitly includes 

Fluorescein Angiography, was included as the architecturally compatible foundation -model 

reference. We note that GPT -4o and Hulu -Med were included as reference points to illustrate the 

domain gap rather than as competitive benchmarks; the primary evidence for our framework's 

effectiveness derives from the internal ablation studies and from the comparisons with the 

domain -specific baselines (ResNet -50 and M2KT). 

Data privacy and deployment consistency were strictly maintained across all baseline 

evaluations: GPT -4o was queried via API with rigorous removal of all p atient -identifying 

information from images and prompts, while Hulu -Med, M2KT, and ResNet -50 were deployed 

locally on secure institutional servers using the same de -identified datasets as our framework. 

For lesion detection task, we formulated it as a multi -label visual question -answering task. 

Given a single FFA image, each model was prompted to identify all present lesion types in a single 

response. For Clin -FFA -VLM, the model generated lesion names via open -ended text generation 

to reflect its i nternalized domain knowledge; the mentioned lesion types were then extracted and 

matched against a predefined lesion dictionary to obtain multi -label predictions. Here, generic 

VLMs (GPT -4o, Qwen -VL, and Hu lu-Med) were provided with multiple -choice, multip le -answer 

candidate options to align their outputs with standard clinical categories. We used F1 -score as the 

primary metric .

For report generation task, we controlled for stylistic variations by prompting all models 

with a standardized clinical guidelin e ( see Supplementary Materials “Report generation 

evaluation prompt” ). Quality was assessed via linguistic metrics (BLEU -439 , ROUGE -L40 ,ARTICLE IN PRESS 

ARTICLE IN PRESS 

CIDEr 41 ), semantic consistency (BERTScore 42 ). Moreover, we custom Entity -level F1 score to 

verify medical factualness. We extracted mentions of key pathological entities (Supplementary 

Materials “Entity Extraction Dictionary” part) from both generated and reference reports, 

applying rule -based normalization to compute F1 scores. 

We then performed an expert -based evaluation of report quality on the internal test set. All 

178 AI -generated reports were independently scored by two ophthalmologists, both of whom 

were blinded to the corresponding ground -truth clinical reports and to each other's ratings. F or 

each report, raters independently assessed two dimensions on a 5 -point Likert scale, the detailed 

rating criteria are presented in Supplementary Materials(See Report Generation Expert 

Evaluation Rubric): 

Correctness: the factual accuracy of the describ ed angiographic findings (e.g., lesion type, 

location, phase -specific dynamics) relative to the corresponding FFA sequence, with higher 

scores indicating fewer factual errors or hallucinated findings. 

Completeness: the coverage of clinically relevant find ings necessary for diagnostic 

reasoning, with higher scores indicating more comprehensive reporting of pathologically 

meaningful features. 

Both raters jointly reviewed a calibration set of 10 reports (not included in the 178 -report 

evaluation cohort) to a lign their interpretation of the rubric. Inter -rater agreement was quantified 

using mean absolute disagreement between raters to characterize the magnitude of scoring 

discrepancy. 

For d iagnostic decision task, we validated the efficacy of the "Image + PredReport "

workflow by comparing Clin -FFA -VLM against pipelines based on GPT -4o and Hulu -Med. To 

ensure a fair structural comparison, these baseline models were evaluated using the identical 

'report -then -diagnose' pipeline as our framework: they first gen erated a clinical report based on 

the FFA images and subsequently inferred the final diagnosis by jointly reasoning over both the 

original images and their self -generated report. We then employed distinct prompting strategies: ARTICLE IN PRESS 

ARTICLE IN PRESS 

baseline models were provided with multiple -choice options to constrain their outputs to valid 

disease (see Supplementary Materials “Close -ended Diagnosis Prompt ”), and Clin -FFA -VLM 

was evaluated using a single -turn, open -ended generation task (see Supplementary Materials 

“Open -ended D iagnosis Prompt ”). This required our model to directly generate the specific 

diagnosis name without the aid of candidate lists, treating diagnosis as a multi -class generation 

problem. 

To quantify the contribution of each modality, we implemented three spe cific ablation 

configurations: 

Image -only: The model predicted the diagnosis using only FFA images, with the report 

context removed from the input prompt. 

Report -only (PredReport -only): The model predicted diagnosis based solely on the textual 

content of the self -generated report without visual features. 

Oracle (Image + Ground -Truth Report): The model utilized images alongside the ground -

truth report provided by clinicians to establish a performance ceiling. 

Performance was quantified via Accuracy, Spe cificity, Sensitivity, and weighted F1 -score. 

Clinical validation and reader study 

To rigorously evaluate the clinical utility of Clin -FFA -VLM in a realistic setting, we conducted a 

prospective reader study utilizing the de -identified ZJU2 -Prospective co hort. The study involved 

five independent readers stratified into three experience levels: two medical students (who 

possessed foundational ophthalmologic and FFA knowledge and preliminary image 

interpretation training) , two ophthalmology residents and a s enior retina specialist . We employed 

a two -phase crossover design with a mandatory 7 -day washout period enforced between rounds 

to minimize memory recall bias. In the initial "Unaided" phase, readers independently diagnosed 

the cases over a one -week period relying solely on raw FFA sequences, with strict protocols 

prohibiting discussion to ensure unbiased interpretation. In the subsequent "AI -Assisted" phase, ARTICLE IN PRESS 

ARTICLE IN PRESS 

readers re -evaluated the same cohort —presented in a re -randomized order to prevent sequence 

memory effects —utilizing both the images and the Clin -FFA -VLM generated narrative reports 

and diagnostic suggestions. We quantified the clinical value of the framework by comparing 

diagnostic accuracy between the two phases, providing a direct measure of the mode l's ability to 

improve decision -making quality across different levels of training. 

Statistic al analysis 

Statistical analyses were conducted using Python (version 3.9) with the Scikit -learn and SciPy 

libraries. Quantitative performance for lesion detection, report generation, and diagnosis is 

reported as means with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) estimated via non -parametric 

bootstrapping with 1,000 resamples. For internal datasets, we employed the Paired t -test for 

report generation metrics, such as BLEU and CIDEr. Diagnostic accuracy was evaluated using 

McNemar’s test, while metrics including F1 -score, sensitivity, and specificity were assessed 

using the Paired Bootstrap Test. 

In the p rospective clinical validation, we evaluated differences between unaided and AI -assisted 

diagnostic accuracy using McNemar’s test with continuity correction. These analyses were 

performed on pooled predictions within the Resident and Student cohorts to acc ount for the 

paired nature of the nominal data. All statistical tests were two -sided, and a P -value of less than 

0.05 was considered statistically significant.
