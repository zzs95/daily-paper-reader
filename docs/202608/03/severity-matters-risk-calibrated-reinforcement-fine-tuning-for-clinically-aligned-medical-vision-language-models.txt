# Severity Matters: Risk-Calibrated Reinforcement Fine-Tuning for Clinically Aligned Medical Vision–Language Models 

YU SU  AND PENG WANG

ABSTRACT In clinical vision-language model (VLM) post-training, the relevant failure mode is not only how often a model is wrong, but whether errors concentrate in cases where missed findings carry greater clinical cost. Risk-Calibrated Reinforcement Fine-Tuning (RCRFT) addresses this mismatch by making severity an explicit signal in reinforcement learning rather than an after-the-fact evaluation stratum. The framework combines a severity-weighted multimodal reward, slot-aware post-rollout edits that respect clinical report structure, and a unified clinical utility spanning report quality, grounding fidelity, calibration, and safety penalties. A formal analysis decomposes the risk-weighted objective into mean utility, a global severity-scaling term, and a severity–reward covariance term; under severity centering only the mean and the covariance remain, indicating that the method favors positive association between utility and case severity rather than simple reward amplification. On MIMIC-CXR, this shift in optimization improves the safety-critical region first: relative to a matched RL baseline, severe-case false negatives fall from 9.8% to 5.6%, and high-risk utility rises from 0.65 to 0.74. The same training signal also lifts general report quality, raising ROUGE-L from 29.9 to 30.8 and RadGraph F1 from 25.8 to 27.3 while lowering RadCliQ from 0.86 to 0.72. Ablations show that each component contributes, with severity weighting accounting for the largest share of the improvement.

I. INTRODUCTION 

Radiology report generation and visual grounding are central tasks in clinical AI, with direct implications for decision support, triage, and documentation workload reduction [1]. Vision-language models (VLMs) have emerged as the domi-nant paradigm for these tasks, while reinforcement fine-tuning has become an increasingly important post-training strategy for adapting foundation VLMs to clinical deployment [2], [3], [4], [5]. By optimizing directly against task-level reward sig-nals [6] such as clinical consistency and grounding accuracy, RL-based post-training can overcome the limitations of purely imitative supervised fine-tuning and better align model outputs

At the same time, radiology VLMs have improved substantially in capability [10], [11], [12], making post-training design increasingly consequential. Even small mis-specifications in the RL objective can propagate into systematic clinical failure modes that remain hidden under aggregate evaluation. Yet current RL objectives for medical VLMs exhibit a fundamental structural limitation: they operate as average-quality surrogates that treat all cases as equally important. A missed tension pneumothorax and a mildly overstated chronic finding contribute equally to the scalar reward, even though their clinical consequences differ substantially [13]. strong aggregate performance while degrading high-risk false-negative behavior. Severity-aware reweighting is well established in supervised learning for medical imaging [14], [15], but remains underexplored in RL objectives for medical VLMs. Moreover, existing post-training frameworks treat reward design in a monolithic or fragmented manner: report quality, grounding fidelity, calibration, and safety-sensitive penalties are either collapsed into a single scalar or optimized in separate stages [16], [17], without a principled formulation that ties their relative importance to clinical risk. The motivation for risk calibration is not merely to reweight training samples, but to align the structure of the optimization objective with the asymmetric cost profile of clinical decision-making [18]. When error costs are heterogeneous, optimizing expected reward yields policies that perform well on average. Such policies may still concentrate errors on a small subset of high-risk cases. A more appropriate objective is to favor policies whose performance is positively aligned with case severity, namely those that remain reliable when clinical stakes are high. This provides a tractable surrogate for capturing heterogeneous risk beyond uniform expectation-based optimization. This perspective motivates incorporating severity directly into the RL objective as a weighting mechanism, rather than treating it as a post-hoc stratification variable. We propose Risk-Calibrated Reinforcement Fine-Tuning 

(RCRFT), a post-training framework that addresses these limitations. RCRFT consists of three coupled components: (1) a severity-weighted reward that upweights high-risk cases via a risk-sensitivity parameter γ ; (2) slot-aware post-rollout edits that revise generated reports along clinically meaningful slots (findings, impression, uncertainty clauses), improving reward signal quality while controlling policy drift; and (3) a multimodal clinical utility that integrates report quality, grounding fidelity, calibration, and safety-sensitive penalties into a unified reward. We show that the resulting objective admits a decomposition into mean utility, a severity-scaling term, and a risk–reward covariance term. Proposition 1 further establishes that, under severity centering, the objective reduces to mean utility plus the covariance between severity and reward. This formulation makes explicit that the method favors policies whose performance aligns with case severity rather than merely optimizing average reward. Our contributions are : 

> •

We formulate a risk-calibrated multimodal RL objective for medical VLMs that explicitly couples report quality, grounding fidelity, calibration, and safety-sensitive penalties through severity-aware weighting.  

> •

We derive a decomposition showing that the objective consists of mean utility, a severity-scaling term, and a risk–reward covariance term; under severity centering, it reduces to mean utility plus covariance, providing a principled interpretation of severity-aware optimization.  

> •

We extend post-rollout editing to structured clinical slots (findings, impression, uncertainty), improving reward signal quality while controlling policy drift, and establish an edit-monotonicity guarantee.  

> •

We provide a comprehensive evaluation protocol with severity-stratified reporting, grounding assessment, cali-bration analysis, and ablations that validate each design component. 

> II. RELATED WORK

Learning-based prediction has advanced rapidly across domains, from image-to-image translation and neural surro-gate modeling to physics-informed networks for scientific and engineering simulation [19], [20], [21], [22]; medical vision-language models extend this trajectory to clinical reporting, where the cost of an error is highly asymmetric. 

> A. RADIOLOGY REPORT GENERATION

Automated radiology report generation has evolved rapidly from transformer-based encoder–decoders [23], [24] toward richer formulations that inject external knowledge [25], [26], [27], exploit structural or observation-guided rea-soning [28], [29], [30], ground generation in organ- or region-aware representations [31], [32], [33], drive generation with diagnosis-aware prompts [34], leverage longitudinal context [35], or combine large language models with clinical tool use [36], [37]. Complementary efforts have emphasized task-aware inclusive frameworks [38], eye-gaze-guided cross-modal alignment [39], reinforced attention with repetition penalties [40], fine-grained image–text alignment [41], and sentence-level image–language contrastive learning [42]. Although these methods collectively improve aggregate text-similarity and clinical-consistency metrics, none incor-porates objective-level severity weighting during training; severity is treated either as a post-hoc evaluation stratification or handled indirectly through attention- or contrast-based surrogates. As a consequence, these methods exhibit the risk-dilution signature we identify in Section III: aggregate utility improves with model capacity and training maturity while Critical-stratum utility plateaus. 

> B. REINFORCEMENT FINE-TUNING FOR MEDICAL VLMs

Medical VLM post-training has progressed from supervised fine-tuning to preference optimization and reinforcement learning. Approaches such as CheXalign [7] and CheXPO [8] show that report generation benefits from task-level supervi-sion, while recent work explores direct RL for medical VLMs, including MedVLM-R1 [4], Med-R1 [5], and grounded chest X-ray foundation models trained with reinforcement learn-ing [3], as well as reasoning-oriented RL frameworks [43]. Foundation-model-based conversational VLMs [1], [10], [11] and instruction-tuned models such as LLaVA-Med [44] demonstrate strong generalization. Edit-based RL improves exploration and sampling efficiency through post-rollout corrections [9], while multimodal preference optimization emphasizes preserving visual conditioning and aligning outputs with clinical value [16], [17]. Despite these advances, most formulations remain centered on expected reward maximization [6], implicitly treating all errors as equally

costly. This assumption is limiting in medical settings where error severity is highly asymmetric, leading to risk dilution 

in optimization. Moreover, calibration is often evaluated post-hoc rather than incorporated into the training objective, creating a gap between confidence and correctness. Finally, report quality and visual grounding are frequently optimized as loosely coupled objectives, which can yield outputs that are fluent but not clinically coherent. A growing line of work applies RL directly to radi-ology report generation and medical reasoning, including MedVLM-R1 [4], Med-R1 [5], grounded chest X-ray founda-tion models trained with reinforcement learning [3], edit-based RL for report correction [9], and earlier policy-gradient systems that optimize clinically accurate generation [45] or semantic factual-correctness rewards [46]. Across these systems severity, when present at all, enters as a reward component or a data stratifier rather than as an explicit weight on the policy gradient. The closest concurrent effort is RadHiera [47], an unpublished preprint that introduces a severity-aware impression reward inside a hierarchical RL pipeline, yet even there severity scales a reward term rather than tilting the objective through a severity–reward covariance. RCRFT instead treats clinical severity as an explicit weight in the RL objective itself, with a formal severity–reward covariance decomposition (Proposition 1) characterizing when and why high-severity gains arise. 

> C. RISK-SENSITIVE REINFORCEMENT LEARNING AND CALIBRATION

Risk-sensitive RL has a long history in robotics [18], autonomous driving, and safety-critical control, where the objective extends beyond expected returns to account for their distribution. Methods such as conditional value-at-risk (CVaR) optimization and distributional RL [48], [49] explicitly model tail risks, while constrained RL enforces safety constraints alongside reward maximization. In parallel, the LLM post-training literature explores selective prediction and abstention [50] to improve reliability, and calibration-aware training [51] shows that incorporating uncertainty into the objective can improve confidence-quality alignment. These ideas are particularly relevant in medical settings, where decision costs are inherently risk-asymmetric . However, they remain only partially explored in multimodal medical RL. Existing radiology RL systems typically evaluate calibration after training and treat severity awareness as a dataset-level stratification rather than an objective-level design principle. As a result, optimiza-tion may remain insensitive to clinically meaningful risk structure. 

> D. POSITIONING

Across these streams, severity enters as a dataset label or an evaluation stratum, and clinical or uncertainty-aware structure is targeted at the loss-function or inference level [33], [36], [39], [42]. RCRFT differs in where clinical risk enters the optimization. Its contribution is the specific integration of objective-level severity weighting with multimodal clinical utility and slot-local edits for radiology VLM post-training, so that the training objective itself favors policies whose gains concentrate on clinically important cases. This makes the severity–reward covariance an explicit and analyzable optimization target rather than a heuristic multi-term loss. The framework further couples this objective with slot-aware clinical edits and a composite reward that jointly targets report fidelity, grounding, calibration, and safety. We do not claim a fundamentally new optimization principle. The severity-weighted objective is an instance of cost-sensitive and risk-sensitive expected-utility weighting [52], [53], and its decomposition is elementary; the contribution is its instantiation at the policy-gradient level for multimodal clinical utility together with slot-local edits, which separates it from distributional or CVaR risk-sensitive RL [48], [49] and from cost-sensitive learning or medical severity weighting applied at the loss or data level. RCRFT is also distinct from the line of medical VLMs based on preference optimization and task-level supervision, such as CheXalign [7], CheXPO [8], MDPO [16], and MMedPO [17]. Those methods encode clinical risk through preference pairs, hard-example mining, or per-token weighting on a likelihood or preference loss, so risk influences which samples are preferred rather than how the expected return is scaled. RCRFT instead keeps the sampling policy and preference structure intact and acts through a severity-tilted reward, so that clinical risk enters as an analyzable covariance term in the return rather than as an implicit property of the preference data. The resulting framework unifies risk-sensitive utility, multimodal conditioning, and calibration-aware decision-making within a single optimization formulation, supported by formal analysis (Section III-E ) and extensive empirical validation.

> 1) WHY SEVERITY BELONGS AT THE OBJECTIVE LEVEL

The decomposition in Eq. 4 clarifies why severity is placed inside the policy-gradient objective rather than at the loss or inference level. Because the per-sample reward is (1 +

γ s(x)) R(x, y), the resulting advantage is severity-tilted, so a high-severity case with above-average utility produces a gradient correction scaled by 1 + γ s(x) rather than by 1; the update therefore moves the policy more for the cases the covariance term γ Cov (s, R) rewards, which an inference threshold cannot reproduce. An inference-time threshold reranks or abstains over outputs sampled from an already frozen policy; it can shift the evaluation operating point but leaves the trained policy, and hence E[R] and Cov (s, R) in Eq. 4, unchanged, so it cannot retrain the model to concentrate quality on high-severity cases. Loss-level reweighting on the supervised objective is closer in spirit but mixes severity

> D. SLOT-AWARE POST-ROLLOUT EDITS

Post-rollout editing augments the RL loop by applying targeted corrections to sampled responses prior to reward evaluation and policy updates. Unlike sentence-level editing in generic RL [9], we restrict edits to clinically defined slots, preserving diagnostic structure while improving reward fidelity. 

> 1) CLINICAL SLOTS

Radiology reports follow a structured format. We define the slot set Sslot = { findings , impression , uncertainty clauses },where each slot corresponds to a semantically coher-ent unit. Findings describe localized abnormalities; the impression summarizes the clinical conclusion; uncertainty clauses encode hedging (e.g., ‘‘cannot exclude’’, ‘‘recom-mend follow-up’’). Restricting edits to these slots aligns post-hoc corrections with clinically meaningful report components. 

> 2) EDIT OPERATOR AND ROLLOUT

Let y ∼ πθ (· | x) denote a sampled response. The edit operator produces ˜y = Edit (y; x) by modifying only slot-identified segments, leaving all other tokens unchanged. The editor is fixed and rule-based: for each slot it applies a deterministic correction derived from CheXpert-extracted labels and reference confidences, insert-ing missing findings, repairing contradictory negations, replacing impression sentences on high-severity cases when key labels are missing or fabricated, and prepend-ing hedging phrases to overconfident assertions on low-confidence references. The editor never jointly trains with the policy.

> E. THEORETICAL PROPERTIES
> 1) ASSUMPTIONS

Three assumptions underpin the results below. They are intentionally modest: their purpose is to preserve the interpretation of the objective, not to guarantee global optimality. 

Assumption 1 (Exogenous Severity): The severity score 

s(x) depends only on the case x and not on the sampled response y.This excludes formulations where severity is inferred from the model response itself, which would entangle optimization with self-referential labeling and weaken the covariance interpretation. 

Assumption 2 (Bounded Reward): After clipping and optional affine normalization, the scalar clinical utility satisfies 0 ≤ R(x, y) ≤ M for some finite constant M > 0 and for all admissible pairs ( x, y). This keeps any one reward channel from dominating the group-relative advantage and is required for the concentration bound in Theorem 2. 

Assumption 3 (Local Edit): The edit operator is local in the sense that each edited response ˜y is produced from the sampled response y by modifying only a bounded subset of clinically defined slots. If edits rewrote the entire output, the edit-improvement interpretation would become closer to imitation than to guided policy improvement. Slot-aware edits keep the perturbation localized, preserving more of the sampled policy behavior while still allowing clinically meaningful corrections to enter the update. The following results make explicit what the proposed objective guarantees at the level of ranking, centered normalization, distributional interpretation, and finite-sample estimation. None constitutes a convergence theorem for policy optimization; rather, they characterize what the objective prefers once Assumptions 1–3 are in force.

> F. TRAINING ALGORITHM

RCRFT is compatible with existing group-based RL meth-ods [65], [66]: the optimizer, rollout process, and reference policy remain unchanged; only the reward supplied to the update is replaced by the risk-calibrated utility Eq. 1 augmented by the edit-improvement term. This makes ablation comparisons against vanilla RL clean, since the only variable is the objective. The full procedure is given in Algorithm 1; the dominant runtime cost remains rollout generation and reward evaluation, with severity lookup and structured editing adding overhead that is linear in group size and does not alter the asymptotic training loop.

> C. FRAMEWORK ANALYSIS
> 1) RISK-SENSITIVE ANALYSIS
> a: SEVERITY-STRATIFIED GAINS

Tables 8 and 9 establish the principal risk-sensitive findings. While the overall utility gain is moderate (0 .76 → 0.78), the distribution of gains across severity strata is the defining result: RCRFT improves Low-stratum utility by only +0.01 (from 0 .78 to 0 .79), High-stratum utility by +0.09 (from 0.65 to 0 .74), and Critical-stratum utility by +0.21 (from 0.53 to 0 .74), so that the gain at the most clinically consequential end of the spectrum is more than twenty times the gain on routine cases. Examining the SFT-to-vanilla-RL transition reveals that standard RL improves over SFT at all strata by a near-uniform +0.05– +0.06, precisely the pattern expected when the covariance term Cov (s, R) is zero (γ = 0). RCRFT specifically amplifies gains where the severity-reward covariance is largest, consistent with the theoretical decomposition in Section III showing that Jrisk 

rewards policies that align performance gains with clinical importance. Calibration also improves structurally: ECE drops from 0 .072 to 0.041 (a 43% relative reduction) and abstention quality rises from 0 .68 to 0.79 , indicating that RCRFT simultaneously achieves better high-severity sensitivity and better-calibrated uncertainty estimates.

> V. THEORETICAL ANALYSIS

This section provides extended discussion and complete proofs for the theoretical results established in Section III-E .These results are presented in the main text to ensure self-containedness. 

> A. ROLE OF THE CENTERED OBJECTIVE

Lemma 1 decomposes the risk-calibrated objective into a mean-utility component and a severity–reward covariance component by recentering severity scores at their population mean. Under this centering, Jc(πθ ) = E[R] + γ Cov (s, R), which makes transparent that the objective simultaneously optimizes aggregate expected utility and the alignment of utility gains with clinical importance. These two components are orthogonal: a policy can achieve high mean utility while exhibiting low covariance (uniform improvement across strata) or high covariance (disproportionate improvement on high-severity cases). The decomposition motivates the severity-stratified evaluation protocol: an improvement in mean utility that is uniformly distributed across strata would produce 1 Cov (s, R) ≈ 0, whereas the empirically observed twentyfold gap in stratum-specific gains implies a substantial positive covariance contribution from RCRFT. 

> B. POLICY RANKING INTERPRETATION

Theorem 1 characterizes the policy preference induced by the centered objective as a comparative-statics result under a mean-utility constraint. When two policies are matched in mean expected reward, their objective values differ solely through the severity–reward covariance: the objective strictly prefers the policy whose utility gains are better concentrated on high-severity inputs. This ranking is a property of the objective, not an optimization guarantee; it holds for any two policies regardless of how they were obtained. Operationally, the theorem implies that among all policies achieving a given aggregate performance level, the risk-calibrated objective selects the one with the highest severity alignment, providing a principled selection criterion that extends beyond aggregate benchmarks. 

> C. FINITE-SAMPLE ESTIMATION BOUND

Theorem 2 establishes a Hoeffding-type concentration bound [64] for the empirical risk objective under independent sampling. The (1 + γ )2 factor reflects an inherent trade-off in importance-weighted estimation: increasing the risk weight 

γ strengthens the objective’s preference for severity-aligned performance but simultaneously widens the range of the weighted sample Zb = (1 + γ sb)Rb, thereby weakening the finite-sample concentration guarantee. This inflation is a characteristic of any estimator that upweights a subset of examples, not a limitation specific to this objective. The bound supports the experimental practice of reporting severity-stratified metrics as means across multiple random seeds with confidence intervals, particularly for high-severity strata where the effective sample size is smaller. 

> D. SEVERITY-TILTED DISTRIBUTION INTERPRETATION

Proposition 2 establishes an exact change-of-measure equiva-lence [53] between the risk-calibrated objective and ordinary expected reward under a severity-tilted distribution Dγ .Specifically, Jrisk (πθ ) = Zγ Ex∼Dγ [R(x, y)], where Zγ =

E[1 + γ s(x)] is a normalizing constant. This equivalence has two implications. First, the proposed objective is not a heuristic reweighting of the reward signal but corresponds precisely to optimizing expected utility under a distribution that assigns higher probability mass to high-severity cases. Second, the result identifies a principled implementation alternative: approximate oversampling of high-severity inputs with batch normalization compensation is mathematically related to the reward-weighting formulation, with discrepancies arising only from discretization and finite-batch normalization.

F. EDIT MONOTONICITY AND SCOPE 

Proposition 3 and Corollary 1 establish sufficient conditions under which the edit-augmented objective Jedit strictly exceeds the risk objective Jrisk . The central quantity is the net edit gain G(x, y) = R(x, ˜y) − R(x, y) − η d(˜y, y), which is the reward improvement from the edited response minus the drift penalty. The Proposition shows that Jedit ≥ Jrisk + ρ (τ −

ε) Pr (Sτ ) when edits are applied only to the set Sτ where the estimated gain exceeds threshold τ > ε . Restricting edit scope to a single clinical finding token span satisfies this condition more reliably than sentence-level rewriting: narrow scope reduces the drift penalty d(˜y, y) while preserving reward improvement on the targeted span, thereby keeping G(x, y)positive on a larger fraction of rollouts. These results formalize the design constraint that edit scope should be as local as is consistent with the clinical correction objective, and provide the theoretical basis for treating edit granularity as a design parameter subject to ablation.

> VI. DISCUSSION

The results demonstrate that incorporating explicit clinical risk into the post-training objective yields a qualitatively different trade-off profile than standard RL: aggregate quality is preserved or improved while the largest gains concentrate on high-severity cases (utility: 0 .65 → 0.74; FN rate: 9 .8% →

5.6%), consistent with the decomposition in Section III where the policy optimizes alignment between performance and clinical importance rather than mean performance alone. Leave-one-out ablations confirm that gains are distributed across all components rather than concentrated in a single design choice, with severity weighting contributing the most when removed (0 .77 → 0.69), followed by slot-aware edits ( −0.06), grounding ( −0.04), and calibration ( −0.03). Several caveats constrain these claims. The severity scorer is rule-based and derived from CheXpert labels, so gains may not transfer if it does not reflect genuine downstream cost, and the framework is validated only on chest X-ray report generation and grounding, leaving generalization to more interactive or multimodal clinical settings open. Metric entanglement is bounded rather than eliminated, since the severity score s(x), the clinical-consistency reward, and the

default evaluation strata share a common CheXpert/CheXbert lineage and could therefore inflate apparent improvements. Two factors limit this risk. First, the grounding reward already draws on a separate bounding-box annotation source. Second, our robustness experiment re-derives the evaluation strata under CheXbert, NegBio, and a radiologist-annotated subset, and the safety-critical gains persist even under the NegBio and radiologist labels that do not share the CheXpert lineage, so they are not solely an artifact of the CheXpert stratifier shared by training and the default evaluation. The residual coupling runs along the CheXpert axis shared by the severity scorer and the default strata, and the radiologist-annotated re-derivation provides the label-disjoint check that bears most directly on it. Nevertheless, the results show that trust-related properties such as calibration-sensitive conservatism [75] and severity-aware emphasis can be partially addressed at the objective level rather than deferred entirely to post-hoc auditing; the modular design also means that a learned severity scorer derived from clinician preference data or escalation policies can be substituted without structural change, and future work may further combine risk-calibrated objectives with selective prediction mechanisms [50] to enable more principled abstention on high-uncertainty cases. 

VII. CONCLUSION 

This paper proposes RCRFT, a post-training framework for medical vision-language models that integrates severity-weighted rewards, slot-aware post-rollout edits, and calibration-sensitive safety penalties. Theoretically, we show that the objective decomposes into mean utility, a severity-scaling term, and a risk–reward covariance term; under severity centering, it reduces to mean utility plus covariance, providing an analyzable alternative to average-reward optimization. Empirically, RCRFT consistently improves both aggregate performance and risk-sensitive behavior over matched RL baselines. It enhances report generation and visual grounding quality while substantially improving high-risk performance, increasing high-risk utility and reducing severe-case false-negative rates. Ablation studies further confirm that all components contribute independently. These results suggest that medical multimodal post-training should be framed in terms of clinically meaningful utility rather than benchmark averages, with implications for reward design, objective formulation, and evaluation protocols. Future work will extend this framework to broader clinical settings, including multimodal reasoning, structured reporting, and human-in-the-loop severity annotation.
