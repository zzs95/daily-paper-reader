Abstract

reports rather than pixel-level annotations. However, many clinically impor-tant analyses require spatially localized evidence. This mismatch limits the 

reuse of large-scale image–report archives for spatially resolved quantitative 

analysis, as heterogeneous clinical findings c annot b e d irectly t ranslated into

## Introduction 

In routine clinical practice, radiologists do not produce pixel-level spatial annotations 

as part of the standard workflow. Instead, they document abnormalities in the findings 

section of radiology reports using natural language [1]. As a result, hospitals routinely 

accumulate large-scale image–report pairs, whereas high-quality spatial annotations 

remain scarce [2]. This mismatch is consequential because many clinically important 

retrospective analyses require more than image-level recognition: they require spatially 

resolved evidence of where an abnormality is located and how extensive it is [3]. 

Without such localization, tasks such as abnormality burden estimation and burden-based case stratification r emain d ifficult to sup port at sca le [4]. Recent studies have explored language-guided segmentation as a way to infer 

abnormality regions from clinical language and thus reduce reliance on manual pixel-level annotations [5–10]. However, most existing methods still rely on simplified text 

inputs, typically in the form of disease labels or short location phrases, rather than the 

full findings narratives encountered in routine clinical practice [11–14]. Although such 

settings facilitate stable training and controlled comparison, they also reduce the prob-lem to a much cleaner and more structured form than real radiology documentation. 

As a result, strong performance under simplified t ext c onditions d oes n ot necessar-ily indicate that these methods can support report-based spatial analysis in clinical 

archives. For such methods to be useful in retrospective cohort studies, they must 

confront the way spatial information is naturally documented in routine reporting. In full radiology findings, l ocalization c ues a re r ich b ut i nherently unstructured 

[15]. Radiologists typically anchor abnormalities using relational anatomy, morpho-logical modifiers, and contextual narrative, rather than explicit geometric boundaries 

[16, 17]. Moreover, reporting styles vary widely between institutions and individual 

radiologists, meaning the exact same spatial abnormality is often described in differ-ent ways [18, 19]. The central challenge is therefore not the availability of supervision, 

but the absence of a reliable mechanism for transforming heterogeneous narrative 

descriptions into consistent spatial grounding signals at the pixel level. Without such 

a mechanism, even large-scale image–report pairs cannot be translated into spatially 

grounded annotations [20]. To make routine image–report data actionable for spatial annotation, we develop 

Clinical-Findings-Guided Segmentation (CF2Seg), a report-guided framework that ARTICLE IN PRESS

learns from full clinical findings i n c hest X -rays. We s upport t his s tudy w ith a

multi-source benchmark of more than fifty thousand e xaminations, each with expert-drawn spatial annotations and a corresponding radiology report across diverse thoracic

CF2Seg preserves the information contained in full findings narratives. Specifically, we

we assess whether CF2Seg-derived masks preserve downstream quantitative infor-mation by measuring their agreement with expert-derived proportional lesion extent 

estimates. Overall, these analyses position CF2Seg not only as a stronger report-guided 

segmentation framework, but also as a step toward making routine image–report 

archives more useful for spatial annotation and retrospective quantitative reuse.

Few-Shot Learning Capability 

In real-world clinical deployment, segmentation models are often required to adapt to 

new centers or emerging cohorts with severely limited pixel-level annotations, as expert 

radiologist labeling is both labor-intensive and costly. To evaluate robustness under 

such data-scarce conditions, we simulate this scenario by restricting the training data 

to 10% of the original mixed chest X-ray corpus, while keeping all test cohorts fixed. All 

language-guided medical segmentation models (LViT-T, RecLMIS, LanGuideMedSeg, 

TeViA, FMISeg, and our method) are retrained on this 10% split using the same 

protocol as in the full-data setting. For each thoracic disease, we quantify robustness 

by computing a performance decrease rate, defined a s t he r elative d rop i n D ice when 

training with 10% data compared to 100% data, and visualize the per-class decline 

across models in Fig. 5. Reducing the labeled training set to 10% degrades all methods, but the decline 

is smaller for our model overall. On common NIH findings s uch a s a telectasis, car-diomegaly, consolidation, effusion, and fibrosis, competing methods cover a broad Dice 

range of 0.136–0.811 under limited supervision, whereas our model remains within a

narrower and higher range of 0.540–0.851. The advantage becomes more pronounced 

for clinically challenging lesions. For calcification, fracture, nodule, pleural thickening, 

and pneumothorax, including the PTX-498 and SIIM-ACR cohorts, baseline methods 

fall to Dice scores between 0.003 and 0.725 with only 10% training data, while our 

model maintains a higher range of 0.187–0.787. These results indicate that the pro-posed design not only improves full-data performance but also safeguards diagnostic 

utility even when the available labeled data are reduced to as little as 10% of the 

original training set, a scenario that closely matches real-world deployment at new 

institutions with scarce pixel-level annotations. 

Robustness to image and text perturbations

curated benchmarks. In real deployment, the difficulty of report-guided annotation is 

amplified by c ross-institution variation: h ospitals d iffer no t on ly in im age acquisition 

and reconstruction conditions, but also in reporting style, completeness, and termi-nology. As a result, models trained with limited labeled data must often operate on 

imperfect inputs whose distribution deviates from the training environment. To mimic

design two families of test-time perturbations on the mixed chest X-ray benchmark

levels (Fig. 6). For image perturbations, we corrupt the test images with three standard operators

Ablation study

A, B, and C in Tables 1 and 2. We compare the full model with four ablated vari-ants: w/o A&B&C, w/o A, w/o B, and w/o C. Quantitatively, the full model achieves

## Discussion 

A central obstacle in clinical image segmentation is not only that expert-drawn 

masks are costly and scarce, but also that this scarcity is amplified b y heterogeneity 

across institutions. In routine practice, hospitals accumulate large volumes of image– report pairs, whereas high-quality pixel-level annotations remain limited and unevenly 

distributed across sites, diseases, and reporting styles. This imbalance is especially con-sequential for retrospective quantitative analysis, where clinically useful conclusions 

often depend on spatially resolved evidence of lesion extent rather than image-level 

recognition alone. In chest radiography, the problem is further complicated by cross-institution variation in acquisition conditions, report completeness, and descriptive 

terminology, so that the same abnormality may be documented differently and appear 

under different visual distributions. CF2Seg is motivated by this practical setting: not 

by replacing radiologists with report-driven segmentation at the point of care, but by 

making routine image–report archives more reusable for spatial annotation, burden 

estimation, and retrospective cohort analysis under realistic clinical variability. This clinical objective also changes the standard by which the method should be 

judged. If the aim is to make routine image–report archives reusable in practice, per-formance on a fixed b enchmark i s n ecessary b ut n ot s ufficient. The mod el mus t also 

remain usable when local mask supervision is scarce, when target abnormalities are 

rare or unseen during training, and when image quality or report wording deviates 

from the source distribution. For this reason, we evaluate CF2Seg not only in the fully 

supervised setting, but also under low-label adaptation, leave-one-disease-out general-ization, and realistic perturbations in both imaging and reporting. These experiments 

are designed to test whether the generated masks remain stable enough to support 

downstream quantitative use, including burden estimation and burden-based case 

grouping, under the same kinds of variability that characterize real clinical archives. Our surrogate analysis of lesion burden was designed to evaluate whether CF2Seg-derived masks preserve information relevant to downstream quantitative reuse. Diffuse 

findings such as pleural effusion and atelectasis are challenging on 2D projection imag-ing because their boundaries are often gradual or partially obscured by overlapping 

anatomy. This ambiguity may explain the mild proportional deviations observed in 

larger-burden cases and indicates that individual case-level estimates should still be 

interpreted with expert oversight. Nevertheless, the observed correlation, concordance, 

and Bland–Altman results suggest that CF2Seg-derived masks retain broad propor-tional trends in lesion extent. These findings p rovide p reliminary e vidence t hat such 

masks may support retrospective cohort-level analyses, where the goal is to summa-rize burden patterns across groups rather than to assign stand-alone severity scores to 

individual patients. Further validation on larger and more diverse datasets, along with 

formal assessment of inter-reader variability, will be important for better characterizing 

the robustness and generalizability of these findings. Lesion-level trends and ablations together suggest that the gain of CF2Seg does 

not arise from a larger backbone, but from changing how clinical findings constrain

## Methods 

Data curation and construction 

To support model training and evaluation, we systematically collected and inte-grated all publicly available chest X-ray image–mask datasets that we were able 

to identify, resulting in a multi-source resource of 53,386 de-identified i mages with 

expert-annotated masks. We organize this resource into two complementary views, 

lesion-centric and disease-centric, to capture both fine-grained l ocal abnormalities 

and broader disease-level radiological semantics. The lesion-centric view focuses on 

pixel-level delineation of focal abnormalities and covers 16 lesion targets. Its major 

sources include NIH Chest X-rays (ChexDet, 6,337 image–mask pairs) [33, 34], QaTa 

for COVID-19 infection segmentation (9,258 images) [35], SIIM-ACR Pneumotho-rax (2,669 images) [36], and the multi-center PTX-498 cohort (498 images) [37]. 

The disease-centric view captures disease-level radiological semantics with relatively 

coarser spatial supervision. It is mainly supported by COVID-QU-Ex (33,920 images) [38], spanning COVID-19, non-COVID pneumonia, and normal lungs, and is further ARTICLE IN PRESS 

## ARTICLE IN PRESS 

complemented by tuberculosis-related cohorts, including Shenzhen and MC [39], to 

cover chronic and diffuse abnormalities that are difficult to define with discrete lesion 

boundaries. In addition to image–mask pairs, our framework requires a textual finding descrip-tion for each annotated sample. Because publicly available chest X-ray segmentation 

datasets usually do not provide report findings paired with the pixel-level annotations, 

we construct such text supervision through a retrieval-guided generation pipeline. 

Specifically, w e e xtract a nd o rganize m ore t han 4 00,000 r adiologist-authored finding 

sentences from multi-institutional radiology reports to build a finding s entence mem-ory. Given an input chest X-ray, we use a pretrained CXR-CLIP [40] model to compare 

the image against all candidate sentences in the memory and retrieve the five sentences 

with the highest image–text similarity as supporting evidence. This retrieval step provides the large language model (LLM) [41] with stylis-tic prompts drawn from real radiology reports. Without such retrieved evidence, 

prompting the LLM using image-related information alone may lead to hallucinated 

or clinically implausible descriptions. At the same time, we do not use the retrieved 

sentences directly as supervision. Although they provide authentic radiology expres-sions, the retrieved sentences may not fully match the lesion appearance, anatomical 

location, or extent of abnormality in the current image. We therefore provide the 

retrieved evidence, together with image-level information and the dataset-provided 

target abnormality category, to an LLM to generate a single case-specific finding that is 

more coherent, concise, and better aligned with the abnormality to be segmented. The 

resulting generated finding, r ather t han t he o riginal r eport t ext o r t he r etrieved sen-tences themselves, is used as the textual supervision in the subsequent report-guided 

segmentation framework. After candidate findings a re g enerated, w e f urther a pply a n a utomated multi-stage quality-control procedure before including them in the training set. Specifically, 

we use a separate LLM [42], distinct from the generator, as a structured judge to 

assess each candidate finding f rom f our a spects: l ocalization, n amely w hether the 

described position matches the annotated region; morphology, namely whether the 

textual description is consistent with the visual appearance of the abnormality; con-sistency, namely whether the finding a grees w ith t he i mage c ontent, t he abnormality 

label, and the retrieved evidence sentences; and clarity, namely whether the text is 

concise, readable, and suitable for downstream conditioning. The judge is prompted 

to score each aspect independently and return an overall quality score. Importantly, this judge is used as a filtering m echanism r ather t han a s t he final 

source of supervision. When the overall score is below 0.8, the candidate finding is

Bimodal Aligner for Frequency-Aware Early Fusion. 

Most medical language-guided segmentation frameworks still adopt late fusion: the vision encoder commits high-resolution features from image statistics alone, and text enters only at the decoder as a coarse gate, so real findings sentences rarely shape feature formation [49]. In chest radiographs, however, subtle abnormalities are often weak in contrast and can be suppressed once the encoder has already committed its hierarchical representation. The core challenge is therefore to inject report information early enough to influence feature formation, while preserving the low-level structures that are critical for lesion boundary delineation. To address this issue, we adopt encoder-side early fusion by inserting lightweight Bimodal Aligner (BA) blocks into selected ViT layers so that report-calibrated tokens can influence visual representations throughout the depth while keeping the backbone frozen [50, 51]. Each BA block couples frequency-aware visual refinement with cross-modal grounding, allowing the encoder to become simultaneously report-aware and structurally robust under low contrast, projection overlap, and cross-institution vari-ation. In a ViT-based backbone such as RAD-DINO [17], different transformer blocks encode different levels of semantic abstraction, forming a hierarchical representation of the radiograph. We therefore insert BA blocks into a small set of representative transformer layers (early, middle, and late), turning them into cross-modal interaction points where visual tokens are refined under text-aware constraints while all backbone parameters remain frozen.
