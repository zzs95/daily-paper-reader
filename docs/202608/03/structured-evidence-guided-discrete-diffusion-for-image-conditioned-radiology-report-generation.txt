Abstract 1

Radiology report generation (RRG) converts chest X-rays into structured findings and impressions, 2

but autoregressive systems can propagate early entity, negation, or anatomical errors, and language-3

model-based systems may rely on language priors when visual evidence is weak. We propose SEDRRG, 4

structured evidence-guided discrete diffusion for radiology report generation, an image-conditioned frame-5

work that refines the whole report sequence through discrete token denoising. SEDRRG combines Swin-6

V2 hierarchical global and patch-level evidence with denoising-time global-local gating, and uses multi-7

factor structured supervision to align token recovery with report-derived medical token/phrase cues, 8

image-text alignment, and section organization. Experiments on IU X-Ray and MIMIC-CXR show com-9

petitive benchmark-level performance against representative autoregressive, knowledge-enhanced, expert-10 

token-based, and language-model-assisted baselines under reproduced and literature-reported comparison 11 

settings. A complementary CheXbert-based 14-observation clinical-efficacy evaluation on MIMIC-CXR 12 

yields precision of 0.322, recall of 0.375, and F1 of 0.346. Ablation and controlled analyses support the 13 

contributions of hierarchical evidence encoding, gated conditioning, and structured supervision. These 14 

results position SEDRRG as a benchmark-oriented algorithmic framework, not as evidence of clinical 15 

deployment readiness. 16

## 1 Introduction 19 

Radiology report generation (RRG) aims to automatically produce diagnostic reports from medical images 20 

and has become an important task in medical vision–language learning. Unlike natural image captions, 21 

radiology reports are long, section-aware documents that communicate diagnostic observations, uncertainty, 22 

negation, anatomical localization, and clinically relevant impressions. A useful RRG system must therefore be 23 

linguistically coherent, conditioned on radiographic evidence, and consistent with clinical reporting structure. 24 

Recent Scientific Reports studies on medical image captioning and automated report generation have further 25 

shown the relevance of generative pretrained language models and multimodal Transformer encoder–decoder 26 

architectures for chest X-ray reporting tasks [1, 2]. 27 

Existing RRG methods face a persistent tension between global report coherence and localized visual 28 

evidence conditioning. Full-image encoder–decoder models capture image-level context but can miss subtle 29 

or spatially constrained findings, while region- or entity-aware systems improve localized description but 30 

## ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT ARTICLE IN PRESS ARTICLE IN PRESS may still suffer from inconsistent narratives. In addition, most conventional systems generate reports au-31 

toregressively from left to right. Once an early entity, negation, or anatomical location is decoded incorrectly, 32 

subsequent tokens may inherit and amplify the error. 33 

Large language models and medical vision–language models have improved fluency and enabled more 34 

interactive radiology reporting. However, stronger language priors do not by themselves guarantee faithful 35 

image-conditioned reporting, especially when visual evidence is injected only once, represented coarsely, or 36 

converted into a fixed abnormality prompt before decoding. Radiology reporting instead requires persistent 37 

access to visual evidence throughout generation, so that report-level organization and localized finding de-38 

scriptions can be refined jointly. These limitations motivate a generation mechanism that can repeatedly 39 

revisit the whole report while preserving both global anatomical context and localized visual cues. To ad-40 

dress this need, we design an image-conditioned discrete denoising framework in which hierarchical visual 41 

evidence is injected throughout iterative report refinement rather than only at the start of decoding. 42 

We propose SEDRRG, which denotes structured evidence-guided discrete diffusion for radiology report 43 

generation , as an image-conditioned framework for chest X-ray report generation. Rather than decoding 44 

strictly left to right, SEDRRG treats reporting as a full-sequence discrete token denoising process. A Swin 45 

Transformer V2-based visual encoder extracts both a global anatomical feature and patch-level localized vi-46 

sual evidence. A denoising-time gated global–local conditioning module adaptively injects these two evidence 47 

streams into a Transformer denoiser at each reverse diffusion step. A multi-factor structured supervision 48 

objective further regularizes token recovery with report-derived medical token/phrase cues, image–text align-49 

ment, and section-organization signals. This design directly addresses the limitations described above by 50 

keeping image evidence available throughout iterative report refinement rather than relying on a single static 51 

visual prefix or a one-pass autoregressive commitment. 52 

The scope of this study is algorithmic rather than deployment-oriented. Following benchmark-focused 53 

RRG studies that evaluate generation mechanisms through public datasets, automatic report-generation 54 

metrics, ablations, and qualitative analysis, we ask whether image-conditioned discrete denoising can im-55 

prove standard benchmark report generation when guided by hierarchical visual evidence and structured 56 

supervision. We therefore distinguish benchmark-level generation performance from clinical factual correct-57 

ness throughout the manuscript, and do not interpret overlap-based metrics or single-case visualizations as 58 

evidence of diagnostic reliability or clinical readiness. 59 

The main contributions of this study are as follows: 60 

• We formulate radiology report generation as image-conditioned discrete token diffusion, enabling full-61 

sequence iterative refinement instead of strictly autoregressive left-to-right decoding. 62 

• We introduce hierarchical visual evidence encoding and denoising-time gated global–local conditioning, 63 

allowing global anatomical context and patch-level evidence to guide report refinement throughout 64 

generation. 65 

• We design a multi-factor structured supervision objective that uses report-derived medical token/phrase 66 

cues, image–text contrastive alignment, and section-aware organization signals to regularize diffusion-67 

based report recovery. 68 

• We evaluate SEDRRG on IU X-Ray and MIMIC-CXR using benchmark comparisons, controlled abla-69 

tions, visual-granularity analysis, diffusion-strategy analysis, qualitative examples, inference-cost pro-70 

filing, and a complementary MIMIC-CXR clinical efficacy evaluation based on CheXbert-derived 14-71 

observation labels, while explicitly limiting the claims to algorithmic benchmark performance rather 72 

than clinical deployment validation. 73

## 2 Related work 86 

2.1 Radiology report generation 87 

Radiology report generation has evolved from generic image captioning toward structured medical vision– 88 

language generation. Early neural systems used encoder–decoder or hierarchical recurrent models to produce 89 

long reports from visual features, while co-attention mechanisms associated textual descriptions with image 90 

regions [3]. Transformer-based approaches further improved long-range dependency modeling. R2Gen intro-91 

## ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT ARTICLE IN PRESS ARTICLE IN PRESS duced a memory-driven Transformer for recurrent reporting patterns [4], and R2GenCMN used cross-modal 92 

memory networks to strengthen image–text correspondence [5]. Recent work in Scientific Reports has also 93 

explored GPT-based medical image caption completion and multimodal Transformer report-generation ar-94 

chitectures that connect vision Transformer encoders with GPT-style decoders [1, 2]. Subsequent algorithm-95 

oriented RRG studies have treated report generation as a problem of data-deviation mitigation, knowledge 96 

distillation, and multi-level cross-modal alignment. For example, PPKED explores posterior abnormal-97 

region information and prior medical/report knowledge to reduce visual and textual data bias, whereas 98 

UAR formulates RRG as global and local cross-modal semantic alignment through discrete token unifi-99 

cation, representation alignment, and text-to-image refinement [6, 7]. Other methods explored semantic 100 

queries, class-activation-map attention, contrastive attention, retrieval augmentation, and learnable expert 101 

tokens to improve visual evidence conditioning and report specificity [8–12]. These approaches have advanced 102 

benchmark RRG, but most still rely on autoregressive left-to-right decoding and often inject image features 103 

through static or weakly structured conditions. In contrast, SEDRRG maintains hierarchical visual evidence 104 

throughout full-sequence discrete denoising. 105 

2.2 Medical vision–language and LLM-based reporting 106 

Recent large language models and medical vision–language models have shifted RRG toward more fluent 107 

and interactive systems. BootstrappingLLM adapts LLMs using in-domain instance induction and coarse-108 

to-fine decoding [13], while retrieval-augmented and prompt-guided approaches use similar cases or clinical 109 

instructions to guide report generation [11, 14]. R2GenGPT studies the modality gap between visual features 110 

and frozen LLM word embeddings through shallow, deep, and parameter-efficient delta alignment [15]. 111 

Concept- and abnormality-grounded methods further align visual representations with diagnostic criteria or 112 

disease-specific prompts. RadAlign aligns images with medical diagnostic concepts [16]; KARGEN integrates 113 

disease knowledge with regional features in a frozen LLM [17]; MAIRA-2 studies grounded chest X-ray report 114 

generation with localized findings [18]; META-CXR injects abnormality-aware prompts into an LLM-based 115 

generator [19]; and MFDP integrates multi-view features with enhanced disease prompting on IU X-Ray 116 

and MIMIC-CXR [20]. Recent lesion-aware Scientific Reports work has further combined lesion-guided 117 

attention, contrastive learning, and LLM-based report aggregation to bridge global image reasoning with 118 

region-level abnormality description [21]. These studies highlight the importance of explicit grounding and 119 

visual-language alignment, but strong language priors may still produce fluent statements that are weakly 120 

supported by image evidence. SEDRRG is complementary to this line of work because it studies a non-121 

LLM generation mechanism in which global and patch-level image evidence is repeatedly injected into each 122 

denoising step. 123 

2.3 Diffusion-based text and report generation 124 

Diffusion models generate data by gradual corruption followed by iterative denoising [22, 23]. Discrete 125 

diffusion extends this principle to categorical variables [24], and diffusion-based language models such as 126 

Diffusion-LM and DiffuSeq show that iterative denoising can support controllable or conditional text gen-127 

eration [25, 26]. Recent diffusion-related RRG studies have explored discriminator-assisted diffusion, semi-128 

autoregressive diffusion-style decoding, knowledge-graph-guided diffusion, multi-stage discrete diffusion, and 129 

LLM-assisted diversity-augmented generation [27–31]. These methods demonstrate the potential of diffusion-130 

style generation for medical reporting, but they typically rely on additional discriminators, abnormal-word 131 

semantic noise, radiology knowledge graphs, multi-stage fusion modules, or LLM assistance. The closest 132 

line of prior work is discrete-diffusion MIRG/RRG, which also studies iterative token refinement for medi-133 

cal report generation. SEDRRG is distinguished by combining Swin-V2 hierarchical global–patch evidence, 134 

denoising-time token-wise global–local gating, section-aware MFSL, and implementation-consistent iterative 135 

top-k structured decoding under a dual-dataset chest-X-ray RRG benchmark protocol. Thus, the key dis-136 

tinction of SEDRRG is not merely using diffusion for text, but maintaining persistent hierarchical visual 137 

evidence and report-derived structured supervision throughout image-conditioned full-sequence denoising. 138 

Supplementary Information, Table S1 provides a compact side-by-side comparison of diffusion-related RRG 139 

methods, but the principal distinctions are summarized here so that the main text remains self-contained. 140 

## ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT ARTICLE IN PRESS ARTICLE IN PRESS 2.4 Benchmark evaluation and factuality considerations 141 

Standard automatic metrics such as BLEU, ROUGE, METEOR, and CIDEr remain widely used in 142 

benchmark-oriented RRG studies because they provide reproducible comparisons across datasets and 143 

prior methods [6, 7]. They are useful for evaluating algorithmic changes such as visual conditioning, 144 

decoding mechanisms, and structured objectives, but they should not be interpreted as complete clinical 145 

safety or diagnostic-correctness measures. Factuality-oriented protocols, including CheXpert/CheXbert 146 

label matching, RadGraph F1, RadCliQ, and GREEN, provide complementary clinical signals when the 147 

research objective is clinical-factuality or deployment-oriented validation [32–36]. This paper focuses on 148 

the algorithmic question of whether visual-evidence-conditioned discrete denoising improves benchmark 149 

report generation. Accordingly, our evaluation uses standard NLG metrics, CIDEr when comparable values 150 

are available, ablations, diffusion-strategy analysis, qualitative examples, and inference-cost profiling. To 151 

provide complementary clinical-factuality evidence, we include a MIMIC-CXR clinical efficacy evaluation 152 

based on CheXbert-derived 14-observation labels extracted from generated and reference reports. This 153 

label-level evaluation is reported as a complementary clinical consistency signal, while fine-grained factuality 154 

assessment with RadGraph-style entity–relation extraction, RadCliQ/GREEN-style error analysis, and 155 

radiologist review remains important follow-up validation for deployment-oriented studies. 156

3.2 Overall Framework 198 

SEDRRG follows a five-stage workflow, as illustrated in Fig. 2: hierarchical visual evidence encoding, 199 

denoising-time global–local conditioning, conditional discrete diffusion denoising, structured report decoding, 200 

and training-time multi-factor structured supervision. This organization separates the model/inference flow 201 

from the training-only supervision flow. The solid arrows in Fig. 2 indicate how image evidence and report 202 

tokens are processed to generate a structured report, whereas the dashed arrows indicate forward corruption 203 

and supervision signals used only during training. 204 

The hierarchical visual evidence encoder first converts the input chest X-ray into two complementary 205 

evidence streams. The global anatomical feature fg summarizes image-level context, such as cardiomediasti-206 

nal configuration and overall pulmonary status. The patch-level visual evidence tokens Fp preserve spatially 207 

localized visual cues that are important for findings such as opacity, atelectasis, effusion, and consolidation. 208 

Using both streams prevents the generator from relying only on a single pooled representation or only on 209 

local tokens without report-level context. 210 

The denoising-time global–local conditioning module then transforms these evidence streams into a visual 211 

condition for the denoiser. At each denoising step, the module computes global and local visual conditions 212 

and learns an adaptive gate conditioned on the current hidden states and timestep. The resulting condition 213 

Ct is injected into the Transformer denoiser, allowing the model to use global anatomical information for 214 

report-level coherence and patch-level evidence for localized entity refinement. 215 

The conditional discrete diffusion denoiser forms the central generation module. During training, the 216 

reference report sequence x0 is corrupted into a noisy sequence xt through a categorical forward process. 217 

During reverse denoising, the Transformer denoiser receives xt, the timestep t, and the visual condition 218 

Ct, and predicts vocabulary distributions Pt and clean-token estimates ˆx0. Unlike autoregressive decoding, 219 

this formulation refines the whole report sequence iteratively, making it possible to revise earlier and later 220 

positions under the same image-conditioned context. 221 

Structured decoding is used only during inference to convert predicted token distributions into a section-222 

aware report ˆR. It preserves mandatory report markers, suppresses degenerate repetition, and formats the 223 

generated tokens into Findings and Impression sections when required by the benchmark protocol. This 224 

decoding stage produces the final report; it is distinct from the MFSL objective, which is used only for 225 

training. 226

3.3 Hierarchical Visual Evidence Encoding 234 

Radiology report generation requires visual evidence at multiple semantic granularities. Some clinical state-235 

ments depend on global anatomical context, such as cardiomediastinal enlargement, mediastinal contour, 236 

bilateral lung aeration, or overall pleural status. Other statements require localized visual evidence, such as 237 

focal opacity, atelectasis, pleural effusion, consolidation, or subtle abnormal shadows. Conditioning report 238 

generation on a single image-level representation may therefore discard spatially localized findings, whereas 239 

relying only on patch-level features may weaken global anatomical understanding. To address this issue, 240 

SEDRRG encodes each radiograph into complementary global and patch-level visual evidence. 241

The global and patch-level features play different roles in the diffusion generator. The global represen-262 

tation fg provides image-level context for determining the overall report semantics and section-level orga-263 

nization. In contrast, Fp preserves spatially distributed evidence that can be attended to when recovering 264 

localized clinical entities. This distinction is important for diffusion-based report generation: early denoising 265 

steps require stable global semantics to establish the coarse report trajectory, while later denoising steps 266 

benefit from localized visual evidence to refine entity-level descriptions. 267 

Rather than relying on an external abnormality classifier or a pre-computed knowledge base, SEDRRG 268 

keeps the visual evidence inside the generation process. The global branch and patch branch use independent 269 

projections and normalization layers so that the pooled anatomical representation and the local visual tokens 270 

can preserve complementary information before entering the denoising-time conditioning module. This design 271 

follows an evidence-preserving visual conditioning formulation: it strengthens image-conditioned generation 272 

through structured representation and repeated conditioning, while avoiding an additional disease-prompting 273 

stage that could commit the decoder to a fixed abnormality decision before report generation. 274 

The hierarchical visual evidence is therefore defined as 275 

Evis (I) = {fg, F p}. (9) This representation is subsequently injected into the diffusion denoiser through denoising-time gated global– 276 

local conditioning, as described in Section 3.5. In this way, the visual encoder does not merely provide 277 

static image features; instead, it supplies structured evidence that guides full-sequence report refinement 278 

throughout the reverse diffusion process. 279 

3.4 Image-Conditioned Discrete Token Diffusion 280 

Autoregressive report generators produce tokens sequentially from left to right. Although effective for fluent 281 

text generation, this factorization is vulnerable to exposure bias and error propagation, especially in long 282 

radiology reports where findings, negations, and impressions should remain mutually consistent. To alleviate 283 

this limitation, SEDRRG formulates report generation as an image-conditioned discrete token diffusion 284 

process. The model learns to recover a clean structured report from a corrupted token sequence through 285 

iterative denoising. 286

Data materials. We evaluate SEDRRG on two widely used chest X-ray report generation benchmarks: 509 

IU X-Ray [38] and MIMIC-CXR [39]. The MIMIC-CXR-JPG release provides the corresponding image files 510 

and metadata for large-scale chest radiograph analysis [40]. 511 

IU X-Ray. IU X-Ray contains 7,470 chest X-ray images associated with 3,955 de-identified radiology 512 

reports. Each report may include sections such as Findings , Impression , and Indication . Following common 513 

RRG practice, we adopt the conventional 7:1:2 train/validation/test split used by prior report-generation 514 

studies. We keep the same fixed split throughout all comparisons and ablations rather than creating a new 515 

random partition, which improves comparability with earlier RRG work. This dataset provides a limited-data 516 

setting in which language generalization and image-conditioned generation are especially challenging. 517 

MIMIC-CXR. MIMIC-CXR is a large-scale chest radiography dataset collected from routine clinical 518 

practice. The MIMIC-CXR-JPG release contains 377,110 chest radiographs from 227,835 studies and pro-519 

vides standardized metadata for medical vision–language research. Compared with IU X-Ray, MIMIC-CXR 520 

is more challenging because it contains longer reports, a larger vocabulary, heterogeneous clinical expressions, 521 

and a broader distribution of abnormal findings. For MIMIC-CXR, we use the official train/validation/test 522 

split provided with the MIMIC-CXR-JPG benchmark setting and preserve the official study-level separation 523 

to avoid leakage across evaluation partitions. For IU X-Ray, we follow the widely adopted split protocol used 524 

by previous RRG studies, including R2Gen and R2GenCMN, to ensure fair comparison. 525 

For both datasets, reports are converted into structured token sequences with explicit section markers. 526 

We retain the Findings and Impression sections when they are used by the corresponding benchmark proto-527 

col, normalize text to lowercase, remove non-informative symbols, and tokenize reports using dataset-specific 528 

medical word/ngram tokenizers constructed only from the training annotations. Candidate medical n-grams 529 

of lengths 1 to 4 are collected from normalized training reports and frequency-filtered during preprocessing. 530 

For IU X-Ray, we used a fixed 1,970/281/567 train/validation/test split, a frequency threshold of 3, an 531 

effective vocabulary size of 763, and a training/preprocessing sequence cap of 60 tokenizer positions. The 532 

released IU X-Ray inference configuration uses sample_max_len =12 phrase-aware tokenizer positions; be-533 

cause frequent medical n-grams may be represented by individual phrase tokens, one tokenizer position may 534 

detokenize to multiple words. For MIMIC-CXR, we used the official MIMIC-CXR-JPG split, a frequency 535 

threshold of 10, an effective vocabulary size of 4,902, and both a training/preprocessing cap and released 536 

inference sampling length of 100 word-token positions. The reported vocabulary sizes include the gener-537 

ated medical word/ngram entries and special tokens used by the corresponding preprocessing configuration. 538 

When a benchmark protocol evaluates only the Findings section, we follow the same protocol; no Impression -539 

section target is included, and Lstruct is applied only to the structure tokens available in the single-section 540 

target. Hyperparameters are selected only on the validation set, and final results are reported once on the 541 

held-out test set under the fixed benchmark split. To make the partitioning and preprocessing assumptions 542 

auditable, the train/validation/test counts, unit of analysis, report-section handling, missing-section policy, 543 

tokenizer construction, and released/restricted reproducibility materials are summarized in Supplementary 544 

Information, Table S7. 545 

Dataset access and ethics. This study used publicly available, de-identified chest X-ray datasets. No 546 

new human participants were recruited, and no additional patient data were collected by the authors. The 547

data-use agreements. Therefore, additional ethics approval and informed consent were not required for this 549

Evaluation metrics. Following established benchmark-oriented RRG studies, we report standard au-579

CIDEr when confirmed values are available [6, 7]. BLEU measures n-gram precision at different gran-581

Because the main contribution of this work is algorithmic, the evaluation is intentionally framed as a 589 

reproducible benchmark study of the proposed generation mechanism. We use standard NLG metrics and 590 

CIDEr-style consensus evaluation because these metrics are consistently reported by the selected baselines, 591 

and we complement them with component ablations, diffusion-strategy analysis, visual-granularity analysis, 592 

qualitative examples, and inference-cost profiling. This design follows a line of algorithm-oriented RRG 593 

studies, such as PPKED and UAR, that analyze architectural or alignment mechanisms through public 594 

benchmark datasets, automatic report-generation metrics, and controlled component studies [6, 7]. The 595 

purpose of this protocol is therefore to isolate the effect of image-conditioned discrete denoising and structured 596 

evidence modeling under comparable benchmark settings. 597 

We emphasize that these automatic overlap-based metrics quantify textual similarity and consensus with 598 

reference reports, but they do not directly measure clinical factual correctness, diagnostic reliability, or de-599 

ployment safety. Clinical-factuality protocols such as CheXpert/CheXbert-style label matching, RadGraph-600 

style entity–relation extraction, RadCliQ/GREEN-style error analysis, and radiologist assessment provide 601 

complementary evidence when the objective is clinical reliability or deployment-oriented validation [32–36]. 602 

Clinically oriented studies such as META-CXR and R2GenGPT illustrate the value of such complementary 603 

clinical-efficacy evaluation when the research aim includes clinical reliability analysis [15, 19]. 604 

As a complementary clinical efficacy evaluation, we use CheXbert-based 14-observation label extraction 605 

on MIMIC-CXR: No Finding, Enlarged Cardiomediastinum, Cardiomegaly, Lung Opacity, Lung Lesion, 606 

Edema, Consolidation, Pneumonia, Atelectasis, Pneumothorax, Pleural Effusion, Pleural Other, Fracture, 607 

and Support Devices. CheXbert is applied identically to generated and reference reports to obtain label 608 

vectors, and the same label order is used for both sets of reports. Positive observations are treated as 609 

positive labels, whereas negative, uncertain, and unmentioned observations are mapped to the non-positive 610 

class following the binary label-matching protocol used for this evaluation. Precision, recall, and F1 are then 611 

computed in a micro-averaged manner over all samples and all 14 labels on the MIMIC-CXR test split. This 612 

CheXbert-based evaluation is performed after generation and is not used as a training loss. The reported 613

metrics. They do not establish fine-grained clinical factuality or radiologist-validated safety. Accordingly, 615

Implementation details. The visual encoder was implemented using a Swin Transformer V2 backbone 617 

[37]. The input radiographs were resized and normalized using ImageNet statistics. During training, each 618 

image was resized to 256 pixels and randomly cropped to 224 × 224 ; during validation and testing, images 619 

were resized to 224 ×224 without random augmentation. This resolution follows common image-classification 620 

backbone settings and keeps the dual-dataset benchmark training feasible on a single GPU. It should be 621 

interpreted as a benchmark preprocessing choice rather than an assertion that 224 × 224 images preserve 622 

all diagnostically relevant CXR detail for clinical reading. The limitation of reduced spatial resolution is 623 

acknowledged in the Discussion, and future deployment-oriented studies should evaluate higher-resolution 624 

encoders and region-level supervision. We did not use random left–right flipping, because laterality can 625 

be clinically meaningful in chest radiology reports and an unpaired horizontal flip may introduce side-626 

specific image–text inconsistency. The Swin Transformer V2 backbone used a patch size of 4, an embedding 627 

dimension of 96, stage depths of {2, 2, 6, 2}, attention heads of {3, 6, 12 , 24 }, a window size of 7, an MLP ratio 628 

of 4, and a stochastic depth rate of 0.1. The final visual feature dimension was 768. The final-stage 7 × 7629 

Swin feature map was flattened into Np = 49 patch-level visual tokens. The Swin-V2 visual features have 630 

dimension 768 and are projected into the 512-dimensional denoiser hidden space; the average-pooled final-631 

stage feature was projected separately as the global anatomical feature. The visual encoder was not frozen; it 632 

was fine-tuned jointly with the denoising generator using a smaller learning rate than the report-generation 633 

branch. 634 

The report generator was implemented as a Transformer-based discrete diffusion denoiser. The token 635 

embedding and denoiser hidden dimension were set to d = 512 , the feed-forward dimension was set to 512, 636 

the number of Transformer layers was set to 3, the number of attention heads was set to 8, and the dropout 637 

rate was set to 0.1. The diffusion process used 9 denoising steps with the cosine cumulative corruption 638 

schedule described above unless otherwise specified in the strategy analysis. The replacement distribution 639 

was concentrated on [MASK] tokens in the main experiments. The training/preprocessing sequence cap and 640 

vocabulary size were set in a dataset-specific manner: IU X-Ray used a cap of 60 tokenizer positions with 641 

an effective vocabulary size of 763, whereas MIMIC-CXR used a cap of 100 word-token positions with an 642 

effective vocabulary size of 4,902. The released inference configuration uses sample_max_len =12 phrase-643 

aware tokenizer positions for IU X-Ray and sample_max_len =100 word-token positions for MIMIC-CXR. 644 

Medical token/phrase consistency and report-structure supervision signals were derived from the reference 645 

reports during training and were not used at inference time. 646 

The structured objective used the default MFSL weights λ1 = 1 .0, λ2 = 0 .8, λ3 = 0 .5, and λ4 = 0 .3, with 647 

contrastive temperature τ = 0 .07 for the default runs. These values were kept fixed for the held-out test 648 

evaluation and all component ablations unless the ablation explicitly removed the corresponding loss term. 649 

The model was trained with a batch size of 16 using Adam with AMSGrad and a weight decay of 5×10 −5.650 

The visual encoder was fine-tuned with a learning rate of 5 × 10 −5. The report denoiser and remaining non-651 

visual parameters used dataset-specific learning rates: 2 × 10 −5 for IU X-Ray and 8 × 10 −7 for MIMIC-CXR. 652 

A StepLR scheduler was used. Following the released training scripts, IU X-Ray used a step size of 50, decay 653 

factor 0.1, maximum 100 epochs, and seed 9233, whereas MIMIC-CXR used a step size of 1, decay factor 654

SEDRRG is positioned as an algorithm-oriented RRG study. It differs from Transformer encoder–decoder 766 

studies that mainly modify the visual or textual backbone, because it replaces strictly left-to-right generation 767 

with image-conditioned full-sequence denoising. It also differs from knowledge-distillation and cross-modal-768

gressive decoder or refining text-to-image attention in a standard Transformer, SEDRRG injects hierarchical 770 

visual evidence repeatedly along the discrete denoising trajectory [6, 7]. It further differs from LLM- and 771

The ablation studies show that HVE and MFSL are complementary. Visual evidence improves image-775 

conditioned generation, whereas MFSL constrains the denoising trajectory using report-derived structured 776 

signals. The medical token/phrase consistency surrogate increases supervision on selected clinically salient 777

diffusion provides the refinement mechanism, while structured supervision guides the refinement process 781 

toward visual-evidence-conditioned and structurally plausible reports. 782 

6.1 Limitations and future work 783 

Several limitations remain. First, SEDRRG is mainly trained with image–report pairs and report-level 784 

supervision, without dense region-level annotations such as bounding boxes, region masks, or fine-grained 785 

image–sentence alignments. The benchmark image preprocessing also uses 224 × 224 inputs for compatibil-786 

ity with the selected Swin-V2 backbone and single-GPU training; this setting is not intended to represent 787 

the spatial resolution required for clinical image interpretation, and higher-resolution encoders should be 788 

explored in future work. Very subtle or rare findings may therefore still be missed when visual cues are 789 

weak or underrepresented. Second, although the unedited qualitative example indicates coverage of several 790 

key entities, it also shows that SEDRRG can produce redundant phrases and local expression inconsisten-791 

cies. This limitation reinforces that the method should be interpreted as an algorithmic report-generation 792 

model rather than a clinically deployable reporting system. Third, although we include a complementary 793 

14-label clinical efficacy evaluation on MIMIC-CXR, this metric remains a label-level approximation. It 794 

captures whether major observations are preserved, but it cannot fully evaluate anatomical grounding, tem-795 

poral change, uncertainty, severity, or fine-grained disease–anatomy relations. The observed clinical efficacy 796 

## ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT ARTICLE IN PRESS ARTICLE IN PRESS profile is moderate: the recall of 0.375 and precision of 0.322 indicate that both missed clinically relevant 797 

observations and false-positive findings remain. Therefore, we do not claim that SEDRRG is clinically reli-798 

able or ready for deployment based on automatic metrics alone. More fine-grained factuality evaluation with 799 

RadGraph-style entity–relation extraction, RadCliQ/GREEN-style error analysis, and radiologist assessment 800 

remains necessary for deployment-oriented validation. Finally, iterative denoising increases inference cost 801 

compared with one-pass generation; however, the efficiency analysis shows that the default 9-step setting 802 

provides a practical quality–cost trade-off. 803 

## 7 Conclusion 804 

SEDRRG formulates radiology report generation as image-conditioned full-sequence denoising and integrates 805 

hierarchical visual evidence encoding, denoising-time global–local conditioning, and multi-factor structured 806 

supervision. Experiments on IU X-Ray and MIMIC-CXR indicate that the proposed components pro-807 

vide complementary benefits across standard report-generation metrics, CIDEr-style consensus evaluation, 808 

ablation studies, qualitative analysis, and inference-cost profiling. A complementary MIMIC-CXR clinical 809 

efficacy evaluation yields a precision of 0.322, recall of 0.375, and F1 score of 0.346, indicating moderate auto-810 

matic label-level clinical-efficacy performance with remaining missed and false-positive findings. The results 811 

suggest that SEDRRG is an algorithm-oriented alternative to strictly autoregressive report decoding and 812 

LLM-prompt-based generation under public benchmark protocols, while the main comparison table should 813 

be understood as a mixed reproduced/literature benchmark rather than a fully unified reimplementation of 814 

every baseline. They should not be interpreted as evidence of clinical deployment readiness. Future work 815 

will explore accelerated sampling, stronger region-level evidence modeling, fine-grained clinical factuality 816 

assessment, expert validation, and broader external evaluation. 817
