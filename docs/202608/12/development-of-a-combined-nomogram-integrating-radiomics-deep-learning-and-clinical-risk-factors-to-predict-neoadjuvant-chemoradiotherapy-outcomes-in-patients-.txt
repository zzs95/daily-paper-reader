# Abstract 

# Objective 

To develop a nomogram used to non-invasive prediction of nCRT outcome in patients with rectal cancer. 

# Materials and Methods 

A total of 292 rectal cancer patients were retrospectively collected and randomly divided into a training (n = 230) and a validation dataset (n = 62). Both intra-tumoral and peri-tumoral regions were manually labeled on the T2-weight MR images. Deep features and radiomics features were extracted from the intra-tumor regions only and the combined intra- and peri-tumoral regions, respectively, and used for the development of deep learning models and radiomics model. Clinical risk factors were selected through univariate and multivariate regression analysis. Furthermore, a nomogram was constructed by integrating DL features, radiomics features and clinical risk factors. The performance of these models was evaluated by receiver operating characteristics curve (ROC) analysis and decision curve analysis (DCA). 

# Results 

The AUCs of the DL model and radiomics model based on intra-tumor regions were 0.666 (95% CI, 0.534–0.780) and 0.817 (95% CI, 0.698–0.903) in the validation dataset, respectively. The performance of DL model and radiomics model was improved after integrating peri-tumoral regions, with AUCs increasing to 0.739 (95% CI, 0.612–0.842) and 0.845 (95% CI, 0.731–0.924) in the validation dataset, respectively. The nomogram showed the best predictive capability with AUCs achieving 0.921 (95% CI, 0.824–0.874) in the validation dataset, which was signi fi cantly higher than other models (all p-values < 0.05). All models showed good calibration, and the DCA demonstrated that the net bene fi t of the nomogram was higher than other models across majority range of threshold probabilities. 

# Conclusion 

The nomogram incorporating deep learning features, radiomics signatures and clinical variables showed good performance and can be served as a noninvasive tool for predicting postoperative outcomes in patients with rectal cancer. 

# Introduction 

Rectal cancer (RC) is one of the most prevalent gastrointestinal malignancies worldwide, ranking third in incidence and second in cancer-related mortality[ 1]. Approximately 70% of the cases are diagnosed in 

> Page 3/22

the locally advanced stage when the tumor were founded[ 2], posing a signi fi cant threat to an individual’s physical and mental health. 

The standard treatment for locally advanced RC involves neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (nCRT) followed by total mesorectal excision (TME)[ 3]. nCRT is a therapy method that combines chemotherapy and radiotherapy. It can reduce the tumour volume, downstage the tumour, increase the resectability of surgery, eliminate possible peritoneal metastatic cells, decrease the postoperative local recurrence rate, and improve the sphincter preservation rate[ 4–6]. 

The outcome of nCRT is the basis for patient's subsequent treatment strategy: patients who achieve a complete clinical response(PCR) after nCRT may undergo a "watch and wait" strategy; For patients with partial responder to nCRT, an individualised and more intensive nCRT treatment approach would be carry out in order to promote further tumour regression and improve the pathological complete response (pCR) rate; While Non-responders to nCRT treatment can undergo timely surgical resection of the tumour, avoiding tumour progression and adverse reactions caused by radiotherapy and chemotherapy[ 4, 7–9]. Accurate prediction of nCRT outcome is important to clinical. Additional, accurate prediction of nCRT outcome is bene fi t to interests of patients, e.g, spare patients from unnecessary side effects of radiotherapy and chemotherapy, save unnecessary economic expenses, and prevent patients from risking tumour progression during the waiting period for surgery and missing the optimal time for surgery. 

The gold standard for evaluating the effectiveness of nCRT in RC is pathological examination after tissue sampling[ 12 ]. However, this evaluation method depends on rectal puncture sampling, and is limited by the puncture sampling site and technology. Additionally, this method is associated with invasive and infectious complications, making it unsuitable for follow-up monitoring of the effect of nCRT in patients with RC. Digital rectal examination is a clinical commonly used evaluate method through the assess of the presence of indurations, protrusions, and ulcers in the rectum. Simple and practicable is its outstanding advantage, While this, it cannot determine residual tumours or quantify the depth of tumour invasion via a surgeon's  fi nger touch[ 13 ]. Another commonly used method of nCRT outcome evaluation is the detection of serum carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), a tumour marker for RC, which is widely used in prognosis and disease monitoring. Studies[ 14 ] have shown that CEA levels ≤ 5 μg/L after nCRT indicated patients had achieved clinical and pathological complete remission. But for RC patients without CEA secretion, CEA values always remain within the normal range, making this method is challenging to accurately determine and quantify the effectiveness of nCRT[ 12 ]. Accurate evaluate of treatment effectiveness of nCRT is challenging, and there is a lack of non-invasive, accurate, and personalised evaluation methods for assessing nCRT e ffi cacy. 

After nCRT, lesions might be accompanied by oedema,  fi brosis, and in fl ammation, making it more challenging to evaluate the effect of nCRT via MRI alone, a single diagnostic method might not su ffi ce to meet clinical needs. Currently, Deep learning, based on a convolutional neural network, is similar to the human neural network and can automatically learn features of target images on multiple levels. It can 

> Page 4/22

extract more comprehensive, deeper, and three-dimensional high-dimensional image features, revealing the biological characteristics of lesions[ 16 –18 ]. Thus, this study aimed to establish an effective pre-nCRT prediction model for RC patients using MRI and computer deep learning analysis. This method will integrate laboratory parameters, clinical markers, conventional imaging, and high-dimensional features extracted by deep learning before nCRT treatment, with the aims to build a muti-model nomogram used to non-invasive prediction of nCRT outcome to improve RC patients prognosis. 

# Materials and Methods 

# Patients 

This retrospective study was approved by Institutional Review Board (IRB) from Qujing Central Hospital of Yunnan Province(version No.202301) and written informed consent from participants has been waived as the retrospective nature of this study. Pre-operative T2-weighted images of patients with rectal cancer from January 2019 to January 2021 were initially collected and analyzed. The recruitment criteria were: (1) Patients had undergo nCRT and following TME (n = 1217); (2) Patients who underwent MRI examination before nCRT; (3) MR images without motion artifacts and metal artifacts; (4) Patients with pathological tumor regression grading report (valid group: TRG1 + TRG2, invalid group: TRG3); (5) Intact laboratory test results [including carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), carbohydrate antigen(CA) 19 − 9, CA125 results]; The exclusion criteria were: (1) Patients who had not undergo MRI examination before nCRT (n = 243); (2) MR images with motion artifacts and metal artifacts (n = 10); (3) Lack of laboratory test results (n = 85); (4) Patients with other malignant diseases (n = 6); (5) Patients without pathological tumor regression grading report (n = 581). Finally, a total of 292 patients were enrolled and were randomly assigned into the training dataset (n = 230) and the validation dataset (n = 62). The  fl owchart for patient enrollment was shown in Fig. 1. 

nCRT protocol 

The nCRT treatment plan for the patients comprised long-term chemoradiotherapy as follows: the radiotherapy target area included the rectal tumour area and the regional lymph node drainage area. The radiation intensity was DT 45.0-50.4 Gy, with 1.8-2.0 Gy per fraction, for 25 fractions. The standard dose of capecitabine was 825mg/m2, administered orally twice a day (for a total dose of 1650mg/m2/day), and given on the day of radiotherapy  fi ve times a week. TME was performed 6–8 weeks after radiotherapy. 

# Acquisition of MR images 

All the MR images in our study were obtained with 3.0 Tesla MR scanner (SIGNA Architect, GE Medical System, America; MAGENTOM Skyra, Siemens Healthcare Sector, Germany). The acquisition parameters were as follows: The mainly scanning sequence was fast spin-echo T2-weight imaging (FSE-T2WI). Repetition/echo time (TR/TE) = 6890/100 ms; echo train length = 25; slice thickness = 3 mm; spacing 

> Page 5/22

between slices = 3.9mm; number of averages = 3;  fl ip angle = 150°, Field of view (Fov) = 23cm×23cm, voxel size = 0.7mm×0.7mm×4.5mm. Patients received special bowel preparations before the MR examination, such as patients were advised to consume liquid food on the day before the examination and were administered a routine cleansing enema to ensure that the rectum was clear and empty 3–4 h before the examination. 

Reference Standard for Pathology 

The outcome of nCRT for RC was con fi rmed through pathological analysis of surgical samples. The Department of Pathology at local hospital provided the necessary records, which were accessed through their online database. The evaluation of the nCRT outcome was based on the eighth edition of the tumour regression grading (TRG) scoring system of the American Joint Committee on Cancer. The partial responders group was de fi ned as having TRG1 or TRG2 scores, while the Non-responders group was de fi ned as having a TRG3 score. 

# Annotation of the intra-tumoral and peri-tumoral regions 

Both the intra-tumoral and peri-tumoral regions were manually labeled on the T2-weighted MR images by a radiologist with 10 years of experience in abdominal imaging. The three-dimension segmentations were manually labeled within the border of the region of interest (ROI) by using the ITK-SNAP software (v3.8.0,  http://www.itksnap.org ). In addition, the ROIs was revised and modi fi ed by a senior radiologist with 15 years of experience. 

# Selection of the clinical variables 

Clinical risk factors including patient characteristics (age, gender), radiological  fi ndings {extramural vascular invasion (EMVI), circumferential resection margin (CRM), MR-T stage, MR-N stage, MR-M stage, tumor location and tumor gross type} and serum biomarkers {carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), carbohydrate antigen 125 (CA125) and carbohydrate antigen 19 − 9 (CA19-9)} were collected from the Electronic Medical Records (EMR) and Radiology Information System (RIS) of local hospital. These clinical risk factors were  fi rstly analyzed by univariate analysis and those signi fi cantly associated with postoperative outcome were retained and further selected by multivariate analysis, and only the clinical risk factors with a p-value less than 0.05 were used for model construction. 

# Pretreatment of the labeled MR images and the design of predictive models 

Before model development, the N4 bias  fi eld correction algorithm was  fi rstly applied to the MR images to correct the low frequency intensity non-uniformity. Then the resampling and interpolation were applied, and the MR images were reconstructed to the isotropic voxels of 1 mm 3 with B-spline surface 

> Page 6/22

construction method. Based on the manually labeled intra-tumoral ROIs and peri-tumoral ROIs, two radiomics models and two DL models were constructed: the Rad-Tumor model and DL-Tumor model using only the intra-tumoral ROIs as input images, and the Rad-Tumor+Peri model and DL-Tumor+Peri model using the combination of intra- and peri-tumoral ROIs as input images. In addition, the Radscore and DLscore of each patient were calculated by the Rad-Tumor+Peri model and the DL-Tumor+Peri model, respectively. 

The conceptual illustration of the image preprocessing and model construction was shown in Fig.  2.

# Radiomics model development 

The radiomics features were extracted by using an open-source python package named PyRadiomics (version 3.0,  https://pyradiomics.readthedocs.io/en/latest ) with the bin size  fi xed to 32, and a total of 1561 radiomics features including 14 shape features, 306  fi rst-order statistics features and 1241 texture features [238 Gray Level Dependence Matrix (GLDM) features, 374 Gray Level Co-occurrence Matrix (GLCM) features, 272 Grey Level Size Zone Matrix (GLSZM) features, 272 Gray Level Run Length Matrix (GLRLM) features and 85 Neighboring Gray Tone Difference Matrix (NGTDM) features] were extracted from each manually labeled ROI. 

The inter-observer reliability of the radiomics features was  fi rstly evaluated and 30 ROIs were randomly selected and labeled by two radiologists independently. Only the radiomics features with ICCs > 0.80 were considered to be stable and retained for subsequent analysis. Next, Pearson correlation analysis was applied, and the highly correlated multicollinear features with |r|  ≥ 0.95 were removed. Finally, the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression was performed to prevent the over fi tting and reduce computation complexity, and the most discriminating features were selected with penalty parameter tuning conducted via ten-fold cross-validation. 

Based on the selected radiomics features, the logistic regression classi fi er was used for the construction of the radiomics models. 

# Deep learning (DL) model development 

In order to improve classi fi cation accuracy and suppress over fi tting, several sophisticated data augmentation methods were applied to the labeled ROIs prior to model development, including the 3-dimensional rotation, translation, scaling (0.9, 0.95, 1.05 and 1.1 times) and  fl ipping. After data augmentation, the sample size for model training had increased to 8 times that of the original data. 

The backbone architecture for the DL model used in this study was ResNet50, which is a convolutional neural network with 50 layers deep. By using Bottlenecks (the basic residual blocks in ResNet50), which contain skip connections that allow the input to be directly passed to a later layer, ResNet50 can maintain the quality of the features and meanwhile increase its depth. The output of the last residual 

> Page 7/22

block was fed into an average pooling layer and then the learnable feature vector is passed through a fully connected layer, which produced the  fi nal classi fi cation output. 

The training process of the DL models was performed by using the Adam optimizer with a weight decay of 10 − 6  and an initial learning rate of 0.001. The Cross-Entropy Loss was used to measure the difference between the output predicted by the DL model and the ground truth. The minibatch size was set to 16, and dropout rate was set to 0.5 for the acceleration of convergence. The model was implemented by four NVIDIA RTX A4000 GPUs on Ubuntu 18.04.6 LTS, Python 3.7.13 and PyTorch 1.12.1. The cross-entropy loss and corresponding AUC during the training process of the DL models were presented in Supplementary Fig. 1. 

# Development of the nomogram 

In order to build a more individualized model and integrate the radiomics feature, deep learning features and clinical risk factors, a nomogram was established by incorporating the DLscore calculated by the DL-Tumor+Peri model, Radscore calculated by the Rad-Tumor+Peri model and the selected clinical factors in the training dataset. 

The binary classi fi cation performance of the predictive models was assessed by the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis in the training and validation dataset with respect to the area under the curve (AUC). In addition, the sensitivity, speci fi city, PPV and NPV were calculated under the optimal cutoff threshold which was determined by the maximum Youden Index. 

# Clinical utility analysis 

To evaluate the consistency between the predicted probability and actual observed rate, the Hosmer– Lemeshow test was applied and the calibration curve of each model was generated via 1000 bootstrapping resampling method. In addition, the clinical usefulness of the predictive models was evaluated and compared with decision curve analysis (DCA) by calculating the net bene fi ts across a range of reasonable threshold probabilities. 

# Statistical 

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 23.0) and MedCalc software (version 18.0). The Mann–Whitney U test and the Chi-square test were used to compare the continuous variables and categorical variables between two groups, respectively. The difference between two AUCs was assessed by Delong’s test. The calibration curve and the nomogram were plotted using the “rms” package (version 6.3), and the decision curve was plotted using the “rmda” package (version 1.6). A two-sided p less than 0.05 was considered statistically signi fi cant.

Comparison of the radiomics models and the deep learning models 

The intra-tumoral regions-based radiomics model (Rad-Tumor model) and deep learning model (DL-Tumor model) had achieved AUCs of 0.672 (95% CI, 0.607-0.732) and 0.843 (95% CI, 0.789-0.887) in the training dataset, and 0.666 (95% CI, 0.534-0.780) and 0.817 (95% CI, 0.698-0.903) in the validation datasets, respectively. After the combination of both intra- and peri-tumoral regions, the AUCs of the radiomics model (Rad-Tumor+Peri model) and deep learning model (DL-Tumor+Peri model) were increased to 0.745 (95% CI, 0.683-0.800) and 0.866 (95% CI, 0.815-0.907) in the training dataset, and 0.739 (95% CI, 0.612-0.842) and 0.845 (95% CI, 0.731-0.924) in the validation dataset, respectively(Figure 3). The deep learning models outperformed radiomics models in the training dataset (DL-Tumor model vs Rad-Tumor model, p = 0.001; DL-Tumor+Peri model vs Rad-Tumor+Peri model, p = 0.010), and although not statistically signi fi cant, the similar results were observed in the validation dataset (DL-Tumor model vs Rad-Tumor model, p = 0.068; DL-Tumor+Peri model vs Rad-Tumor+Peri model, p = 0.196). In addition, the incorporation of peri-tumoral regions could also improve the performance of the radiomics model (Rad-Tumor model vs Rad-Tumor+Peri model, p = 0.015 in the training dataset and 0.133 in the validation dataset) and the deep learning model (DL-Tumor model vs DL-Tumor+Peri model, p = 0.567 in the training dataset and 0.667 in the validation dataset). 

Construction of the nomogram 

To integrating radiomic features and deep learning features from MR images as well as related clinical variables, a nomogram was developed by incorporating the EMVI, tumor location and the risk scores calculated by the Rad-Tumor+Peri model (Radscore) and the DL-Tumor+Peri model (DL score) in the training dataset (Figure 4). 

Comparison of the Radscore, DLscore and nomogram 

The performance of the Radscore (Rad-Tumor+Peri model), DLscore (DL-Tumor+Peri model) and the nomogram was evaluated in both training and validation datasets (Figure 5). The nomogram incorporating radiomics features, deep learning features and clinical variables exhibited the best discrimination capability with AUCs achieving 0.928 (95% CI, 0.887-0.958) and 0.921 (95% CI, 0.824-0.974) in the training and validation datasets, respectively, which were signi fi cantly higher than that of the Radscore or DLscore (Delong’s test, all p-values < 0.05).

Clinical utility analysis 

The calibration and decision curve analysis were performed in the validation dataset to evaluate the clinical utility of the predictive models. Good consistency of all the predictive models was found between the predicted probability and actual rate (Figure 6). 

The Hosmer–Lemeshow test indicated no signi fi cant deviation from an ideal  fi tting, as the non-signi fi cant statistic of the Rad-Tumor model, Rad-Tumor+Peri model, DL-Tumor model, DL-Tumor+Peri model and the nomogram were 0.206, 0.490, 0.791, 0.922 and 0.368 respectively. The decision curve analysis also demonstrated that the Radscore, DLscore and the nomogram performed better than the treat-all and treat-none strategies. Furthermore, the nomogram showed higher net bene fi t than the Radscore and DLscore across the majority range of threshold probabilities (Figure 7). 

# Discussion 

> Page 12/22

The accurate diagnosis of nCRT outcomes for patients with RC is closely associated with patient's economic costs, toxic side effects caused by radiotherapy and chemotherapy, and treatment protocol making. The search for the best method to evaluate the effect of neoadjuvant therapy for RC has been a constant focus for clinicians and researchers. In this study, we construct several models, including a radiomics model, a deep learning model, a clinical model, and a nomogram model based on the radiomics, deep learning, and clinical characteristics of the tumour and peritumoural adipose tissue in patients with RC. Then we compared the diagnostic e ffi cacy of each model and found that the comprehensive nomogram model had the best diagnostic e ffi cacy. Furthermore, we evaluated the clinical practicability and calibration of the nomogram model. We aim to provide an accurate, easy-to-use, and personal nCRT response evaluate tool for clinical practice. 

In recent years, integrating deep learning and radiomics methods has become a research hotspot[13, 19, 20]. In this study, we found that the diagnostic e ffi cacy of the deep learning model based on tumour characteristics of RC was higher than that of the radiomics model in the training and validation groups (training group: 0.843 vs. 0.672; validation group: 0.817 vs. 0.666). Additionally, the diagnostic e ffi cacy of the deep learning models based on the features of peritumoural fat of RC was higher than that of the radiomics model in the training group and validation groups (training group: 0.866 vs. 0.745; validation group: 0.845 vs. 0.739), and the combination of them yield higher AUC than that of deep learning or radiomics methods alone. Radiomics can obtain statistical features from medical images through high-throughput computing and convert medical digital images into quantitative high-dimension data[7, 21, 22]. While, deep learning can obtain digital features from images by learning deep nonlinear mapping of data through layer-by-layer training[23-25]. On the other hand, the radiomics method relies on the accurate description of the lesion area and manual screening of features. The different details of the edge will affect the prediction results. The research process of deep learning is abstract and input-to-output or end-to-end process. Deep learning and radiomics are two research methods that can reveal the biological characteristics of lesions from different perspectives and have different research processes. The relationship between them is not antagonistic or incompatible. Adversely, the combination of these two methods not only captures abstract features that cannot be discerned by the naked eye but also depicts the distribution information of the voxel intensity in the lesion area, can effectively improve diagnostic accuracy by forming complementary advantages of multidomain information. 

Perirectal fat is a part of the visceral fat that surrounds the rectum within the mesorectal fascia (MRF) [26]. The mesorectal fat serves as a conduit for nutrient supply, for catabolite drainage to and from the normal rectal wall and rectal tumours via vessels and lymphatics[27], And can limit tumour outward invasion as a natural barrier. A previous study[28] has shown that perirectal fat not only plays a role in RC carcinogenesis and development by secreting multiple adipokines and cytokines but also is an independent predictor of disease-free survival and overall survival— a higher PFA results in a larger capacity for tumour cells within the MRF and subsequently reduces the incidence of a positive circumferential resection margin. Researchers[26-29]believe that perirectal fat contains valuable information regarding RC prognosis and can re fl ect tumour macro-and micro-environment changes, e.g, Jaya’s study found that radiomics features of mesorectal fat could predict pCR, local and distant 

> Page 13/22

recurrence, and post-treatment T and N stages of patients with RC[28]. Our study yielded similar results to previous studies, indicating that perirectal adipose tissue features improved the diagnostic performance of models assessing response to nCRT in RC, whether using radiomics, deep learning. Speci fi cally, our results showed that the models including perirectal adipose tissue features had higher area under the curve (AUC) values than the models comprising tumour features alone in the training and validation groups (training group: radiomics method, 0.745 vs. 0.672; deep learning method, 0.866 vs. 0.843. validation group: radiomics method, 0.739 vs. 0.666; deep learning method, 0.845 vs. 0.817). So we can conclude that perirectal adipose tissue does contribute to the assessment of nCRT response in RC. 

As a highlight of this study, we combined clinic characters, radiomics, deep learning features of tumor and perirectal adipose tissue to capture information about tumour biology that might not be visible to the human eye to improve diagnostic performance. Our combined model outperformed the radiomics and deep learning models alone in the training and validation groups (training group: 0.928 vs. 0.745, 0.866; validation group: 0.921 vs. 0.739, 0.845). 

However, this study also had some limitations that should be acknowledged: First, it was a retrospective study, and thus selection bias may exist. Second, we only used data from a single centre. Multicentre data or the implementation of external validation will help strengthen the model’s robustness. Lastly, radiomics and deep learning analysis methods involve complex computer operations and may not be easily accessible for some researchers without appropriate expertise. 

# Conclusion 

In conclusion, a nomogram model integrating radiomics, deep learning and clinical features had excellent diagnosis performance and the potential to serve as a preoperative biomarker for predicting nCRT outcomes of patients with rectal cancer in clinical practice.
