Title: Heart Failure Outcome Prediction Using Artificial                     Intelligence–enabled Coronary Artery Calcium CT Chamber Volumetry                     Compared to American Heart Association PREVENT and the Agatston Score for Heart                     Failure Risk Stratification

URL Source: https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/abs/10.1148/ryct.250406

Markdown Content:
## Abstract

### Purpose

To evaluate whether artificial intelligence (AI)–derived chamber volumetry from coronary artery calcium (CAC) CT improves heart failure (HF) risk prediction.

### Materials and Methods

This retrospective study included asymptomatic patients without known cardiac disease undergoing CAC CT between 2010 and 2023. CAC CT images were analyzed using a validated AI model to calculate chamber volumes. HF events were identified based on _International Classification of Diseases, Ninth and Tenth Revisions_ codes. Cox proportional hazard regression models incorporating chamber volumes, adjusted for Predicting Risk of cardiovascular disease EVENTs-Heart Failure (PREVENT-HF) score, were used to assess the association with HF. Time-dependent areas under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUCs) at 3, 5, 8, and 10 years were calculated to compare the performance of volumetry, PREVENT-HF, CAC scoring, and their combination.

### Results

A total of 5892 patients were included (mean age ± SD, 58.2 years ± 9.4; 3258 male). During a mean follow-up of 4 years ± 3, 377 patients (6.3%) developed HF. Larger left atrial, left ventricular, right atrial, and left ventricular myocardial volumes were associated with increased HF risk (_P_< .001). The composite, multivariable Cox regression model incorporating all chamber volumes, CAC score, and PREVENT-HF scores outperformed PREVENT-HF (AUC, 0.80 vs 0.76; ΔAUC, 0.04; _P_< .001) and CAC scores (AUC, 0.80 vs 0.70; ΔAUC, 0.10; _P_< .001) alone in predicting 10-year HF risk. A phase-volume interaction effect was identified, indicating that diastolic volumes were independently associated with higher HF risk than were systolic volumes (_P_< .001), adjusted for PREVENT-HF.

### Conclusion

AI-derived cardiac chamber volumetry obtained from CAC CT improved HF risk prediction compared with PREVENT-HF or CAC scores alone.

## Summary

Coronary artery calcium (CAC) CT–derived cardiac chamber volumes improved heart failure risk predictions over the PREVENT-HF clinical risk and CAC scores, enabling reliable chamber dilatation assessment in asymptomatic individuals.

## Key Points

■ In a retrospective study of asymptomatic patients without previous cardiovascular disease, artificial intelligence (AI)–derived coronary artery calcium (CAC) CT–obtained chamber volumes helped predict incident heart failure (10-year area under the receiver operating characteristic curve [AUC], 0.76 [95% CI: 0.72, 0.80]).

■ AI-derived volumetry combined with the PREVENT-HF clinical risk and CAC scores outperformed the PREVENT-HF score alone in predicting 10-year heart failure risk (AUC, 0.80 vs 0.76; ΔAUC, 0.04; _P_< .001).

■ Left atrial volume most strongly and consistently helped predict incident heart failure, particularly diastolic-phase volumes, with a 10-year AUC of 0.72 (95% CI: 0.67, 0.76).

## Introduction

Heart failure (HF) poses a growing public health burden, with the prevalence in the United States projected to reach 8.7 million by 2030 and an estimated annual increase in associated health care expenditures of US$ 22.3 billion ([1](https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/abs/10.1148/ryct.250406#core-collateral-r1)). Among Medicare beneficiaries, the estimated 1-year mortality rate of HF approaches 30%, largely because of late diagnosis and advanced disease severity at presentation ([2](https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/abs/10.1148/ryct.250406#core-collateral-r2)). Although early detection is important for slowing HF progression, identifying at-risk individuals within asymptomatic populations remains a significant challenge ([3](https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/abs/10.1148/ryct.250406#core-collateral-r3)). Imaging-derived markers, including left atrial (LA) and left ventricular (LV) volumes, have demonstrated strong prognostic value for HF-related hospitalization and mortality when measured at cardiac MRI or echocardiography ([4](https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/abs/10.1148/ryct.250406#core-collateral-r4)–[8](https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/abs/10.1148/ryct.250406#core-collateral-r8)). However, the incorporation of volumetric imaging into clinical screening strategies for subclinical HF remains limited, in part because of the costs, time burdens, and limited scalability of these modalities ([9](https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/abs/10.1148/ryct.250406#core-collateral-r9),[10](https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/abs/10.1148/ryct.250406#core-collateral-r10)).

There is increasing interest in deriving atrial and ventricular measurements from low-dose cardiac CT, given its use in coronary artery disease screening. Recent advances in deep learning (a form of artificial intelligence [AI]) models have enabled the automatic, high-fidelity segmentation of the cardiac chambers from noncontrast CT scans ([10](https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/abs/10.1148/ryct.250406#core-collateral-r10)–[12](https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/abs/10.1148/ryct.250406#core-collateral-r12)). Such AI-enabled chamber volumetry offers the opportunity to extend the prognostic value of coronary artery calcium (CAC) CT beyond atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease risk assessment to include HF risk stratification. With CAC CT use increasing more than threefold since 2006 and more than 1.5 million such scans obtained annually in the United States, this modality represents a promising platform for opportunistic HF risk assessment at scale ([13](https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/abs/10.1148/ryct.250406#core-collateral-r13)–[16](https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/abs/10.1148/ryct.250406#core-collateral-r16)).

Despite this potential, important knowledge gaps remain. First, the predictive value of AI-derived chamber volumes obtained from CAC CT has not been fully evaluated in the context of contemporary clinical HF risk models, such as the American Heart Association’s Predicting Risk of cardiovascular disease EVENTs–Heart Failure (PREVENT-HF) equation, which integrates key clinical and laboratory variables ([17](https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/abs/10.1148/ryct.250406#core-collateral-r17),[18](https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/abs/10.1148/ryct.250406#core-collateral-r18)). Second, the real-world robustness of AI-derived volumetry remains incompletely understood in the context of the acquisition-phase variability inherent to CAC CT. Because these scans are acquired at only one phase of diastole or systole, rather than at end-diastole or end-systole, phase-dependent differences in the chamber volume may meaningfully influence absolute measurements, risk thresholds, and predictive performance ([16](https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/abs/10.1148/ryct.250406#core-collateral-r16),[19](https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/abs/10.1148/ryct.250406#core-collateral-r19)).

To address these gaps, the aim of this study was to evaluate the ability of AI-derived cardiac chamber volumetry on CAC CT images to predict incident HF both independently from and in conjunction with the PREVENT-HF clinical risk calculator. The secondary aim of this study was to examine the influence of the cardiac acquisition phase (eg, diastolic vs systolic) on volumetric measurements, HF risk stratification, and prognostic performance.

## Materials and Methods

### Study Design and Sample

In this multiethnic, retrospective cohort study, patients who underwent CAC CT between January 2010 and May 2023 at Emory University–affiliated health care facilities in Atlanta, Georgia, were included. This study received ethical review from Emory University and followed Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act regulations. The institutional review board approved the study and waived the need for informed consent. Patients from this study were part of the Emory Cardiac CT Registry and were included in a previous study on CAC scoring and clinical risk scores (_n_ = 7610) ([20](https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/abs/10.1148/ryct.250406#core-collateral-r20)).

Patients were considered eligible if they _(a)_ had undergone CAC CT, _(b)_ had documented follow-up, _(c)_ had no known cardiac disease, _(d)_ were asymptomatic at the time of CT, _(e)_ met the PREVENT-HF age range criteria (30–79 years), and _(f)_ had clinical data sufficient to calculate the PREVENT-HF risk score.

Patients were excluded if their electronic medical records indicated a history of HF and cardiac disease; they had previous events associated with _International Classification of Diseases, Ninth and Tenth Revisions_ (ICD-9 and ICD-10) codes; and had any of the diagnoses listed in [Appendix S1](https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/abs/10.1148/ryct.250406#supplementary-materials) and [Table S1](https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/abs/10.1148/ryct.250406#supplementary-materials). Self-reported race was obtained from the electronic health record.

### CAC CT

Electrocardiographically gated, noncontrast CAC CT scans were obtained at 120 kVp and reconstructed at 3-mm section thickness, following clinical protocols. The scanners and vendors used were Siemens Somatom Definition AS, Somatom Flash, and Somatom Force (Siemens Healthineers); Philips iCT 256 (Philips); Canon Aquilion One (Canon); and GE CT750 HD (General Electric).

### Cardiac Chamber Volumetry and Analysis

Cardiac chamber volumes were measured during the systolic or diastolic phase, defined by R-R interval percentages of 0%–55% and 55%–100%, respectively. The cardiac phase was determined from the Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine header and series description. The relevant approach is described in [Appendix S2](https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/abs/10.1148/ryct.250406#supplementary-materials). An AI-enabled automated cardiac volumetry tool consisting of a three-dimensional segmentation network combined with a conditional variational autoencoder was used to quantify LA, LV, LV myocardial, right ventricular (RV), and right atrial (RA) volume. The image-to-image segmentation of a diastolic and systolic patient is shown in [Figure 1](https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/abs/10.1148/ryct.250406#fig1). The AI prototype used in this article was used for research purposes and may not be commercially available; further model specification is included in [Appendix S3](https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/abs/10.1148/ryct.250406#supplementary-materials).

### PREVENT-HF

The American Heart Association PREVENT-HF risk score was designed to estimate the 10-year risk of incident HF for patients age 30–79 years without cardiovascular disease at baseline. Clinical variables used to calculate the risk score included blood pressure, total cholesterol level, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol level, estimated glomerular filtration rate, and body mass index (calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared), as measured within 6 months of CT.

### HF Events

HF events were identified as the first historical HF ICD-10 code in the patient’s medical record. The time to event was calculated as the interval between the CAC CT examination and the first HF diagnosis. To assess the validity of the ICD-9– and ICD-10–based event ascertainment, a random 10% of the cohort was manually reevaluated using their full medical records and clinical notes, ensuring that equal proportions of patients with and without ICD-9– and ICD-10–identified HF events were included in the evaluation.

### Statistical Analysis

The patients were categorized as HF-positive or HF-negative at prespecified follow-up time points (3, 5, 8, and 10 years) on the basis of whether an HF diagnosis had been recorded by that time. The baseline characteristics of each diagnosis group are reported as means ± SDs and medians and IQRs depending on their distribution. Chamber volumes are presented as means and 95% CIs. Clinical risk and categorical data were dichotomized and are presented as percentages and counts. Comparisons were performed with two-tailed _t_ tests, Wilcoxon rank sum tests, and χ 2 tests. A global comparison across volumetric quartiles was performed first using analysis of variance. Cause-specific Cox proportional hazards models were used to evaluate the associations of the individual chamber volumes and the PREVENT-HF score with the risk of incident HF. In multivariable analyses, chamber volumetry models were adjusted for clinical risk by incorporating the PREVENT-HF risk score, which integrates established HF risk factors (including blood pressure, total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, glomerular filtration rate, and body mass index). In addition, an additional volume–phase interaction term was derived to represent differences in chamber volumetry when patients were imaged in diastolic or systolic phase. Competing risks were accounted for by treating non-HF death as a censoring event. Fine-Gray subdistribution hazards models and Aalen-Johansen estimators were used to estimate the cumulative incidence and absolute risk of HF in the presence of the competing risk of non-HF death. Hazard ratios (HRs) were normalized per 1-SD increase in the variable. Significance for individual chamber volumes and the interaction term was assessed using the Wald test, and differences in event-free survival among quartile groups were evaluated using the log-rank test.

The time-dependent area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) was calculated using 10-fold cross-validation to determine the predictive value of each metric and any improvements provided by the multivariable combination. Risk predictions were aggregated across folds to obtain cross-validated AUC estimates, with further fold stratification by event status to preserve outcome balance. The time-dependent AUCs were calculated at the 3-, 5-, 8-, and 10-year follow-up horizons. Incremental prognostic value beyond the PREVENT-HF clinical risk model and the CAC score was assessed using comparisons of the cross-validated ΔAUC, performed using the DeLong test with direct comparisons with the PREVENT-HF 10-year clinical risk model and the CAC score.

All tests of statistical significance were two sided, and a _P_ value less than .05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. Multiple comparison correction was performed using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure. The 95% CIs were obtained using 3000 bootstrapped samples. All the statistical analyses were performed in Python (version 3.12.2; _[https://www.python.org/downloads/release/python-3122/](https://www.python.org/downloads/release/python-3122/)_). Statistical analyses were conducted using specialized survival and statistical modeling libraries, including scikit survival, lifelines, and statsmodels.

## Results

### Patient Characteristics

Among an initial multiethnic group of 19 325 asymptomatic patients who had undergone CAC CT, 13 433 were excluded because of missing clinical variables required for calculation of the PREVENT-HF score and missing follow-up data; the remaining 5892 patients, who met the inclusion and exclusion criteria, had a mean follow-up period ± SD of 4 years ± 3 ([Fig 2](https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/abs/10.1148/ryct.250406#fig2)). [Table 1](https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/abs/10.1148/ryct.250406#tbl1) presents the baseline characteristics of the study sample at 3, 5, 8, and 10 years. The mean age was 58 years ± 9; 2634 patients (44.7%) were female and 3258 patients (55.3%) were male. The median CAC score was 4.8 (IQR, 0.0–81.8), and the median 10-year PREVENT-HF score was 3.0 (IQR, 1.5–5.7). Among the patients who were followed up, 377 (6.3%) developed HF, with an estimated cumulative incidence of 19.8% at 10 years ([Appendix S3](https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/abs/10.1148/ryct.250406#supplementary-materials)). Patients who developed HF were older (62.4 years ± 8.9 vs 54.0 years ± 8.7; _P_< .001), had a higher body mass index (29.0 ± 4.6 vs 27.1 ± 4.2; _P_< .001) and CAC score (44.0 [IQR, 0.6–253.2] vs 0.0 [IQR, 0.0–45.0]; _P_< .001), and were more likely to have hypertension (79.0% vs 52.0%; _P_< .001). A total of 2265 patients (38.4%) were imaged in the systolic phase (0%–55% of the R-R interval), and 3627 patients (61.6%) were imaged in the diastolic phase (55%–100% of the R-R interval).

### Chamber Volume Quantification

Overall, the baseline mean LA, LV, and LV myocardial volumes were greater in patients who developed HF ([Table 1](https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/abs/10.1148/ryct.250406#tbl1)). The LA volume was 79.5 mL (95% CI: 76.9, 82.1) in the HF group and 64.9 mL (95% CI: 62.2, 67.6) in the non-HF group (_P_< .001). Similarly, the LV volume was 120.1 mL (95% CI: 116.0, 124.2) in the HF group and 113.0 mL (95% CI: 108.3, 117.8) in the non-HF group (_P_< .001). The LV myocardial volume was 118.6 mL (95% CI: 115.6, 121.5) in the HF group and 111.2 mL (95% CI: 107.7, 114.8) in the non-HF group (_P_< .001).

### Cardiac Volumetry and Incidence of Heart Failure

The predictive performance of chamber volumetry for HF at 3, 5, 8, and 10 years is shown in [Table 2](https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/abs/10.1148/ryct.250406#tbl2). Across the time intervals, compared with the other chamber volumes, LA volume consistently demonstrated the greatest independent predictive performance for the development of HF ([Fig 3](https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/abs/10.1148/ryct.250406#fig3)). Specifically, time-dependent receiver operating characteristic curve analysis revealed that the left atrium had greater discriminative performance, with AUCs of 0.64 (95% CI: 0.60, 0.68) at 5 years and 0.72 (95% CI: 0.67, 0.76) at 10 years; comparatively, the LV volume exhibited AUCs of 0.55 (95% CI: 0.51, 0.59) at 5 years and 0.61 (95% CI: 0.56, 0.66) at 10 years. In addition, the AUCs of the LV myocardial volume were 0.58 (95% CI: 0.55, 0.62) at 5 years and 0.62 (95% CI: 0.57, 0.67) at 10 years. In contrast to the left chamber volumes, the right-sided chamber volumes exhibited limited discrimination.

### Model Performance

Detailed model performance evaluation metrics are given in [Table 2](https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/abs/10.1148/ryct.250406#tbl2). The chamber volumes alone outperformed the CAC score alone, but there was no evidence of a difference with the performance of the PREVENT-HF score. Specifically, compared with the CAC score alone, the all-chamber model demonstrated greater discrimination at 5 years (0.70 vs 0.57; ΔAUC, 0.13; _P_< .001) and 10 years (0.76 vs 0.70; ΔAUC, 0.08; _P_ = .03). When the LA volume was added to the PREVENT-HF score, the predictive performance for HF increased at 8 and 10 years, with AUCs of 0.75 versus 0.72 (ΔAUC, 0.03; _P_ = .006) and 0.76 versus 0.79 (ΔAUC, 0.03; _P_ = .01), respectively.

Further gains were observed when the chamber volumes, PREVENT-HF score, and CAC score were combined in a multivariable, composite model ([Fig 5](https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/abs/10.1148/ryct.250406#fig5)). Time-dependent AUC analysis revealed that the composite model achieved greater predictive performance than existing clinical and imaging metrics. Specifically, the composite model demonstrated the greatest discrimination among the investigated parameters across all time horizons, with AUCs of 0.73 (95% CI: 0.70, 0.76) at 5 years and 0.80 (95% CI: 0.76, 0.84) at 10 years. Cross-validated receiver operating characteristic curve analysis at 10 years of follow-up revealed that the composite model provided predictive gains over the CAC score (ΔAUC, 0.10; _P_< .001) and PREVENT-HF risk score (ΔAUC, 0.04; _P_< .001). Across all time intervals, the composite model provided consistent improvements in prediction over the CAC score and PREVENT-HF risk score, with ΔAUCs ranging from 0.10 to 0.16 and 0.03 to 0.04, respectively.

### PREVENT-HF Risk Score Analysis

A substantial number of low-risk patients with a PREVENT-HF risk score less than 5% presented with enlarged cardiac chambers and subsequently developed HF ([Fig 6](https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/abs/10.1148/ryct.250406#fig6)). After the Cox models were adjusted for the PREVENT-HF quartiles, the results of subanalyses revealed additional, independent gains by chamber volumetry over the PREVENT-HF score alone. In patients whose PREVENT-HF fell within the second quartile (representing the low- to intermediate-risk group), LA volume (HR, 2.65; _P_< .001) and LV myocardial volume (HR, 2.49; _P_< .001) provided incremental risk stratification over clinical risk assessment. The strongest and most consistent associations were observed in the highest PREVENT-HF quartiles, in which all five chamber volumes demonstrated independent associations with HF risk. Further details on the subanalysis of chamber volumes in predicting HF for the different PREVENT-HF quartiles is included in [Table S3](https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/abs/10.1148/ryct.250406#supplementary-materials).

### Interaction Analysis

The results of univariable and multivariable analyses following inclusion of the interaction term are presented in [Table 3](https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/abs/10.1148/ryct.250406#tbl3). Univariable analyses of chamber volumes revealed that LA, LV, RA, and LV myocardial volume were associated with an increased risk of HF regardless of whether diastolic or systolic imaging was performed, although the HRs were notably higher for patients who underwent diastolic imaging. The multivariable Cox proportional hazards model fully adjusted for the PREVENT-HF risk score revealed an interaction effect between cardiac phase and volume (_P_< .001). The results of hazard analysis indicated that a 1-SD increase in diastolic LA volume was associated with a 76% increased risk of HF (HR, 1.76 [95% CI: 1.49, 2.07]). In contrast, there was no evidence of an effect of a 1-SD increase in the systolic LA volume on the risk of HF (HR, 1.15 [95% CI: 0.97, 1.36]). A 1-SD increase in LV myocardial volume was associated with an increased risk of HF for patients in both the systolic and diastolic imaging groups, with HRs of 3.18 (95% CI: 1.82, 5.56) and 2.07 (95% CI: 1.19, 3.63), respectively. Interaction effects between both the LA and RA volumes and cardiac phase were also identified before and after adjustment for PREVENT-HF, indicating differential prognostic effects according to cardiac phase (LA volume-phase interaction, fully adjusted model: β = 0.428; _P_< .001).

## Discussion

In this retrospective cohort of 5892 asymptomatic patients, AI-derived cardiac chamber volumetry from CAC CT scans was independently associated with incident HF, with LA, LV, and LV myocardial volumes demonstrating the most consistent prognostic performance. A multivariable composite model comprising the PREVENT-HF risk score, the CAC score, and the chamber volumes demonstrated the greatest time-dependent discriminability across multiple follow-up horizons (AUC > 0.72) and greater predictive performance for HF risk prediction over the individual model components (PREVENT-HF ∆AUC, 0.04 [_P_< .001]; CAC score ∆AUC, 0.14 [_P_< .001]). Finally, volumetric measurements obtained during the diastolic phase demonstrated stronger associations with HF risk than those acquired during the systolic phase, indicating meaningful performance dependence on the acquisition phase. Collectively, these findings support the use of AI-derived cardiac chamber volumetry as an adjunct to contemporary HF risk assessment based on widely available CAC CT imaging.

The PREVENT-HF risk score was designed to estimate the long-term risk for incident HF. In our study, the AUC of the PREVENT-HF score increased over time, with the highest value of 0.76 obtained at the 10-year time point, consistent with its intended goal of 10-year risk prediction ([17](https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/abs/10.1148/ryct.250406#core-collateral-r17),[21](https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/abs/10.1148/ryct.250406#core-collateral-r21)). However, the PREVENT-HF score does not incorporate direct measures of cardiac structure or remodeling. In this context, the addition of chamber volumetry improved HF risk prediction over the PREVENT-HF and CAC scores alone, with the composite model demonstrating the greatest discrimination across all follow-up intervals. These improvements were accompanied by meaningful gains in predictive performance, with the integration of imaging-based chamber volumetry into PREVENT-HF and the CAC score improving the cross-validated ΔAUC across all follow-up intervals, peaking at 0.04 at year 10. This reflects a greater identification of individuals who subsequently developed HF, as well as more accurate classification of lower-risk individuals. Previous studies have highlighted heterogeneity in the risk of HF within PREVENT-HF risk strata, suggesting that population-calibrated clinical models may not adequately capture individual-level susceptibility. This is supported by the findings of previous studies; notably, Murphy et al ([22](https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/abs/10.1148/ryct.250406#core-collateral-r22)) reported that the PREVENT-HF risk calculator overestimated the relative HF risk by 62.6%. Of note, a substantial portion of the patients classified as low risk by use of the PREVENT-HF or CAC score in this study exhibited enlarged cardiac chambers and developed HF, underscoring the complementary prognostic value of structural remodeling in clinical risk assessment. Taken together, these findings suggest that integrating imaging-based chamber volumetry with established clinical and CAC-based risk models provides a more complete and individualized assessment of HF risk.

Among the heart chamber volumes, the LA volume was the strongest individual predictor of HF, extending the findings of previous works, demonstrating the unique prognostic benefits of LA volume ([23](https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/abs/10.1148/ryct.250406#core-collateral-r23)–[26](https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/abs/10.1148/ryct.250406#core-collateral-r26)). Previous studies using echocardiography, cardiac MRI, and cardiac CT have consistently linked increased LA volume to HF outcomes ([27](https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/abs/10.1148/ryct.250406#core-collateral-r27)–[30](https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/abs/10.1148/ryct.250406#core-collateral-r30)) because LA volume can reflect pathophysiologic HF processes, including booster-pump failure, electrical desynchrony, reservoir dysfunction, and impaired conduit function ([31](https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/abs/10.1148/ryct.250406#core-collateral-r31)). Reduced LA emptying in the setting of increased LA volume may further reflect elevated atrial afterload secondary to increased LV diastolic pressure ([30](https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/abs/10.1148/ryct.250406#core-collateral-r30)). The early changes noted in LA size and volume in incident HF may precede overt ventricular remodeling, suggesting a plausible mechanism by which the LA volume better helps predict incident HF than the LV volume ([31](https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/abs/10.1148/ryct.250406#core-collateral-r31)), particularly in asymptomatic patients with relatively short-term follow-up. The results of our study support the prediction strength of LA volume as one of the earliest indicators of future HF development. This finding aligns with previous population-based data ([23](https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/abs/10.1148/ryct.250406#core-collateral-r23)–[26](https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/abs/10.1148/ryct.250406#core-collateral-r26),[31](https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/abs/10.1148/ryct.250406#core-collateral-r31)), including that from a study by Fredgart et al ([26](https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/abs/10.1148/ryct.250406#core-collateral-r26)) involving a sample of 14 557 patients, of whom 129 developed new-onset HF over a median follow-up time of 2.1–3.4 years, revealing that LA enlargement was associated with HF with an HR of 2.40. Similarly, in our study, after PREVENT-HF was adjusted for, a 1-SD increase in diastolic LA volume was associated with a 76% increased risk of incident HF ([30](https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/abs/10.1148/ryct.250406#core-collateral-r30),[32](https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/abs/10.1148/ryct.250406#core-collateral-r32)).

Approximately 40% of patients in this study underwent CT during the mid-systolic phase, reflecting real-world acquisition practices in which systolic gating is often favored in individuals with higher body mass indexes or elevated heart rates to reduce motion artifacts. The cardiac acquisition phase significantly modified the prognostic performance of LA volumetry, with diastolic LA volume demonstrating stronger associations with incident HF than with systolic measurements both before and after adjustment for PREVENT-HF. These findings are physiologically coherent and align with those of previous studies using echocardiography and cardiac MRI that have revealed the greater prognostic value of end-diastolic chamber measurements relative to end-systolic volumetry ([33](https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/abs/10.1148/ryct.250406#core-collateral-r33)–[38](https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/abs/10.1148/ryct.250406#core-collateral-r38)). The persistence of HF risk prediction across both acquisition phases supports the robustness and generalizability of AI-derived chamber volumetry in CAC CT while also emphasizing the value of phase-specific interpretation when assessing structural HF risk.

The integration of AI-derived chamber volumetry into CAC CT imaging provides significant practical implications for HF risk assessment. Because CAC CT is already widely performed for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease risk stratification, AI-derived volumetric analysis enables the identification of clinically meaningful noncoronary findings without additional patient inconvenience, radiation exposure, or financial burden. Algorithms for automated chamber segmentation can enhance clinical efficiency, providing large-scale, simultaneous volumetry quantification that would otherwise require difficult, time-intensive manual analysis. In this context, chamber volumetry derived from CAC CT may serve as a tool for opportunistic HF risk enrichment by identifying individuals with subclinical structural remodeling who may benefit from closer surveillance or preventive approaches. Although future applications of AI-based cardiac analysis to other CT platforms, such as nongated CT for lung cancer screening, warrant investigation, the present findings support the feasibility and potential value of leveraging existing CAC CT imaging to enhance HF risk assessment ([12](https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/abs/10.1148/ryct.250406#core-collateral-r12)), a need underscored by the increasing incidence of HF and its massive economic burden on the health care system ([1](https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/abs/10.1148/ryct.250406#core-collateral-r1)).

This study had limitations. First, both previous CVD events and incident HF were analyzed on the basis of ICD-9 and ICD-10 codes, which may have led to an underestimation of event incidence due to institutional return bias. However, a recent study by Bates et al ([39](https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/abs/10.1148/ryct.250406#core-collateral-r39)) revealed that among Medicare patients, principal ICD-9 and ICD-10 diagnostic codes accurately identified the presence of HF in 98% of hospitalizations. Second, HF events were not differentiated by ejection fraction subtype (HF with reduced ejection fraction vs HF with preserved ejection fraction) because of limited data availability, precluding subtype-specific analyses. Third, other than traditional risk factors measured at the time of CT acquisition, time-varying risk factors were not assessed.

In conclusion, AI-derived chamber volumes from CAC CT improved HF risk prediction when combined with the PREVENT-HF risk calculator. These findings underscore the promising clinical utility of AI-derived volumetric analysis using CAC CT scans, providing valuable prognostic data without the need for further patient inconvenience or additional radiation exposure. Future studies should further investigate the prognostic implications of cardiac phase variability for chamber volumetry across HF subtypes, specifically HF with reduced ejection fraction vs HF with preserved ejection fraction.
