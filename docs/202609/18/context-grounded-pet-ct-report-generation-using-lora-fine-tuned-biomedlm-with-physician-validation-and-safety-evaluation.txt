Existing approaches to automated report generation can be broadly categorised into three paradigms: (i) template-based systems that assemble predefined text based on detected findings[[23](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#ref23 "Automatic medical report generation: A comprehensive review of methodologies and applications"),[25](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#ref25 "Proceedings of the AAAI Conference on Artificial Intelligence")]; (ii) retrieval-based systems that identify similar prior cases and adapt their associated reports[[25](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#ref25 "Proceedings of the AAAI Conference on Artificial Intelligence")]; and (iii) generative neural models that condition language generation on imaging features or structured clinical data[[20](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#ref20 "Automated radiology report generation: A review of recent advances"),[26](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#ref26 "Automatic radiology report generation with deep learning: A comprehensive review of methods and advances")] Template and retrieval systems produce lexically similar text when test cases closely resemble training data but fail to adapt to novel clinical presentations or patient-specific combinations of findings. Generative LLM approaches offer the flexibility to produce individualised reports; however, prior work has highlighted the risk of hallucinations, defined as the generation of plausible-sounding but clinically unsupported findings.[[27](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#ref27 "Survey of hallucination in natural language generation"),[28](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#ref28 "Best practices for large language models in radiology")] This is particularly concerning in nuclear medicine, where fabricated SUVmax values, incorrect laterality, or erroneous clinical findings could directly influence treatment decisions.[[28](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#ref28 "Best practices for large language models in radiology")]

Despite recent advances in Vision Language Model (VLM) and LLM-based PET-CT report generation[[24](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#ref24 "ConTEXTual Net 3D: Vision-language modeling in PET/CT for visual grounding of positive findings"),[29](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#ref29 "Proceedings of the AAAI Conference on Artificial Intelligence")–[31](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#ref31)], systematic evaluation remains limited. In particular, few studies have examined grounded, context-faithful report generation using biomedical LLMs with structured multi-region inputs or incorporated integrated clinical safety metrics and independent physician review. Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) methods, such as Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA), offer a practical solution for adapting large biomedical language models to domain-specific report generation tasks.[[31](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#ref31),[32](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#ref32 "Best practices for large language models in radiology")] These approaches substantially reduce the computational cost and memory requirements associated with full-parameter fine-tuning.[[33](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#ref33 "PeFoMed: Parameter efficient fine-tuning of multimodal large language models for medical CXR")] Furthermore, LoRA-based adaptation has been shown to improve factual consistency and control over hallucinations in medical text generation compared to purely retrieval-free baselines.[[14](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#ref14 "Toward expert-level medical question answering with large language models"),[17](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#ref17 "Large language models encode clinical knowledge"),[27](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#ref27 "Survey of hallucination in natural language generation"),[34](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#ref34)]

We hypothesise that a BioMedLM fine-tuned using LoRA on structured clinical context inputs can generate clinically coherent and context-grounded PET-CT reports that are acceptable to nuclear medicine physicians. We further hypothesise that such a system will demonstrate low rates of unsafe hallucination and accurate representation of anatomical laterality when evaluated using domain-specific faithfulness metrics. This study presents: (1) a clinical context extraction and prompt construction pipeline for multi-region PET-CT reporting; (2) a faithfulness-augmented training framework incorporating entity coverage, hallucination penalties, and negation-aware rewards; (3) a comprehensive evaluation framework including natural language generation (NLG) metrics, clinically grounded safety dimensions, temporal validation, cross-group generalisation, and independent physician review; and (4) a grounded reconstruction algorithm for generating high-coverage reference reports for evaluation.

## MATERIAL AND METHODS

### Dataset and ethics

This retrospective study used data from Molecular Imaging and Therapy, Ghaziabad, collected between December 2023 and March 2024, during which Author 1 (DP) was affiliated with the centre. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee of Amity University Uttar Pradesh, Noida, India (Approval No. AUUP/IEC/ SEPT/2024/15; dated 18 September 2024), and the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants for the use of their imaging and clinical data for research and publication. As data collection (December 2023-March 2024) preceded the date of formal ethics committee approval (18 September 2024), this study constitutes a retrospective analysis of previously acquired clinical imaging and report data. Although the imaging examinations and report generation occurred before the date of formal ethics committee approval, no data extraction, de-identification, or research analysis was undertaken until ethical approval had been obtained. No prospective data collection was performed under this approval. The informed consent obtained from participants covered the use of their imaging and report data for research and publication purposes, including secondary analyses such as the development of computational and language-model-based tools; consent was not obtained with explicit reference to “AI model development” as a named use, and this is acknowledged as a limitation. All data used for model development were de-identified prior to use.

The dataset comprised 170 patients: 85 with confirmed or suspected breast cancer demonstrating at least one fluorine-18 fluorodeoxyglucose (18 F-FDG)-avid breast lesion and undergoing whole-body 18 F-FDG PET-CT for staging, restaging, or treatment response assessment. 85 control patients were reported with no evidence of malignancy on imaging. This balanced design was specifically chosen to ensure that the model was trained on both pathological and normal appearances across all anatomical regions, mitigating region-level reporting bias. The target cohort size for this feasibility study was informed by, rather than statistically powered by, a standard proportion-based calculation:

$n = \frac{Z^{2} \times P \times \left(\right. 1 - P \left.\right)}{\text{d}_{2}} ,$
where Z = 1.96 for a 95% confidence level, _p_ = 0.30 (reported prevalence of metastatic breast cancer), and d = 0.10 (precision), yielding a minimum value of 80.5. This calculation, based on disease-prevalence estimation, does not constitute a formal statistical power calculation for language-model training or validation, for which no validated samplesize methodology currently exists; it was used only as a practical reference point for the target size of a balanced feasibility cohort. This was rounded up to 85 for practical convenience. Accordingly, 85 patients were included in the lesion-positive cohort, with an equal number of controls for balanced analysis. All examinations were performed on a General Electric (GE) Discovery IQ-Gen 2 PET-CT scanner using a standardised 18 F-FDG whole-body protocol.

No patients with Brain as a primary clinical target were excluded at the data collection level; however, the Brain region was excluded from primary quantitative evaluation due to the known high degree of templating in brain PET-CT reports (91.2% boilerplate overlap in this cohort), with results reported separately as supplementary material [[Supplementary material](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#supp1)].

Supplementary material

Structured PET-CT report text was extracted for each of five anatomical region-sections per patient: Brain, Head and Neck, Thorax, Abdomen and Pelvis, and Musculoskeletal, together with a whole-body report section. After applying quality filters (minimum section length 30 characters; minimum three report sections per patient), the final dataset comprised 836 section reports across the 170 patients. The dataset was partitioned using stratified patient-level splits into training (70%; 118 patients), validation (15%; 26 patients), and test sets (15%; 26 patients), stratified by clinical group (breast cancer/normal). Patient-level splitting ensured that no sections from any individual patient appeared in more than one split, preventing data leakage.

### Structured clinical context extraction

The primary model input was a structured clinical context extracted from the reference report text. The model receives this structured finding list together with explicit instructions to generate a coherent PET-CT report section while preserving laterality, quantitative measurements, SUVmax values, and negations, and then generates physician-style narrative prose. This input format is functionally analogous to the output of an upstream image-analysis system (e.g., lesion detection, segmentation, or quantitative PET-CT analysis); the task is therefore appropriately described as structured-finding-to-narrative report generation. An important limitation of the current study design is that the structured finding lists used for training and evaluation were extracted from reference report text rather than generated by an independent image-analysis model. Consequently, the structured inputs used for generation and the reference report text share a common source, introducing a dependency that limits the independence of the evaluation. This limitation is discussed further in discussion section, and full pipeline validation using independently image-derived structured findings is identified as a necessary direction for future work. The rich_context_to_prompt() function converted the structured context dictionary into a standardised prompt template comprising (i) structured bullet-point findings, (ii) fixed generation instructions requiring preservation of laterality, measurements, SUVmax values, and negations, and (iii) a report-generation trigger presented to the language model.

A rule-based extraction pipeline processed each section text using clause-level negation scope detection, dedicated primary-lesion spatial patterns (supporting laterality, quadrant, and morphology extraction), lymph node station classification with negation awareness, explicit negative finding catalogues, surgical and treatment history extraction, and FDG uptake pattern catalogues. Briefly, negation-scope detection used a rule-based clause-window approach: negation cue words (e.g., “no”, “without”, “absence of ”, “unremarkable”) were matched against a controlled list, and the scope of negation was extended to the end of the containing clause (delimited by conjunctions, commas, or sentence boundaries), such that entities falling within this span were tagged as negated. Laterality extraction used regular-expression patterns anchored to anatomical nouns (e.g., “right breast”, “left axillary”, “bilateral”), with priority given to laterality terms adjacent to the primary lesion site over laterality mentions in incidental or comparison contexts. SUVmax extraction used a numeric-pattern matcher (e.g., “SUVmax of 4.2”, “SUVmax: 4.2”) that bound each extracted value to the nearest preceding anatomical entity within the same clause. Explicit negative-finding catalogues comprised predefined phrase lists for each organ system (e.g., “no FDG-avid lesion”, “no significant uptake”, “normal appearance”) used to flag uninvolved structures. When no primary lesion, laterality, or SUVmax value could be confidently extracted for a section, the corresponding fields were left empty rather than imputed, and the downstream prompt was constructed from the remaining available fields (handled jointly with the field-dropout augmentation described in Method: Context augmentation section). Fallback report-level templates (Method: Inference and post-processing section) were applied only at the post-generation stage for low-faithfulness outputs and were not used during context extraction.

The master extractor (_extract\_rich\_context()_) generated a JSON-serializable context dictionary containing: primary lesion attributes (morphology, laterality, quadrant, size, SUVmax), lymph node findings, negative findings, surgical history, lung findings, skeletal findings, all quantitative SUVmax values, and all lesion measurements. An impression classifier derived a categorical finding impression (significant_ finding, no significant_finding, or indeterminate) from the structured context. For the physician review booklet, a parallel function (_reference\_to\_clinical\_context()_) converted the ground-truth reference text into a standardised, structured bullet-point format, preserving all SUVmax values inline with their anatomical sites, and serving as the Panel A clinical context shown to reviewing physicians.

### Prompt construction

Model input prompts were constructed using _rich\_context\_to\_ prompt()_, which derived clinical context bullets directly from the source section text, preserving lesion-location-SUV bindings. The prompt format comprised four sections: (1) REGION label, (2) Clinical Context block with bullet-point findings (one sentence per bullet), (3) an instructions block specifying faithfulness requirements, and (4) a generation trigger (_"Generate PET-CT report section:"_). The target during training was the verbatim cleaned section text. Maximum prompt length was capped at 450 tokens; maximum generation length at 512 tokens; maximum total sequence length at 1,024 tokens.

### Model architecture and fine-tuning

The base model was BioMedLM (stanford-crfm/BioMedLM), a 2.7-billion-parameter causal language model developed by the Stanford Centre for Research on Foundation Models (CRFM), pre-trained on PubMed (MEDLINE) abstracts and full-text biomedical literature.[[35](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#ref35)] BioMedLM is a GPT-2-architecture model with domain-specific pretraining that has demonstrated strong performance on biomedical question answering and clinical text tasks.[[35](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#ref35)] Parameter-efficient fine-tuning was implemented using Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)[[34](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#ref34)] applied to attention and feed-forward projection layers (target modules: c_attn, c_proj, c_fc), with rank r = 32, alpha = 64, and dropout = 0.05. The rank selection was validated through an ablation study (r ∈ {8, 16, 32, 64} × attention-only vs. full-module targets), confirming r = 32 full-target as the optimal trade-off between performance and parameter efficiency (ROUGE-L: r32_full = 0.443 vs r64_full = 0.457 on validation set).

Training hyperparameters were learning rate 1×10-4 with cosine decay, batch size 2 with 8 gradient accumulation steps (effective batch size 16), 30 epochs with early stopping (patience = 5 epochs), warmup ratio 0.06, and weight decay 0.01. Fine-tuning and validation were performed using Python, PyTorch, Hugging Face Transformers, and the Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) library on a single NVIDIA A40 graphics processing unit (GPU) with 48 GB of memory. GPU memory utilisation was not continuously logged during the original experiments; therefore, the exact peak GPU memory consumption could not be retrospectively recovered. All experiments were conducted using fp16 precision, with only the LoRA adapter parameters optimised while the BioMedLM base-model weights remained frozen. The cross-validation, cross-group, and temporal-validation experiments required 27.8 h of cumulative active training time across all LoRA training jobs. Final primary-model training and prediction generation using three independent random seeds required approximately 2.31 h of active training time per seed. Including all training, prediction-generation, and evaluation stages, the complete LoRA pipeline required approximately 4 days and 10.5 h of total wall-clock time.

### Faithfulness-augmented trainer

A custom training framework was developed by extending the Hugging Face Trainer to incorporate domain-specific auxiliary loss components, in addition to the standard cross-entropy negative log-likelihood loss (LNLL). Auxiliary objectives have been shown to improve sequence generation quality and control output properties.[[36](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#ref36 "Neural machine translation by jointly learning to align and translate"),[37](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#ref37 "Proceedings of the 55th Annual Meeting of the Association for Computational Linguistics (Volume 1: Long Papers)")]

*   (1) **Entity Coverage Loss (λcov = 0.3):** Penalises the model for failing to generate critical entity tokens (e.g., laterality, SUVmax values, and organ names) present in the clinical context. This is computed as the mean negative log-probability of target entity tokens.

*   (2) **Hallucination Penalty (λhall = 0.2):** Penalises the generation of numeric tokens do not present in the input context, specifically targeting fabricated SUVmax values and measurements.[[27](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#ref27 "Survey of hallucination in natural language generation")]

*   (3) **Negation Reward (λneg = 0.1):** Encourages the generation of tokens representing normal or negative findings (e.g., “no”, “unremarkable”, “normal”, “preserved”), promoting accurate reporting of uninvolved structures.[[38](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#ref38 "Learning to summarize from human feedback")]

The total training loss was defined as:

L total = L NLL + λ cov•L coverage + λ hall•L hallucination + λ neg•L negation

### Context augmentation

To reduce overfitting to fixed context field orderings and improve robustness to partially specified inputs, a context-augmented dataset was employed with two training-time augmentations. Input perturbation and corruption strategies have been shown to improve generalisation and robustness in neural text generation.[[39](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#ref39 "Proceedings of the 2019 Conference on Empirical Methods in Natural Language Processing and the 9th International Joint Conference on Natural Language Processing (EMNLPIJCNLP)"),[40](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#ref40 "Proceedings of the 58th Annual Meeting of the Association for Computational Linguistics")]

*   (1) Random field-order shuffling (_p_ = 0.30): applied within the structured context block to reduce positional bias.

*   (2) Random field dropout (_p_ = 0.10 per field): simulating missing or incomplete upstream extraction.

These augmentations reflect real-world deployment scenarios where clinical context inputs may be partially specified or exhibit variability in structure and phrasing.

### Inference and post-processing

Final model inference was performed using beam search with grounding-based reranking. Beam search and related decoding strategies are known to influence output diversity and quality in neural text generation.[[41](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#ref41 "The curious case of neural text degeneration"),[42](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#ref42 "Proceedings of the 56th Annual Meeting of the Association for Computational Linguistics (Volume 1: Long Papers)")] Candidate outputs (beam width = 4) were evaluated using a grounding function that assessed primary laterality alignment, SUVmax fidelity, preservation of negative findings, surgical history consistency, and overall clinical coherence. Reranking based on auxiliary scoring functions has been shown to improve generation quality beyond likelihood-based decoding.[[43](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#ref43 "Proceedings of the 2016 Conference of the North American Chapter of the Association for Computational Linguistics: Human Language Technologies")]

A beam-width ablation study on the validation set (beams ∈ {1, 2, 4, 6}) showed that greedy decoding (beam = 1) achieved the highest ROUGE-L (0.590) and laterality accuracy (0.200), while larger beam widths resulted in modest reductions in lexical metrics. Beam width = 4 (ROUGE-L = 0.551) was selected as the primary configuration to balance output diversity and clinical plausibility, despite variability in laterality metrics across beam settings.

A post-generation faithfulness filter (threshold = 0.60) was applied to identify outputs with low grounding scores. Post-hoc filtering is a common strategy for mitigating hallucinations in neural text generation. Outputs failing this threshold were replaced using context-derived fallback templates; 8/128 test outputs (6.3%) required this intervention. To separate the contribution of the language model from that of this rule-based correction step, held-out test set metrics are reported both before fallback replacement (raw model output for the 103 primary-region sections used for all headline metrics in this study, i.e. excluding the 25 Brain sections as per Method: Structured clinical context extraction section, and including the 8 low-faithfulness outputs) and after fallback replacement (the 8 flagged outputs replaced by templates, as used for the primary results in Results: Held-out test set section): ROUGE-L was 0.546 [0.514–0.577] before fallback versus 0.548 [0.517–0.578] after, and BERTScore-F1 was 0.911 [0.905–0.917] before versus 0.912 [0.907–0.918] after. These minimal differences indicate that the fallback mechanism's contribution to the reported performance metrics is negligible, confirming that the fine-tuned language model itself is responsible for the vast majority of the observed performance.

### Evaluation design

The evaluation framework comprised five complementary components:

*   (a) **Primary quantitative evaluation:** Five-fold patient-level stratified cross-validation on primary regions (excluding Brain), with 95% bootstrap confidence intervals (_N =_ 5,000 resamples; patient-clustered).[[44](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#ref44)] The primary outcome metric was ROUGE-L; secondary metrics included ROUGE-1, ROUGE-2[[45](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#ref45)], Metric for Evaluation of Translation with Explicit ORdering (METEOR)[[46](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#ref46)], BLEU[[47](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#ref47)], and BERTScore-F1.[[48](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#ref48)]

*   (b) **Held-out test set evaluation:** Evaluation on a fixed test set of 26 patients (up to 104 section-level comparisons across four primary regions), with Bonferroni-corrected Wilcoxon signed-rank tests (α = 0.05/20 = 0.0025) against all baselines. Effect sizes were reported as rank-biserial correlation (r).

*   (c) **Temporal split validation:** Model training and evaluation using chronological patient splits (trai _n =_ 579, validatio _n =_ 129, test = 128 sections) to assess temporal generalisability.

*   (d) **Cross-group generalisation:** Bidirectional cross-training (train on breast cancer, test on normal; and vice versa) to evaluate generalisation across clinical groups. Performance differences between same-group and cross-group settings were reported.

*   (e) **Clinical safety metrics:** Five domain-specific metrics were computed for the fine-tuned model on primary regions: (1) laterality accuracy (proportion of Thorax and Head & Neck cases with correct primary laterality); (2) clinical entity F1 score (overlap of anatomical and pathological entities); (3) negative finding preservation rate; (4) SUVmax faithfulness (agreement between generated and context-derived SUVmax values); and (5) hallucination rate. To avoid ambiguity, the following distinct quantities are reported and should not be conflated.

SUVmax faithfulness (metric 4) evaluates whether generated SUVmax values are supported by the source clinical context and is reported as a separate quantitative consistency measure. The numerical hallucination penalty used during training (Method: Faithfulness-augmented trainer section) operates at the token level, penalising the generation of numeric tokens absent from the input context.

The automated hallucination rate (metric 5, Results: Clinical safety metrics section) is defined at the level of an individual report section (i.e., a patient-region pair), not at the level of individual entities. Each report section is assigned a binary hallucination label using a rule-based classifier that combines multiple factual consistency signals. Specifically, a section is classified as a hallucination if predefined thresholds are exceeded, including excessive unsupported SUV generation, multiple hallucinated anatomical entities, or a low faithfulness score. Entity-level measures such as hallucinated organs and organ-level matching are therefore used as inputs to the classification process, but the reported hallucination rate is calculated as the proportion of report sections meeting the hallucination criteria among all evaluated sections.

Finally, the clinically unsafe hallucination count (Method: Physician review section) is a physician-assigned per-case rating (0–3) of clinically meaningful fabricated findings as judged by an expert reviewer with access to the reference report. This measure is most directly relevant to patient safety and was 0/3 for all 28 reviewed cases (Results: Physician review section). We therefore emphasise that SUVmax faithfulness, automated hallucination rate, and physician-assessed unsafe hallucinations quantify different aspects of generation quality and should not be interpreted as interchangeable measures.

### Physician review

Twenty-eight cases were selected using stratified sampling from the primary evaluation set, ensuring representation across all four primary anatomical regions and error categories (good, acceptable, poor, hallucination). A tri-panel blinded review booklet was prepared for each case: Panel A (structured clinical context derived from the reference), Panel B (grounded reconstruction for evaluation), and Panel C (ground-truth reference report). Panel B, the model output under review, was generated by the fine-tuned BioMedLM-LoRA model from the Panel A clinical context using the inference and post-processing pipeline described in Method: Inference and post-processing (including beam-search reranking and faithfulness-filter fallback where applicable); Panel C was the original, unmodified clinician-authored reference report for the same section. Physicians were provided with all three panels simultaneously. Panel A served as the source clinical context, Panel C served as the reference report, and Panel B was the model-generated report evaluated by the reviewers. Physicians scored Panel B across nine evaluation dimensions: six continuous scale dimensions (factual correctness 1-5, completeness 1-5, clinical usefulness 1-5, coherence 1-5, style fluency 1-5, and unsafe hallucination severity 0-3), two categorical ratings (overall acceptability A/B/C; laterality correctness Y/N/NA), and one count measure (sentence-level hallucination count). Review of the completed scoring sheets confirmed that both physicians assessed and recorded laterality ratings for all 28 reviewed cases across all four anatomical regions (Thorax, Head and Neck, Musculoskeletal, and Abdomen and Pelvis), with no NA designations used; laterality correctness was therefore evaluable for all 28 cases.

Two independent nuclear medicine physicians (Physician 1 and Physician 2) independently evaluated each case using the nine evaluation dimensions described above. The reviewers were blinded to each other's evaluations and scores throughout the review process. Inter-rater reliability was evaluated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC [[2](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#ref2 "PET/CT imaging in treatment planning and surveillance of sinonasal neoplasms"),[1](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#ref1 "Prognostic significance of FDG-PET/CT based radiomics analysis in newly diagnosed multiple myeloma: A comparative study with clinical assessment")]) for continuous variables and weighted Cohen's kappa (κ) for categorical outcomes, both standard measures of agreement in observer studies.[[49](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#ref49 "Intraclass correlations: Uses in assessing rater reliability"),[50](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#ref50 "A coefficient of agreement for nominal scales")]

### Baselines

Four comparison systems were evaluated: (1) Template baseline: region-specific normal-appearance templates without contextual adaptation; (2) term frequency–inverse document frequency (TF-IDF) retrieval: bigram TF-IDF– based retrieval of the most similar training report[[51](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#ref51 "A statistical interpretation of term specificity and its application in retrieval")]; (3) Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) hybrid: retrieval-augmented generation combining retrieved context with BioMedLM-based generation[[52](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#ref52 "Retrieval-augmented generation for knowledge-intensive NLP tasks")]; and (4) Vanilla BioMedLM: non-fine-tuned BioMedLM used in a zero-shot setting. GPT-4 was evaluated in zero-shot and two-shot configurations as an upper-bound reference model where applicable.[[53](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#ref53 "GPT-4 technical report")] Due to limited control over deterministic decoding and variability across repeated API calls, a consistent full metric evaluation (BLEU, METEOR, BERTScore) was not feasible; therefore, comparison was restricted to ROUGE-L as the primary metric.

### Implementation details

All experiments were implemented in Python 3.10 using PyTorch ≥2.0,[[54](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#ref54)] Hugging Face Transformers ≥4.46,[[55](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#ref55 "Proceedings of the 2020 Conference on Empirical Methods in Natural Language Processing: System Demonstrations")] and the Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) library for Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA). BERTScore was computed using the roberta-large backbone.[[48](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#ref48)] ROUGE scores were calculated using the rouge-score library, and METEOR was computed using the Natural Language Toolkit (NLTK). Training was performed with gradient checkpointing enabled to reduce GPU memory requirements. Reproducibility was ensured through three-seed training (seeds: 42, 123, 456), with results reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) across seeds. All sources of randomness were controlled by setting seeds at the Python, NumPy, and PyTorch levels.

## RESULTS

### Dataset characteristics

The final dataset comprised 170 patients (85 with breast cancer and 85 controls), yielding 836 structured section-level reports. The training, validation, and test splits included 118, 26, and 26 patients, respectively, with balanced group representation (59 breast cancer and 59 controls in training; 13 and 13 in both validation and test sets). Brain reports exhibited the highest degree of templating (91.2% boilerplate overlap) and were excluded from primary evaluation according to the pre-specified protocol. Musculoskeletal reports demonstrated moderate templating (42.4%). Temporal coverage was verified using a scan_dates.csv file containing 170 entries spanning the acquisition period, enabling chronological validation splits (579 training, 129 validations, and 128 test sections).

### Lora rank ablation

The LoRA ablation [[Table 1](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#T1)] demonstrated that rank configuration affects performance. Attention-only targets (c_attn) at r = 8 and r = 16 yielded ROUGE-L scores of 0.081 and 0.077, respectively. Full-module targets (c_attn + c_proj + c_fc) showed a monotonic increase in ROUGE-L with increasing rank: r = 16 achieved 0.343, r = 32 achieved 0.443, and r = 64 achieved 0.457, a marginal gain of +0.014 from r = 32 to r = 64. Rank r = 32 (full-module) was selected as the primary configuration, achieving 96.9% of the r = 64 ROUGE-L while using fewer trainable parameters. An interpretation of these findings, including the implications of attention-only versus full-module targeting, is provided in Discussion section. Beyond the LoRA rank/target-module ablation, an ablation of individual structured-context fields (clinical history, prior imaging, laboratory values) was not performed in this study; this is noted as a limitation in Discussion section. and the relative contribution of individual context fields to report quality is identified as a direction for future work.

### Multi-seed stability

Three-seed training (seeds 42, 123, 456) demonstrated reproducible performance on primary regions: ROUGE-L 0.550 ± 0.016 (per-seed: 0.546, 0.533, 0.572), METEOR 0.527 ± 0.032, and BERTScore-F1 0.911 ± 0.002 [[Fig 3](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#F3)]. The low standard deviation across seeds indicates stable model performance that is not dependent on random initialisation. Faithfulness filtering outcomes [[Fig 3](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#F3)] further demonstrated consistent grounding behaviour, with 120/128 (93.8%) outputs meeting the threshold (≥0.60) without requiring fallback, supporting the robustness of the generation pipeline.

### Beam search ablation (decoding strategy)

Beam-width ablation on the validation set [[Fig 4](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#F4)] evaluated the impact of decoding strategy on text quality (ROUGE-L) and clinical correctness (laterality accuracy). Greedy decoding (beam = 1) achieved the highest ROUGE-L (~0.590) and laterality accuracy (~0.200), indicating that deterministic decoding preserves lexical fidelity and reduces side-assignment errors. Relative to greedy decoding (beam = 1; ROUGE-L: 0.590), larger beam widths produced lower ROUGE-L scores overall (beam = 2: 0.543; beam = 4: 0.551; beam = 6: 0.549), with modest variation among the non-greedy settings. Laterality performance also varied across beam widths, with a marked drop at beam = 4, followed by partial recovery at beam = 6. Despite achieving the highest ROUGE-L, greedy decoding (beam = 1) produced repetitive and less diverse outputs. Beam = 4 was selected as the primary decoding configuration based on validation-set analysis to balance lexical quality and output diversity, reflecting practical reporting requirements beyond single-metric optimisation. These results suggest that wider beam search does not improve factual correctness in this domain and may introduce variability in structured attributes such as laterality. As ROUGE-L captures lexical overlap but not narrative structure or diversity, decoding strategy selection was guided by both quantitative metrics and qualitative assessment of report coherence.

### Statistical testing

Bonferroni-corrected Wilcoxon signed-rank tests (α_adjusted = 0.0025) demonstrated statistically significant differences across all pairwise model comparisons. BioMedLM-LoRA consistently outperformed the Template Baseline across all evaluated metrics, with substantial improvements in lexical and semantic similarity (Δ_ROUGE-L = +0.249, r = 0.797; Δ_METEOR = +0.344, r = 0.868; all _p_< 10-10). Similarly, comparisons with the RAG Hybrid model showed clear and statistically robust gains (Δ_ROUGE-L = +0.376, r = 0.822; _p_< 10-10), indicating improved contextual integration and report generation fidelity. In contrast, TF-IDF Retrieval achieved higher ROUGE-L scores (Δ_ROUGE-L = −0.269, r = 0.865; _p_< 10-10), reflecting its inherent advantage in maximising lexical overlap within a closed, same-site corpus. This behaviour is expected for retrieval-based methods, which select pre-existing reports with high surface-level similarity rather than generating outputs conditioned on structured clinical context. Consequently, while TF-IDF demonstrates superior performance on lexical similarity metrics, this does not necessarily translate to improved clinical fidelity, contextual grounding, or generalisation. These aspects are more directly captured by the generative modelling approach and are discussed further in Results: Physician review section. Effect sizes for key comparisons are summarised in [Fig 5](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#F5).

### Region-level performance

Performance varied systematically by anatomical region [[Fig 6](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#F6)]. Brain reports achieved the highest scores (ROUGE-L ≈ 0.940), reflecting the highly standardised and template-driven nature of reporting in this region. Among primary regions, Abdomen and Pelvis (ROUGE-L ≈ 0.660) and Head and Neck (≈ 0.560) demonstrated stronger performance compared to Thorax (≈ 0.410), the latter reflecting greater clinical complexity and heterogeneity in breast oncology PET-CT reports. Musculoskeletal performance (≈ 0.580) was comparable to Head and Neck. Within-region analysis showed consistent patterns across clinical groups (breast cancer vs. normal), with group differences most pronounced in Thorax, where breast cancer cases exhibit substantially greater variability than surveillance or normal studies.

Hallucination analysis, defined as proxy-flagged SUV over-generation rate, revealed that Abdomen and Pelvis normal cases showed the highest rates (∼42%). This was primarily attributable to lexical divergence in describing physiological uptake patterns rather than the introduction of clinically incorrect findings. In contrast, Thorax breast cancer cases showed lower hallucination rates (∼8%), suggesting improved grounding when richer and more specific contextual signals are present.

### Temporal split and cross-group validation

Temporal split validation (chronological partition: 579 train, 129 val, 128 test sections) yielded ROUGE-L = 0.687 [0.649-0.726] and METEOR = 0.630 [0.586-0.676] [[Fig 2](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#F2)], closely matching cross-validation performance (Δ_ROUGE-L = −0.005), confirming temporal stability and absence of scan-date-dependent performance drift. The five-fold cross-validation (Results: Five-fold cross-validation section, ROUGE-L 0.692 ± 0.034) and the temporal split (ROUGE-L 0.687) both used the same model configuration, decoding settings (beam = 4), and post-processing pipeline (including the faithfulness filter and fallback templates of Method: Inference and post-processing section), evaluated across multiple folds or a single chronological split of comparable size. The held-out test set result reported in Results: Held-out test set section (ROUGE-L = 0.545) corresponds to the single, pre-specified 26-patient held-out test partition used for baseline comparison, identical in size to one cross-validation fold but representing one specific patient split rather than an average across splits; the lower value on this particular partition, relative to the cross-validation mean, reflects sampling variability in case difficulty across the 26 held-out patients (consistent with the fold-to-fold range of 0.654–0.730 observed in cross-validation) rather than a methodological difference, as the model configuration, decoding settings, and post-processing steps were identical across all three evaluations.

Cross-group evaluation [[Fig 8](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#F8)] demonstrated a generalisation delta of Δ_ROUGE-L = 0.142 (same-group CV mean 0.692 vs. cross-group mean 0.550). The asymmetry between conditions was expected: training on normal/testing on breast_cancer (ROUGE-L = 0.458) was harder than the reverse (training on breast_cancer/testing on normal: ROUGE-L = 0.642). This asymmetry likely reflects that training exclusively on templated, normal-appearing reports provides limited exposure to the complex oncological language required to generalise to breast cancer test cases, whereas training on breast cancer cases exposes the model to both pathological and normal-appearing findings, supporting easier generalisation to normal-only test cases. The modest delta (0.142) supports the feasibility of cross-group generalisation, provided some domain overlap is present in training data.

### Physician review

Twenty-eight cases across all primary regions and error categories were independently evaluated by two nuclear medicine physicians. ### Error distribution and memorisation analysis

The detailed error category distribution [[Fig 10](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#F10)] showed that the largest proportion of primary-region outputs were classified as “good” (_n =_ 35), followed by laterality_wrong (_n =_ 20), acceptable (_n =_ 19), hallucination_proxy (_n =_ 14), missing_laterality (_n =_ 13), and poor (_n =_ 2). The prominence of laterality-related categories (laterality_wrong and missing_ laterality: combined _n =_ 33) indicates that side-attribution is the most frequent source of discrepancy in automated evaluation, rather than general clinical inaccuracy.

Eight-gram character-level overlap analysis yielded a mean memorisation score of 0.659 (max = 1.000; cases with overlap > 0.80: _n =_ 51). Higher overlap was predominantly observed in regions with standardised reporting patterns, including Brain (_n =_ 25), Musculoskeletal (_n =_ 14), Head and Neck (_n =_ 6), and Abdomen and Pelvis (_n =_ 6). These findings likely reflect the reuse of domain-standard clinical phrasing (e.g., “No abnormal FDG uptake noted in axial and visualised appendicular skeleton”), which is expected in structured radiology reporting. Given the use of patient-level data splitting, with no overlap between training and test patients, the observed similarity is consistent with the reuse of conventional reporting language rather than obvious evidence of patient-specific memorisation. However, a formal nearest-neighbour analysis comparing generated test outputs against the full training corpus, or a de-templated evaluation in which standardised boilerplate phrases are masked prior to scoring, was not performed in this study. In a small, single-institution dataset with a constrained vocabulary of standard reporting phrases, high n-gram overlap could in principle reflect either appropriate reuse of conventional clinical language or a degree of template memorisation that patient-level splitting alone cannot fully exclude; the two explanations cannot be definitively distinguished without such analyses. This is noted as a limitation in Discussion section, and nearest-neighbour and de-template analyses are identified as priorities for future work.

## DISCUSSION

This study presents a systematic evaluation of parameter-efficient fine-tuning of a biomedical language model for multi-region PET-CT report generation, incorporating clinically grounded safety metrics and independent physician validation. The proposed framework may serve as a downstream interface for AI systems performing SUV quantification and tumour staging, facilitating the clinical integration of quantitative imaging outputs.

The principal finding is that BioMedLM with LoRA fine-tuning, trained on structured clinical context inputs and optimised using faithfulness-augmented loss functions, generates section-level PET-CT report narratives from structured clinical finding lists, with high physician-rated factual correctness (mean 4.59/5) and 100% laterality correctness across all 28 physician-reviewed cases. No clinically unsafe hallucinations or sentence-level hallucinations were identified by either physician in the reviewed subset (one-sided 95% CI: 0–10.7%). These findings should be interpreted as evidence of feasibility within a limited, single-institution, physician-reviewed sample rather than as a demonstration of general clinical safety. An important limitation of the study design is that the structured finding lists used as model inputs were extracted from reference report text rather than generated by an independent image-analysis model. Consequently, the structured inputs and reference outputs share a common source, limiting the independence of the evaluation and preventing direct extrapolation to a fully automated clinical pipeline. While the combined Grade A acceptability rate was 55.4% (Physician 1: 53.6%; Physician 2: 57.1%), indicating that approximately 45% of the reviewed cases required physician revision, the system demonstrated reasonable factual grounding in this feasibility setting. These findings support further investigation of structured-finding-to-narrative PET-CT report generation as a downstream component of clinical AI pipelines, with physician supervision remaining essential.

The discrepancy between automated laterality proxy accuracy (0.271) and physician-assessed laterality accuracy (1.000) warrants careful interpretation. The automated metric requires that laterality expressions in generated outputs conform to predefined regular expression patterns linked to primary lesion contexts. Consequently, 20 cases were classified as ‘wrong side’ by the automated metric; however, physician review indicated that these instances primarily reflected laterality references in non-primary or distributed anatomical contexts, rather than true clinical inversion. This highlights an important methodological limitation: automated laterality proxies designed for structured field matching may underestimate clinical correctness when applied to free-text generation. These findings support the inclusion of expert human evaluation as a complementary component for assessing clinical safety in generative reporting systems.

The LoRA rank/target-module ablation (Results: LoRA rank ablation section) showed that full-module adaptation (attention plus feed-forward projection layers: c_attn, c_proj, c_fc) substantially outperformed attention-only adaptation (c_attn alone) at equivalent rank, with attention-only configurations achieving ROUGE-L of only 0.081 (r = 8) and 0.077 (r = 16) compared with 0.343-0.457 for full-module configurations. These results highlight the importance of adapting both attention and feed-forward components for effective domain-specific language generation: attention layers alone appear insufficient to capture the lexical and factual adaptation required for PET-CT-specific reporting, whereas feed-forward projection layers likely encode much of the domain-specific vocabulary and factual associations that must be updated for this task. This finding is consistent with reports that feed-forward layers in transformer architectures store a substantial share of factual and lexical knowledge and supports prioritising full-module LoRA targeting over attention-only adaptation for clinical text-generation tasks of this kind.

The superior performance of TF-IDF retrieval (ROUGE-L = 0.814) compared with the proposed model (0.545) on lexical metrics is consistent with known properties of retrieval-based systems in small, single-institution datasets. In such settings, highly similar reports often exist for new cases, allowing retrieval to achieve strong lexical overlap with reference texts. However, retrieval approaches have fundamental limitations: they cannot generalise to novel clinical presentations, synthesise findings from incomplete or structured inputs, or provide explicit mechanisms for verifying factual consistency. Moreover, their performance typically degrades in cross-institution settings where similar reference cases are unavailable.

The comparatively lower performance of the RAG hybrid model (ROUGE-L = 0.170) relative to the template baseline (0.296) likely reflects limitations in retrieval quality within this small, single-institution corpus. Specifically, the RAG hybrid retrieved a single nearest-neighbour training report section using the same TF-IDF similarity ranking as the TFIDF Retrieval baseline and prepended it to the model prompt as a labelled “reference example (similar case)”, followed by the target case’s structured clinical context and generation trigger. This augmented prompt was then provided to the non-fine-tuned (vanilla) BioMedLM model for generation. Because the underlying generator was the vanilla BioMedLM model (the same model used for the Vanilla BioMedLM baseline, ROUGE-L = 0.073) rather than the LoRA-fine-tuned model, the retrieved example may not have been effectively integrated with the target case context and may have functioned primarily as additional in-context text. In such settings, retrieved contexts may be weakly matched or introduce irrelevant content, leading to degraded generation quality when combined with a generative model. This highlights a known sensitivity of RAG-based approaches to retrieval accuracy, particularly in limited or homogeneous datasets .

In contrast, the proposed generative framework demonstrated cross-group generalisation (ΔROUGE-L = 0.142) and temporal stability (ROUGE-L = 0.687 on temporal split), while providing quantifiable faithfulness measures (e.g., SUVmax precisio _n =_ 1.000). These properties highlight a key advantage of generative approaches in enabling clinically grounded, adaptable reporting beyond lexical similarity.

The Faithfulness-Augmented Trainer, incorporating entity coverage, hallucination penalties, and negation-aware reward signals, extends standard cross-entropy fine-tuning for clinical text generation by explicitly optimising for factual grounding. The observed SUVmax faithfulness (1.000 ± 0.000) indicates that no unsupported quantitative metabolic values were generated in this evaluation, addressing a critical risk in PET-CT reporting related to the fabrication of numerical measurements . This framework may be adaptable to other imaging domains involving quantitative parameters, such as apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) values in MRI or tracer activity estimation in dosimetry, although further multi-institutional and multi-modality validation is required. Additionally, the current ablation study was limited to LoRA rank and target-module configurations; the relative contribution of individual structured context fields (e.g., lesion localisation vs SUVmax values vs negative findings) to downstream report quality was not systematically evaluated and represents a priority for future investigation.

In comparison with prior work, most studies in automated radiology report generation have focused on chest radiography, where models trained on datasets such as CheXpert have demonstrated strong performance on selected findings.[[56](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#ref56 "Evaluating progress in automatic chest X-ray radiology report generation")] In contrast, PET-CT reporting presents substantially greater complexity: each examination comprises multiple anatomical regions with distinct vocabularies and structural patterns; quantitative parameters must be faithfully reproduced; and the binary classification paradigm typical of chest radiography does not readily translate to the multi-scale, multi-region metabolic characterisation required in oncological PET-CT.[[29](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#ref29 "Proceedings of the AAAI Conference on Artificial Intelligence"),[30](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#ref30 "Vision-language models for automated 3D PET/CT report generation")]

The physician acceptance rate (Grade A) was 53.6% for Physician 1 and 57.1% for Physician 2, with a combined rate of 55.4%. While lower than ideal, these values are comparable to those reported in early-stage clinical text generation systems, where physician acceptability prior to iterative refinement has shown moderate variability across early-stage systems. Review of the non-Grade-A cases indicated that the primary driver of the non-Grade-A cases was narrative structuring and ordering rather than factual content: this is consistent with the lower mean scores for coherence (3.88/5) and style fluency (3.79/5) relative to factual correctness (4.59/5) and completeness (4.77/5) reported below, and reflects the fact that the training objective (Methods: Faithfulness-augmented trainer section) explicitly rewards entity coverage, faithfulness, and negation accuracy but does not directly optimise for narrative ordering or stylistic fluency. We consider this an expected consequence of the current training and decoding configuration rather than an unexplained finding, and a tractable target for improvement (e.g., via supervised fine-tuning on physician-edited outputs) without compromising factual grounding.[[57](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#ref57 "Diagnostic accuracy and clinical value of a domain-specific multimodal generative AI model for chest radiograph report generation"),[58](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#ref58 "Radiologist interaction with artificial intelligence-generated preliminary reports: A longitudinal multireader study")] Importantly, no clinically unsafe hallucinations were identified during physician review, and the majority of non-Grade A cases were attributable to limitations in report structuring and narrative coherence rather than factual inaccuracies.[[59](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#ref59 "Feasibility and acceptability of ChatGPT-generated radiology report summaries for cancer patients"),[60](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#ref60 "Large language models for simplifying radiology reports: A systematic review and meta-analysis of patient, public, and clinician evaluations")]

The combined Grade A acceptability rate of 55.4% indicates that more than half of the generated reports were considered clinically acceptable with only minor edits. At the same time, it also means that 44.6% of reports required further revision before they would be suitable for clinical use, highlighting that the current system should be viewed as a physician-assistance tool rather than an autonomous reporting system. Whether this level of performance translates into meaningful time savings in routine clinical practice remains uncertain because physician editing time was not measured. Reports requiring relatively small revisions may still reduce reporting workload compared with generating reports from scratch, but this requires prospective evaluation. Future studies should therefore compare model-assisted editing with conventional physician dictation while directly measuring reporting time and editing effort. We also could not compare the observed acceptability rate with conventional dictation or transcription error rates because no matched benchmark was available in our cohort. Published error rates vary considerably across imaging modalities, reporting systems, and clinical settings, making direct comparisons difficult. A meaningful comparison would require evaluation using the same case mix and review criteria and should therefore be addressed in future studies. Compared with previous PET-CT and radiology report-generation studies[[24](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#ref24 "ConTEXTual Net 3D: Vision-language modeling in PET/CT for visual grounding of positive findings"),[29](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#ref29 "Proceedings of the AAAI Conference on Artificial Intelligence")–[31](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#ref31)], our framework extends evaluation beyond conventional language-generation metrics by incorporating clinically relevant safety measures, including SUVmax consistency, laterality assessment, hallucination detection, negation preservation, and independent physician review. Although previous studies have reported lexical similarity metrics such as ROUGE and BLEU, and some have incorporated task-specific grounding or benchmark evaluations, a comprehensive physician-adjudicated safety evaluation remains limited. Our framework, therefore, also evaluates whether clinically important findings and quantitative information are faithfully preserved. We consider this integrated clinical evaluation strategy, rather than any single language-generation metric, to be the principal methodological contribution of the present work.

Coherence (3.88/5 combined) and style fluency (3.79/5 combined) were rated lower than factual correctness and completeness, supporting the observation that physician revisions were driven primarily by report organisation and narrative flow rather than factual inaccuracies.[[29](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#ref29 "Proceedings of the AAAI Conference on Artificial Intelligence")] Such shortcomings represent a tractable problem: improvements in fluency and report structuring may be achieved through supervised fine-tuning on physician-edited outputs or by leveraging instruction-tuned models, without compromising the factual grounding observed in this limited feasibility study.

## LIMITATIONS

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the dataset is relatively small (170 patients) and derived from a single institution; external validation at independent nuclear medicine centres is required prior to broader clinical adoption.[[61](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#ref61 "A scoping review of large language model-based approaches for information extraction from radiology reports"),[62](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#ref62 "External validation of deep learning algorithms for radiologic diagnosis: A systematic review")] Second, the automated laterality metric substantially underestimated clinical correctness, as demonstrated by discordance with physician assessment. This highlights the need for more robust evaluation frameworks tailored to free-text clinical generation. Third, the Brain region was excluded from primary evaluation due to its highly templated nature; performance in less structured or more heterogeneous reporting contexts requires further investigation. Fourth, the pre-training corpus of BioMedLM (PubMed literature) differs from clinical reporting language, and future work should explore domain-adaptive pretraining on de-identified nuclear medicine reports.[[63](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#ref63 "Large language models in radiology reporting: A systematic review of performance, limitations, and clinical implications")] Fifth, the physician review was conducted under a context-conditioned generation framework, wherein model outputs were generated based on provided clinical findings. This reflects the intended design of the system, where report generation is driven by clinically relevant input context. While this approach ensures faithful representation of input findings, it may not fully capture the fluency characteristics of a fully integrated endto-end pipeline combining automated context extraction and report generation. Finally, moderate inter-rater agreement (κ = 0.61) and good-to-excellent ICC values across continuous scoring dimensions (0.70 - 0.84) indicate meaningful and reproducible inter-rater reliability, with residual variability in subjective clinical assessment, particularly for borderline cases involving report coherence and acceptability.[[63](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#ref63 "Large language models in radiology reporting: A systematic review of performance, limitations, and clinical implications"),[64](https://ijnm.co.in/context-grounded-pet-ct-report-generation-using-lora-fine-tuned-biomedlm-with-physician-validation-and-safety-evaluation/#ref64 "Prompting large language models and evaluating inter-and intra-rater agreement for cancer progression assessment from radiology reports")] Sixth, the dataset was drawn from a single institution, a single PET-CT scanner model, a single tracer (18 F-FDG), and predominantly breast cancer and normal-control cases; the findings reported here should not be assumed to generalise to other tracers (e.g., PSMA, DOTATATE), cancer types, institutions, scanner platforms, or institution-specific reporting styles without dedicated external validation. Seventh, although the present analysis manually reviewed a stratified 28-case sample including representation from each automated error category, not all 33 laterality-flagged cases and all 14 hallucination-proxy-flagged cases in the full 120-section test set underwent individual physician adjudication; extending manual review to all laterality-flagged and hallucination-proxy-flagged cases would further strengthen confidence in the safety characterisation and is identified as a priority for subsequent work. Eighth, the ablation analysis presented in Results: LoRA rank ablation section was limited to LoRA hyperparameter configurations (rank and target-module selection) and did not evaluate the contribution of individual structured context fields to report quality. The structured context used for model training included lesion localisation, laterality, SUVmax values, morphology, lymph node findings, negative findings, and related PET-CT observations; clinical history fields, prior imaging reports, and laboratory values were not incorporated as independent model inputs and were therefore not available for per-field ablation analysis. Consequently, the independent contribution of each context component to factual correctness, completeness, and clinical usefulness cannot be determined from the current study. A systematic per-field context ablation, selectively omitting or masking individual input fields and measuring the resulting change in report quality, is identified as an important direction for future work.

### Code Availability:

The PET-CT-specific context extraction, prompt construction, faithfulness-augmented training, and evaluation pipeline described in this manuscript was developed in-house by the authors for this study, building on the publicly available BioMedLM model, PyTorch, Hugging Face Transformers, and PEFT/LoRA libraries. The custom code (context extraction functions, prompt templates, faithfulness-augmented trainer, fallback-template logic, and evaluation scripts) is not currently in a publicly hosted repository but will be made available by the corresponding authors on reasonable request, subject to institutional approval, and the authors intend to deposit a de-identified version of the pipeline in a public code repository upon acceptance.

## CONCLUSION

This feasibility study demonstrates that parameter-efficiently fine-tuned biomedical language models can generate coherent, physician-style PET-CT report sections from structured clinical finding lists, with high physician-assessed factual correctness (mean 4.59/5) and, across all 28 reviewed cases, 100% laterality correctness under expert review, with zero unsafe hallucinations and zero sentence-level hallucinations identified by either reviewer (one-sided 95% CI: 0–10.7%). These findings should be regarded as evidence of feasibility in a limited, single-institution, physician-reviewed sample rather than a demonstration of general clinical safety. An important limitation of the current study design is that the structured finding lists used as model inputs were extracted from reference report text rather than from a live, independent image-analysis model; full pipeline independence using upstream image-derived context is a necessary step for future validation. The Faithfulness-Augmented Trainer framework and domain-specific evaluation provide a reproducible methodology for structured-finding-to-narrative clinical text generation that may inform future report-generation pipelines. While the combined Grade A acceptability of 55.4% (P1: 53.6%, P2: 57.1%) indicates that approximately 45% of reviewed cases required physician revision, the system showed reasonable factual grounding in this feasibility setting. These findings support further exploration of structured-finding-to-narrative PET-CT report generation as a downstream text-generation component in clinical AI pipelines, always with physician review. Because not all automatically flagged laterality-flagged (33/120) and hallucination-proxy-flagged (14/120) cases underwent individual physician adjudication, these safety findings should be regarded as supportive rather than conclusive evidence of clinical safety. This remains an early-stage feasibility study, and the system is not ready for autonomous or unsupervised clinical PET-CT reporting; physician review of every generated report remains essential prior to any clinical use. Larger, external, multi-institutional validation using independently extracted, image-derived context is required before any consideration of clinical decision-support deployment in high-volume PET-CT reporting workflows.
