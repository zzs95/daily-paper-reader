# Abstract 

Vision-language models (VLMs) are increasingly applied to medical imaging, yet public benchmarks may reward memorization over perception: their images and questions can enter pretraining corpora, and many items remain answerable from question text alone. We present an automated, agent-driven pipeline that builds multiple-choice benchmarks directly from paired private radiology reports and three-dimensional oncology imaging. It generates two complementary question types: schema-driven items populated deterministically from established reporting frameworks, and report-derived items veri fi ed against the source text. Because the source reports are private and single-institution, the resulting questions cannot have entered any model’s pretraining data, controlling instance-level contamination by construction. We applied the pipeline to four oncology cohorts (liver CT, liver MRI, lung CT and brain MRI; 2,509 cases and 33,870 questions) and evaluated  fi ve contemporary VLMs zero-shot. Accuracies ranged from 0.28 to 0.81 and no model was reliable across cohorts. Replacing the image with a blank input changed accuracy little for the highest-scoring models, and on schema-driven brain questions frontier models scored 0.17–0.19 higher on public than on matched private imaging. The benchmark thus separates image-dependent performance from question-text and dataset-familiarity effects, and the released pipeline lets institutions regenerate it on their own reports and images. 

# Introduction 

Vision-language models (VLMs) are increasingly proposed for clinical imaging, yet whether they can be trusted to read oncology studies remains poorly characterized. To be considered trustworthy, an evaluation must be clinically relevant, statistically robust, and free from confounding variables. Current medical visual question-answering (VQA) benchmarks rarely combine all three of these essential qualities. Manually annotated datasets (VQA-RAD 1, PathVQA 2, SLAKE 3) are clinically curated but small, whereas large web- or literature-sourced sets (OmniMedVQA 4, PMC-VQA 5) scale up but enter VLM pretraining corpora, so high scores may re fl ect memorization rather than perception. Domain-adapted biomedical VLMs, including Med-PaLM 6 and LLaVA-Med 7, established the potential of domain-speci fi cmultimodal adaptation. MIRAGE 8 recently showed that VLMs answer public medical VQA with little accuracy loss when the image is removed, implicating language priors and data leakage. Oncology imaging compounds the di ffi culty: studies are volumetric, so a 2D-input VLM must answer questions using information distributed across multiple slices of an imaging acquisition, which radiologists interpret by reviewing slices and sequences collectively, and every case is a con fi rmed cancer patient, shifting questions from “is there an abnormality” to lesion characterization, disease extent, and treatment-relevant features. Our objective in this work was therefore not to construct an exhaustive model leaderboard, but to determine whether a renewable benchmark generated from private reports can distinguish image-dependent performance from question-text and dataset-familiarity effects.

# An automated, contamination-controlled benchmark 

To evaluate VLMs under these constraints, we built an automated, agent-driven pipeline that generates multiple-choice VQA directly from paired private radiology reports and 3D images (Fig.  1). It produces two complementary question types. RADS-style questions are populated deterministically from a clinician-reviewed schema of visual  fi ndings grounded in established reporting frameworks (LI-RADS 9,Lung-RADS 10 , and a RANO-aligned brain schema 11 ), with limited language-model calls at generation time and therefore standardized. Report-derived questions are generated by a language model from the radiologist’s  fi ndings and impression, then  fi ltered by a report-grounded check that retains only questions whose answer is explicitly supported by the source text. By utilizing private, single-institution source reports, we guarantee that the generated questions remain isolated from any model's pretraining data, inherently preventing instance-level contamination. Furthermore, the pipeline is highly automated, requiring human intervention only for schema and template approval. Full details are in Methods. 

# Zero-shot accuracy across four oncology cohorts 

We applied the pipeline to four oncology cohorts: liver CT, liver MRI, lung CT, and brain MRI, and evaluated  fi ve contemporary VLMs zero-shot. Selected from systems available through commercial APIs or open-weight releases, the panel spans both general-purpose and medically adapted model classes: two frontier closed models (Claude Opus 4.6 and GPT-5.2), one open-weights frontier model (Qwen3-VL-30B), and two purpose-built medical specialists at different scales (MedGemma1.0-27B, MedGemma1.5-4B) 12 . Models received only the image, question, and options, never the report. No model is reliable across cohorts, and none dominates (Table  1): accuracies range from near the random baseline to 0.81, and the ordering of models differs from cohort to cohort. The medical specialist MedGemma1.0-27B has the highest point accuracy in both liver CT cells, Qwen3-VL-30B the highest on lung RADS-style, and Claude Opus 4.6 the highest on liver MRI, lung report-derived, and both brain cells, reaching 0.81 on brain report-derived. Yet that same specialist reaches only 0.47 on brain, a lower accuracy than every general-purpose model, and on liver MRI Qwen3-VL-30B does not exceed its random baseline (0.295 ± 0.016 vs baseline 0.291), consistent with limited transfer of current 2D-VLM pretraining to contrast-rich, multi-sequence MRI. 

# Cohort di ffi culty varies by model class 

Cohort di ffi culty splits by model class. Brain MRI yields the highest accuracies for the frontier models: Claude Opus 4.6 and GPT-5.2 exceed 0.75 on report-derived questions, but the medical specialist MedGemma1.0-27B scores below 0.47 there, lower than its liver CT accuracy, showing that performance varies substantially across imaging cohorts even within a medically adapted model. Liver MRI has the lowest mean accuracy of any cohort: clinical reading integrates information across multiple sequences, which the model must reconcile from separate composite images rather than from a co-registered

volume. Liver CT is hard for a different reason, intrinsically low lesion-to-parenchyma contrast that demands  fi ne-grained discrimination even within one slice. The effect of question type varies with model class rather than being uniform: the frontier models score higher on report-derived items, the MedGemma specialists show no consistent preference, and Qwen3-VL-30B is the only model whose accuracy is higher on RADS-style than on report-derived, doing so on the liver and lung cohorts but not on brain MRI. 

# Accuracy when the image is removed 

A benchmark score is meaningful only if it re fl ects image reading. To separate visual evidence from language-prior shortcuts, we re-ran one model from each class, the two closed frontier models (Claude Opus 4.6, GPT-5.2) and the larger medical specialist (MedGemma1.0-27B), with the image replaced by a blank input, scoring each model on the question set shared by its sighted and blind runs (Table  2). The sighted-minus-blind drop Δ depends on both model and cohort. The largest sighted-minus-blind differences occur in only a few cells: MedGemma1.0-27B loses 0.29 on liver CT report-derived and GPT-5.2 loses 0.12–0.16 on liver MRI. The highest-scoring model also changes little after image removal: Claude Opus 4.6’s Δ falls entirely within the pre-speci fi ed ± 0.05 equivalence margin on every cohort, including liver (liver CT Δ = -0.07 and + 0.01; liver MRI Δ ≈ 0), showing that its performance changes little when visual input is removed. Lung CT accuracy is essentially unchanged when the image is removed for both frontier models (GPT-5.2 Δ = -0.016 and − 0.031; Claude even − 0.16 on RADS-style, blind 0.73 versus sighted 0.56), a pattern compatible with substantial question-text signal; this analysis cannot determine whether that signal arises from pretraining exposure or other text-side cues. Thus, a high benchmark score can coexist with little change after image removal. 

# Public versus private brain imaging under matched generation 

Brain MRI raises a sharper question. Because its public-cohort imaging (PDGM 13 ) could be familiar from pretraining, we asked whether brain accuracy re fl ects tumor reading or image-distribution memorization by repeating the protocol on a second, fully private in-house brain cohort generated with the identical pipeline, and compared the two with matched question generation (Table  3). On report-derived questions, the two cohorts are indistinguishable within the reported intervals, and every model’s blind accuracy falls within the ± 0.05 margin of its sighted accuracy, and the frontier models stay high even when blind (Claude near 0.81, GPT-5.2 near 0.74), showing that report-derived brain accuracy changes little when visual input is removed. The large public-only sighted-minus-blind difference suggested by an earlier, generation-mismatched comparison disappears under matched generation. A difference remains on RADS-style; however, the frontier models score 0.17 to 0.19 higher on public than private imaging (sighted: Claude 0.67 versus 0.50, GPT-5.2 0.65 versus 0.46), whereas MedGemma1.0-27B differs by < 0.01. The sighted-minus-blind patterns differ by imaging source: blinding raises Claude’s private RADS accuracy by 0.133, whereas on public imaging sighted accuracy is similar to or higher than blind accuracy (GPT-5.2 Δ = 0.09). This pattern is compatible with an in fl uence of image-source familiarity, but

the present analysis does not establish its cause or distinguish familiarity from other cohort differences. The blind condition also indicates available nonvisual signal: Brain RADS-style questions are predominantly binary (≈ 92% yes/no), and the blinded frontier models exceed the random baseline by up to 0.17, which may re fl ect skewed clinical base rates or other question-text cues; MedGemma1.0-27B does not exceed its baseline and yields a near-baseline estimate of brain-RADS performance. Contamination conclusions are thus only as trustworthy as the consistency of the benchmark that produces them. 

# Per-subcategory failure patterns 

A per-subcategory breakdown of report-derived questions (Fig.  2) resolves where the aggregate accuracies come from. In every cohort the lowest-accuracy subcategory, pooled across the  fi ve models, is a quantitative or graded judgment: lesion size on liver CT (0.295) and on liver MRI (0.276), Lung-RADS category on lung CT (0.317), and mass-effect grading on brain MRI (0.481). Because subcategories differ in their option-count mix, raw accuracies are not directly comparable across them, so each is also referenced to its own chance level (Supplementary Table 2). Those four subcategories clear chance by only + 0.093, + 0.071, + 0.067 and + 0.190 respectively, whereas qualitative recognition subcategories clear it by far wider margins: anatomic distribution on liver CT (+ 0.467), pleural  fi ndings (+ 0.493) and clinical interpretation (+ 0.522) on lung CT, and oedema on brain MRI (+ 0.515). The pattern is a property of the task rather than of any one system: across the  fi ve models, accuracy on liver CT lesion size spans only 0.25 to 0.35 and on Lung-RADS category 0.28 to 0.41, so no model escapes it. Recognising that a 

fi nding is present is therefore largely within reach, whereas assigning it a size, an ordinal category or a severity grade is not, even when the relevant region is visible and the same model answers recognition questions on the same images well. 

# Multiplicity-controlled con fi rmation 

The intervals in Tables  1 to  3 are pointwise and 76 of them are reported, so before any individual contrast is relied upon we repeated the same case-clustered resampling at higher resolution and controlled the false discovery rate within two families (Methods). Of the 40 accuracy-versus-chance tests, 37 remain above chance after Benjamini-Hochberg correction. The three exceptions are the near-baseline cells: Qwen3-VL-30B on liver MRI for both question types, and MedGemma1.0-27B on brain RADS-style, so those observations are not artefacts of a single unadjusted interval (Supplementary Table 1). 

The 30 unique image-effect cells resolve completely (Table  4): 17 show a Δ that differs from zero after FDR control, 16 are equivalent to zero within the prespeci fi ed ± 0.05 margin by two one-sided tests, and 3 satisfy both, being reliably nonzero yet smaller than the margin. No cell is left undetermined, so at this margin the design distinguishes a genuine image effect from its absence in every condition tested.

The resolved pattern sharpens the main  fi nding rather than softening it. For Claude Opus 4.6, the highest-scoring model, all  fi ve report-derived cells are equivalent to zero, and its only three FDR-signi fi cant effects are negative and all RADS-style: on liver CT (Δ = -0.065), lung CT (-0.163) and private brain (-0.133), removing the image improved accuracy. MedGemma1.0-27B shows the opposite pro fi le, large positive effects on liver CT (+ 0.102 RADS-style, + 0.291 report-derived) but all four brain cells equivalent to zero, two of them reliably nonzero yet still inside the margin. GPT-5.2 lies between the two, with positive effects on both liver cohorts and on public-brain RADS-style (+ 0.092) and equivalence on lung RADS-style and on brain report-derived questions in both cohorts. 

# Discussion 

Our objective was not to construct an exhaustive model leaderboard, but to determine whether a renewable, privately generated benchmark can distinguish image-dependent performance from question-text and dataset-familiarity effects; the  fi ve-model panel is a snapshot of contemporary systems rather than a comprehensive survey, and the per-cohort orderings reported here should not be read as de fi nitive rankings. Read with that scope in mind, these results carry a direct message for clinical adoption: current VLMs, including the medical specialists tested, are not yet reliable readers of volumetric oncology studies presented in the reduced 2D formats such systems accept, and headline accuracies may overstate visual competence wherever the question text is solvable from priors: lung CT, and brain MRI for both public and private imaging, while comparatively large sighted-minus-blind differences are observed on liver for MedGemma1.0-27B and GPT-5.2 (all such differences remain signi fi cant under Benjamini-Hochberg FDR control, and the small brain and lung report-derived differences are con fi rmed equivalent to zero within ± 0.05 by two one-sided tests; Table  4). The subcategory pattern is consistent with this input constraint: size, ordinal category and severity grading all rest on spatial measurement, which survives the montage reduction poorly, whereas presence-or-absence recognition survives it well. The reporting tasks that turn on measurement, size tracking for treatment response, Lung-RADS assignment, mass-effect grading, are thus the least likely to transfer to current 2D-input systems, and they are also the ones that most directly drive management decisions. An alternative reading of the blind-ablation result must be addressed: at roughly 128 × 128 pixels per tiled slice, the montage itself may erase the visual evidence, so that blind-equivalent accuracy re fl ects an impoverished input rather than a model property. Three observations argue against rendering as the sole explanation. First, the montage demonstrably carries exploitable signal: MedGemma1.0-27B gains 0.291 on liver CT report-derived questions from the same composites. Second, the failure pro fi le is not uniform degradation but a systematic dissociation, presence-versus-absence recognition clears chance by wide margins on the same images on which size, ordinal category and severity grading barely clear it, which is the signature of resolution-limited measurement, not of an uninformative image. Third, where the image does change behavior it can do so in either direction, including the three conditions in which removing the image signi fi cantly improved Claude Opus 4.6's accuracy. The montage is therefore best understood as part of what is being evaluated: it is the input format that 2D-interface systems impose on volumetric studies today, and the benchmark measures what survives it. 

> Page 7/21

Two properties of that con fi rmatory analysis matter for how benchmark results should be read. An FDR-controlled test can establish that an effect exists but never that one is absent, and the claims that carry this paper are null-direction claims: that accuracy changes little when the image is removed. They are supported here by equivalence testing against a margin  fi xed in advance, not by a failure to reach signi fi cance, an inference that is not available from an unadjusted interval that merely overlaps zero, though benchmark reports commonly draw it. The three cells that are simultaneously signi fi cant and equivalent show why both procedures are needed, since a difference can be statistically reliable and still too small to matter. The equivalence counts are conditional on the ± 0.05 margin we  fi xed before the analysis; a stricter margin would move cells from equivalent to undetermined without changing which effects are real. Several limitations are inherent. 2D-input models receive each sequence as one 12 × 12 composite image of 144 axial slices rather than as a navigable volume, so an individual slice is rendered at no more than roughly 128 × 128 pixels, understating what slice-aware or 3D-native systems (M3D 14 ,RadFM 15 , Merlin 16 ) might achieve. Report-derived questions, though grounded, can carry residual language-model authoring bias, and their answer keys were validated by an automated report-grounded check with human review at the schema and template level rather than by per-item radiologist adjudication. All cohorts originate from a single institution, so reporting conventions and case mix may not generalize. The blind ablation covers three of the  fi ve models, so visual-reliance conclusions are restricted to those systems. Finally, LI-RADS and Lung-RADS were developed for at-risk screening and surveillance populations rather than cohorts of con fi rmed cancer patients; we use them here as schemas of visually assessable  fi ndings, not as clinical category assignments. Contamination-controlled evaluation thus needs private reports and a generation process held  fi xed across the cohorts being compared, so that apparent visual signals are not generation artifacts. We release the pipeline as an open agent skill so any institution can rebuild a modality-speci fi c, contamination-controlled benchmark on its own paired reports and images, optionally with a locally hosted language model, turning evaluation from a static public artifact into a private, renewable in-house test.

# Methods 

# Study design and cohorts 

We applied the pipeline to four oncology cohorts: brain MRI, liver MRI, liver CT, and lung CT, in which every case is a con fi rmed cancer patient. The brain MRI cohort is a hybrid con fi guration: imaging derives from the publicly available UCSF Preoperative Diffuse Glioma MRI (PDGM) cohort 13  paired with private institutional reports; the other three cohorts are fully private from the same institution. Case and question counts are given in Table  1. A  fi fth, fully private in-house brain MRI cohort is generated identically but reserved for the public/private contamination ablation (Table  3), not the main benchmark. For 2D-input models, each imaging sequence is rendered as a single composite image: 144 axial slices are sampled uniformly across the volume and tiled into a 12 × 12 montage, so that a sequence is presented to the model as one composite image rather than as a navigable volume. Questions that require more than one sequence receive one composite image per required sequence. CT series use organ-appropriate intensity windowing, and each composite is resized so that its longest dimension does not exceed 1,536 pixels, leaving at most roughly 128 × 128 pixels per tiled slice. 

# Benchmark construction: RADS-style questions 

For each dataset, a schema of clinically relevant visual features is induced from a strati fi ed sample of reports and grounded in established reporting frameworks (LI-RADS 7 for liver, Lung-RADS 8 for lung, and an institutional RANO-aligned schema 9 for brain). Each  fi eld corresponds to a  fi nding directly assessable from the image, and clinicians review the schema for visual answerability before generation. Fields are populated deterministically from report text (binary  fi elds map to yes/no; multi-value  fi elds to 3–5 options with dataset-wide distractors). 

# Benchmark construction: report-derived questions 

> Page 14/21

Report-derived questions are generated by prompting a language model with the parsed  fi ndings and impression sections to produce multiple-choice questions spanning the radiologist’s clinical content. Report-derived items contain two to  fi ve answer options, with four-option items predominating; the option range and predominant four-option con fi guration are shown in Fig.  1. To promote breadth, the generation prompt asks the model to span three coarse cognitive levels: perception (recognition or localization of a  fi nding), characterization (description or grading of its imaging features), and diagnosis (integration of  fi ndings into a diagnostic conclusion). These levels serve only as prompt-level scaffolding and an initial coarse categorization; they are not used as prespeci fi ed strata for downstream analysis. Instead, downstream analyses use more granular clinical subcategory tags derived from question content and tailored to the relevant anatomy, imaging  fi ndings, and disease context. Each question undergoes structural validation and a report-grounded check con fi rming that the correct answer is explicitly supported by the source text; failures are discarded. The pipeline runs automatically with human review at only two checkpoints (schema con fi rmation and template approval). The report-grounded check removes factually unsupported answers but cannot fully eliminate residual language-model authoring bias, an inherent limitation of automated question generation. 

# Models and inference 

Five VLMs were selected to represent contemporary systems available through commercial APIs or open-weight releases, while spanning both general-purpose and medically adapted model classes, and were evaluated strictly zero-shot: Claude Opus 4.6 (released 5 February 2026), GPT-5.2 (released 11 December 2025), Qwen3-VL-30B, MedGemma1.0-27B, and MedGemma1.5-4B. Each model received the composite image or images, the question, and the answer options only, and never saw the radiology report. Closed-weights models were accessed through privacy-preserving institutional API endpoints and open-weights models were run on-premises, with no clinical text leaving the institution. Because closed-model serving stacks may evolve after release, these results should be interpreted as speci fi c to the named model releases and evaluation period. 

# Blind ablation and public/private comparison 

To isolate visual from language-prior contributions, models were re-evaluated with the image replaced by a blank input, holding question text, options, and prompt  fi xed (Claude Opus 4.6, GPT-5.2, and MedGemma1.0-27B, reported in Table  2 and Table  3). For every (dataset, question type, model), the sighted and blind accuracies are computed on the intersection of questions that are valid and scored in both runs, so that Δ = sighted minus blind is a same-question contrast; this also removes any effect of runs that sampled different pool sizes. To test whether the brain visual signal re fl ects genuine reading or public-image familiarity, the identical pipeline was used to generate a fully private in-house brain cohort, compared directly with the public PDGM cohort (no subsampling; Table  3). 

# Statistics and reproducibility 

Accuracy is the fraction of valid model responses matching ground truth; invalid or unparsable responses are excluded. Interval estimates are our primary summaries; we additionally report 

> Page 15/21

multiplicity-controlled signi fi cance tests as a con fi rmatory robustness check (described below). Because answer pools mix 2- to 5-option items, per-cell chance varies; a random baseline is computed as the mean of 1/k across each pool’s choice-count distribution. Blind effects are reported as the point difference Δ, de fi ned as sighted minus blind. All analyses are deterministic given the  fi xed seed. All uncertainty in this study is quanti fi ed by a single nonparametric, case-clustered bootstrap (B = 2,000 resamples; seed 42) that resamples cases, not questions, with replacement, so that intervals re fl ect generalization to new patients rather than to new questions from the same patients. The identical procedure is applied throughout Tables  1–3: to the marginal zero-shot accuracies (Table  1), and, for the sighted-versus-blind contrasts (Tables  2 and  3), to the paired per-question difference Δ = sighted - blind computed on the question set scored in both runs, with the same resampled case set applied to both arms so that the pairing is preserved. All reported intervals are 95% percentile intervals. In total we report 40 accuracy intervals (5 models × 4 cohorts × 2 question types; Table  1) and 36 paired-difference intervals (24 in Table  2; 12 in Table  3). These are pointwise, per-cell intervals and are not adjusted for multiplicity. They are therefore not simultaneous: the nominal 95% coverage applies to each interval individually and not to the family, and under repeated sampling roughly 5% of unadjusted intervals would be expected to exclude the true value by chance. The intervals are presented as descriptive measures of estimation precision, and no conclusion in this work is conditioned on any single interval attaining a signi fi cance threshold. Accordingly, we do not infer that two models differ because their marginal intervals fail to overlap, and we assess comparative patterns by their consistency across cohorts, question types, and models rather than by any single cell. As a con fi rmatory robustness check, and using the same case-clustered resampling at higher resolution (B = 10,000; seed 42), we additionally compute multiplicity-controlled tests, controlling the false discovery rate (FDR) within each of two families by the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. Family 1 (40 tests) asks whether each Table  1 accuracy exceeds its per-question chance level (one-sided; statistic the mean of 1[correct] − 1/k over the model’s valid pool). Family 2 (30 unique image-effect tests, with the six public-brain cells shared by Tables  2 and  3 counted once) asks whether Δ differs from zero (two-sided percentile-inversion bootstrap p, consistent by construction with the reported 95% intervals); for these cells we also perform two one-sided equivalence tests (TOST) against the prespeci fi ed ± 0.05 margin, declaring equivalence when the 90% interval lies within ± 0.05. Per-cell p-values, Benjamini–Hochberg q-values and TOST outcomes for the image effect are given in Table  4; the accuracy-versus-chance family is reported in Supplementary Table 1. After FDR control, 37 of 40 accuracies exceed chance (the exceptions are Qwen3-VL-30B on liver MRI and MedGemma1.0-27B on brain RADS-style), and all 30 image-effect comparisons resolve: 17 show a genuine nonzero Δ and 16 are equivalent to zero within ± 0.05 (three are both), with the frontier models’ brain report-derived accuracy equivalent between sighted and blind (Claude Opus 4.6, GPT-5.2 and MedGemma1.0-27B all TOST q < 0.001).
