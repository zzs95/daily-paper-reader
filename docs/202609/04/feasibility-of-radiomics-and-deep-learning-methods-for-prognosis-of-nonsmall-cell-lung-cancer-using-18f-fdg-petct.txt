Abstract 

Accurate prognostication for non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) is challenging due to tumor heterogeneity and the limitations of conventional PET biomarkers. This technical feasibility study compared the difference in prognostic performance of radiomics and deep learning (CNN) methods against conventional biomarkers such as SUVmax and TLG to predict 1-year overall survival (OS 1) in 150 treatment-naïve patients. Using automated lesion segmentation, internal results showed AUCs between 0.75 and 0.80, while external validation initially yielded AUCs from 0.58 to 0.68. Following bootstrapped subsampling, all models showed improved performance, led by the TLG-only model with an AUC of 0.83. Study scope was limited by the lack of feature-level harmonization and variations in image quality and segmentation. Although simple PET biomarkers performed comparably to more complex architectures, they offer the advantage of being robust and clinically accessible. In summary, while radiomics and deep learning models each show feasibility for PET-based prognostication in NSCLC, the radiomics model currently provides greater stability and interpretability, and TLG continues to represent an effective, clinically practical biomarker. 

Keywords: 

NSCLC, 18 F-FDG PET, radiomics, deep learning, CNN, prognosis 

Introduction 

The current clinical staging system for non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) using the Tumor, Node, Metastasis (IASLC/ATS TNM staging system, 9 th edition) classification is based on the prognosis of the patients irrespective of the given treatment(s) 1. However, prognosis within individual stages remains highly variable 2. Current staging systems often fail to adequately explain relapse and survival differences within subgroups, as each stage encompasses a heterogeneous population. Incorporating clinical and molecular biomarkers into the existing TNM framework remains a significant challenge 3. To optimize treatment strategies, further stratification of patients into high- and low-risk categories is essential. Univariate analyses have shown that positron emission tomography (PET) imaging with 18 F-fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) can provide prognostic biomarkers based on tumor metabolism and lesion volume 4. Standardized uptake values (SUVs), particularly maximum SUV (SUVmax), are widely used in

However, the prognostic utility of SUVmax remains inconsistent, with conflicting results reported in the literature 9,10 . Furthermore, within the pathological NSCLC subtypes adenocarcinomas show large variations in tumor metabolism (mainly the intensity of FDG uptake usually referred to as ”FDG avidity”) 11 .Lesion volume can be quantified using metrics such as metabolic active tumor volume (MATV) and total lesion glycolysis (TLG). MATV is derived from segmented tumor regions on PET images, while TLG is calculated as the product of SUVmean and MATV 12,13 .Dissemination features have also demonstrated predictive value in ¹⁸F-FDG PET examinations for diffuse large B-cell lymphoma 14 and have recently been reported to carry prognostic significance in NSCLC as well 15 . In this study, we specifically focus on the maximum distance between two suspicious lesions seen on FDG PET (Dmax bulk ) as a representative dissemination metric. Beyond conventional PET biomarkers, radiomic analysis allows for the extraction of a broader range of quantitative imaging features. Efforts to standardize radiomic feature extraction have resulted in a large, structured set of features 16 . These features can be incorporated into multivariate models to enhance prognostic accuracy 17-21 .In parallel, deep learning methods have shown promise in medical imaging across a range of pathologies 22-24 . Convolutional neural networks (CNNs), in particular, have been applied to FDG PET images to predict clinical outcomes, such as time to progression (TTP) in patients with diffuse large B-cell lymphoma 25 .To the best of our knowledge, a direct comparison of radiomics and deep learning methods has not yet been extensively explored for FDG PET imaging in NSCLC. Furthermore, limited studies have utilized deep learning to directly predict overall survival in NSCLC patients from FDG PET imaging 26,27 . Therefore, it is of particular interest to explore and directly compare the prognostic performance of deep learning and radiomics approaches. The aim of this study was to evaluate the feasibility of predicting 1-year overall survival (OS) in patients with NSCLC using two approaches based on FDG PET imaging: a multivariate radiomics model and a deep learning approach using a CNN and compare these with conventional PET uptake metrics. 

Method 

Patients 

This study examined two different patient cohorts: an internal cohort that was utilized for model training and an external cohort that was used for external validation. The patient cohort used for model training was obtained from the LC-NMR study, comprising individuals who were newly diagnosed with NSCLC between February 2011 and August 2012. The original research was registered on the public database for clinical trials, ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT02024113). The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical rules of the Helsinki Declaration and Good Clinical Practice and was approved by the ethics review board of Amsterdam UMC, Amsterdam, The Netherlands. All study participants provided informed consent. (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1556086416003348?via%3Dihub)

Only treatment-naïve patients with NSCLC were included to create a more consistent group for analysis. The external cohort used for external validation consisted of subjects from the ProLung study with study number NCT03736993. The ProLung study included patients with early stages (stage I-IIIA) of NSCLC. The use of this external data was approved by the institutional review board (Number: 2024.1132). The need for written informed consent was waived due to the nature of the study. No clinical data from the patients were used in the model, except for survival and staging for stratification purposes. 

Patient characteristics 

The internal patient cohort includes 150 patients with a median age of 69 years (IQR 60 – 75 years). The group consists of 102 (68%) male and 48 (32%) female patients. The median overall survival (OS) is 10 months (IQR 6 – 17) and the OS per staging is presented in Table 1. Further patient characteristics are presented in detail in Vanhove et al. 4.The external patient cohort includes 89 patients with a median age of 70 years (IQR 64 – 75), and consists of 52 (58%) male and 37 (42%) female patients. The median overall survival (OS) is 53 months (IQR 43 – 64) and the OS per staging is presented Table 1. Further patient characteristics are presented in detail in Vanhove et al. 4.

PET/CT protocol 

All patients in both the internal study (GEMINI TF Big Bore, Philips) and the external study (Biograph Horizon, Siemens) underwent EARL1 compliant PET/CT scans. All PET/CT examinations were performed at Limburgs PET centrum in Ziekenhuis Oost-Limburg, Belgium. For this study, only the attenuation corrected PET images were assessed. The protocol for the internal dataset is described in Vanhove et al. 4. For the external study, prior to examination, all patients fasted for a minimum of six hours. Blood glucose measurements were conducted to ascertain that blood glucose levels were ≤200 mg/dL. Patients were administered an intravenous injection of 18 F-FDG at a dose of 3 MBq/kg body mass. The radiopharmaceutical 18 F-FDG tracer is produced and delivered by UZ Leuven. The images were captured one hour after tracer administration. Firstly, CT scans were performed. Subsequently, PET scan images were obtained and CT-based attenuation corrected. Both scans covered the region from the mid-thighs to the base of the skull. The emission time for each bed position was dependent on the patient’s weight. 

Lesion segmentation 

The lesions in the PET images were segmented using LIONZ, an automated deep learning-based tool developed at the Medical University of Vienna, Austria 28 . LIONZ was trained on over 1000 18 F-FDG PET/CT scans of patients with multiple cancer types (lymphoma, melanoma, and lung cancer) and healthy subjects. LIONZ is open-source and is available for download at Zenodo. The segmentations comprised the whole tumor burden. Only erroneously segmented injection sites were manually corrected to assess the tool’s performance as a semi-automatic pipeline. 

Radiomics 

The radiomic features were extracted using the ACCURATE tool 29 . A total of 480 radiomic features, including 22 morphological, 50 intensity, and 408 textural parameters, were extracted from the total metabolic tumor volume (MTV) at the patient level. Image preprocessing involved resampling to a 2 × 2 × 2 mm voxel size using tri-linear interpolation. For textural feature calculation, images were discretized with a fixed bin size of 0.25 SUV as it has been shown to provide more robust radiomics features than fixed bin-number discretization 30-32 . Additionally, five standard PET metrics (SUVmax, SUVpeak, SUVmean, MTV, and total lesion glycolysis) were derived from the original, non-resampled images. No feature-level harmonization, such as ComBat 33,34 , was applied. Although EARL 1-compliant 

ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT ARTICLE IN PRESS ARTICLE IN PRESS reconstructions were used to assure scanner performance harmonization, this cannot assure perfect harmonization and some variability in image quality, noise levels (also among patients) and segmentation methods could still be present. COMBAT harmonization would allow to retrospectively further take this variabilities into account. The lack of COMBAT retrospective harmonization remains a limitation for higher-order textural features, which are sensitive to variations in noise, effective resolution, and segmentation that may affect reproducibility 30 .Feature reduction was done by applying pairwise elimination using a threshold of 0.7, chosen as a compromise between dimensionality reduction and feature retention without formal optimization, while forcing SUVmax, TLG, and the maximum distance between the largest lesion and any other lesion (Dmax bulk ) to be retained, which reduced the feature set to 21. Feature selection and model optimization were conducted within a nested 5-fold cross-validation (CV) framework to ensure robust performance estimation. To handle class imbalance, we implemented Logistic Regression with cost-sensitive learning (balanced class weights and a maximum of 1000 iterations) as the base estimator for Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE). We utilized Stratified K-Fold for both the inner and outer CV loops to preserve the distribution of classes and stages, maintaining fold consistency via a fixed random seed. The inner loop optimized the number of features (ranging from 1 to 10) by maximizing the Area Under the Curve (AUC). Following this procedure, the seven top-ranked features were selected for the final "Radiomics model," with the outer CV loop providing the unbiased estimate of its generalization performance. Additional models evaluated included a Three-Feature model comprising SUVmax, TLG, and Dmax bulk ; a Two-Feature model incorporating TLG and Dmax bulk ; and single-feature models using either SUVmax or TLG individually. These features resemble the factors nuclear medicine physicians consider when assessing 18 F-FDG PET-CT exams to predict tumor behavior and prognosis in patients with suspected NSCLC. The radiomics features and their definitions are listed in Supplemental File 1. Each model outputs a binary classification corresponding to the probability of OS within one year. Specifically, the models estimate the likelihood of OS ≤ 1 year, denoted as P(OS₁), and OS > 1 year, denoted as P(OS 0), where a higher P(OS₁) indicates an increased risk of early progression or absence of durable remission. The decision to use a one-year cutoff was made to ensure a balanced training dataset. 

Deep Learning 

The inputs for the CNN model are coronal and sagittal maximum intensity projections (MIPS) of the lesion segmentations by LION (i.e., lesion MIPs), an example of which is shown in Figure 1. Based on the architecture by Ferrandez et al. 25 , the model processes these two views in parallel through identical branches consisting of four convolutional layers, with feature maps doubling at each stage from 16 to 128. Each layer applies (3,3) kernels with ReLU activation, 0.4 dropout to mitigate overfitting, and MaxPooling (sizes 3x3, 3x3, and 2x2). Following a Global Average Pooling 2D (GAP2D) layer, the features from both branches are concatenated at a final fully connected layer. The classifier was compiled using the Adam optimizer (learning rate: 0.0005, decay rate: 0.000001) and a weighted entropy loss function to predict the final probability P(OS 1) via a softmax activation. We implemented a weight-saving strategy where only the model parameters corresponding to the minimum validation loss were retained. 

Statistical analysis 

The internal and external validation of the models was performed by calculating the AUC of the ROC curves. To assess the robustness of the internal model, a bootstrap analysis using replacement sampling was performed on the whole internal dataset using 1000 iterations. For the external validation, an additional analysis was conducted to better match the stratification in the staging of the external group to the internal group through case mixing. In this external validation, 10 samples were taken from stage I and II patients and 10 from stage III patients using 1000 iterations. Model performance for both 

ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT ARTICLE IN PRESS ARTICLE IN PRESS analyses was assessed using AUC values summarized in violin plots to illustrate distribution and robustness. Furthermore, DeLong tests were performed on the full external dataset to compare the model performances, with the TLG-only model as reference, an established PET biomarker 4,12 . This was done to determine if the difference in AUC values was statistically significant.

Results 

The internal performance results are shown in Figure 2. The single-feature models using SUVmax and TLG each achieved an internal AUC of 0.75. Building on this, the Two ￿Feature model showed slightly improved performance with an AUC of 0.76, while the Three ￿Feature model reached an AUC of 0.80. The Radiomics model produced a comparable AUC of 0.78, and the CNN model reached its optimal performance after 37 epochs with an AUC of 0.77. Figure 2 presents the ROC curves and corresponding AUCs for all models. Internal bootstrap analysis demonstrated the stability of these results. The median AUC were 0.79 (IQR 0.76 - 0.82) for the Radiomics model, 0.71 (IQR 0.68 - 0.74) for SUVmax, and 0.74 (IQR 0.71 - 0.77) for TLG. Similarly, the Two-Feature, Three-Feature, and CNN models yielded median AUCs of 0.74 (IQR 0.71 - 0.77), 0.75 (IQR 0.72 - 0.78), and 0.77 (IQR 0.74 - 0.79), respectively. In Figure 3, we present the external validation over the total group. The SUVmax and CNN models yielded the lowest AUC values of 0.58 and 0.59, respectively. The best performing models were the TLG-only and Two-Feature model with AUCs of 0.68 and 0.67, respectively. The Three-Feature and Radiomics models are both trailing with 0.65. Statistical comparisons using DeLong tests were performed on this full external dataset to evaluate whether these differences in performance were significant relative to the TLG-only model. The results showed no statistically significant differences between the reference biomarker and the alternative models. Specifically, the Two-Feature model (p = 0.4795), Radiomics model (p = 0.6641), and Three-Feature model (p = 0.4196) all maintained comparable performance to the TLG-only model. Similarly,

As shown in Figure 4, the bootstrapped subsampled external validation showed considerably better performance across all models, shifting the distribution of AUCs toward higher values. The exception was the SUVmax-only model, which did not improve in this analysis. The TLG-only and Two-Feature models performed best, with median AUCs of 0.83 (IQR 0.67 - 0.93) and 0.82 (IQR 0.67 - 0.93), respectively. The Radiomics model achieved a median AUC of 0.79 (IQR 0.65 - 0.89), while the Three-Feature model achieved a median AUC of 0.75 (IQR 0.61 - 0.86). The CNN produced a median AUC of 0.75 (IQR 0.59 - 0.89) and, together with the SUVmax-only model, showed the greatest variability in performance.

Discussion 

This study assessed the performance of radiomics and deep learning models for predicting 1-year overall survival in NSCLC using 18 F-FDG PET imaging. While both approaches showed promising discriminatory ability, the internal bootstrap analysis confirmed the stability of these findings, with the Radiomics model and CNN demonstrating consistent performance across iterations. Furthermore, the DeLong tests indicated that no significant difference in AUC exists between the more complex models and the established TLG-only biomarker on the full external dataset, suggesting that the alternative modeling approaches maintained comparable technical performance. The size of the internal and external datasets pose significant constraints on model generalizability. Our bootstrap stage sampling analysis revealed substantial variability in AUC estimates, indicating sensitivity to the composition of different stages. This instability is due to the small internal dataset and the mismatch in patient characteristics between the internal and the external datasets. Adding to this, the patients were scanned on different PET/CT cameras, which introduces an additional degree of variability between the two datasets. Furthermore, the focus of this study was primarily on the technical feasibility and inter-comparison of PET-only models rather than the development of a prognostic tool for immediate clinical use. Consequently, the models were evaluated based on their discriminatory power (AUC) rather than their calibration. At this stage of development, the relatively small dataset size and inherent class imbalance make it difficult to produce reliable calibration curves without the risk of overfitting. A notable limitation is that no clinical variables, such as age, sex, histology, or ECOG performance status, were adjusted for or included in the models. As our objective was to evaluate the technical feasibility of 

ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT ARTICLE IN PRESS ARTICLE IN PRESS radiomics and deep learning using PET information exclusively, we did not intend to develop a comprehensive clinical prognostic model. For such a model to be clinically relevant and robust enough for practice, significantly larger datasets would be required, likely involving approximately 1,000 cases with well-documented clinical variables and outcome data as show previously by Lin et al. 35 .Our study employed an automated segmentation pipeline using the LIONZ tool with only manual injection spot corrections. This approach may impact model reliability in several ways: (1) SUVmax measurements could be affected by inclusion of volumes that are erroneously segmented as lesions, (2) Dmax bulk calculations may be influenced by incorrectly segmented volumes, and (3) TLG values could be altered by inclusion of incorrectly segmented volumes. Although automated processing improves reproducibility, segmentation inaccuracies such as these may impact the reliability of radiomic feature extraction and should be considered a potential limitation of our approach. The results of our study are consistent with previous studies indicating that TLG is a good prognostic marker 4,12,13 . The TLG-only and Two-Feature model demonstrated reasonably consistent prognostic performance. Additionally, these two models are advantageous in terms of clinical interpretability, as both rely on two well-understood PET-derived metrics. Prior studies have suggested low prognostic value of SUVmax based on univariate analysis 4, which is in agreement with our findings. Interestingly, although we anticipated that Dmax bulk would significantly contribute to model performance, given that distant metastasis in NSCLC corresponds to stage IV disease 36 , our external dataset results did not support this assumption. This discrepancy may be explained by the total absence of stage IV patients in our external dataset, whereas they comprised nearly 30% of the internal cohort. We would expect that if stage IV patients were included in the external validation, the model performance would likely improve, as it would reduce the class imbalance and include patients with a higher probability of short overall survival, whom the models should identify more easily than stage I or II patients. This study implemented a CNN using the architectural framework described by Ferrandez et al. 25 , but with a significant modification. Our model utilized only lesion MIPs as input, whereas the original incorporated both lesion MIPs and brain-excluded MIPs. Despite this simplification, our approach achieved comparable performance. However, it is worth noting that they benefited from a larger internal and external dataset in Ferrandez et al. 25 , allowing for more robust model development and comprehensive evaluation. Consequently, direct comparisons between our feasibility study and their more extensively validated work should be interpreted with appropriate caution. When the CNN model was presented with a blank MIP representing a patient with no lesions, it yielded a P(OS 1) = 0.35. This baseline probability reflects the model’s inherent bias toward classifying OS 1 in this specific patient cohort and serves as an important reference point for contextualizing classification performance. We would expect a blank MIP to produce a low P(OS 1) given the absence of metabolically active lesions. In the general external validation, the CNN model performs significantly worse than the radiomics models. However, when we introduced a more representative distribution of patient staging with respect to the training dataset, external validation performance was more in line with those seen during training. Since the external dataset contains mostly data from early stage (0 + I + II) patients, this indicates that the model is much more effective at predicting outcomes for stage III patients than for 

ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT ARTICLE IN PRESS ARTICLE IN PRESS early stage patients. Previously, it has been shown that model performances can vary substantially among external testing datasets 37 , most likely due to patient demographics and disease stage prevalence. This was also confirmed in a simulation study 38 . Therefore, we performed the external data bootstrap analysis using stage stratification to better match the case mix with the training dataset, confirming the performance of our simple PET metrics, radiomics, and deep learning models as seen during training. However, this rebalancing risks leading to overly optimistic interpretations, and the actual generalizability of these results to unselected real-world cohorts remains unproven. To transition from this technical feasibility study to a clinically relevant application, future research must involve larger, more balanced cohorts and a more extensive external validation process. Furthermore, such studies should include the assessment of calibration curves to ensure that the predicted probabilities align accurately with observed clinical outcomes, a step that was omitted here as we focused on evaluating initial discriminatory potential. Our models performed similarly to models used in previous studies using internal PET/CT validation. Van Timmeren et al. 21 reported AUCs ranging from 0.66 to 0.89 for predicting 2-year survival in 138 patients, while Taheri et al. 20 achieved an internal AUC of 0.71 for a 5-year survival outcome in 170 patients. More recently, Lin et al. 35 utilized PET radiomics in a sample of 261 patients to predict a 6-month binary outcome of durable clinical benefit. They reported an AUC of 0.591 in their internal testing cohort for PET-only modeling, but they observed significantly improved results with an AUC of 0.819 when combining features from PET, CT, and multimodal PET/CT information. Consistent with the findings of Lin et al. 35 , it is evident that if a model is to be developed for clinical research, the use of multi-modal approaches is crucial 39 . A limitation of the current study is that we focused exclusively on PET data and did not incorporate CT information, which may limit the immediate clinical utility of the findings. However, the aim of this work was specifically to compare the performance of radiomics versus deep learning models based on PET only, providing a direct comparison of these methodologies within a metabolic imaging framework. 

Conclusion 

This study evaluated the technical feasibility of radiomics and deep learning models for predicting one-year overall survival in patients with NSCLC using FDG PET imaging. Radiomics-based approaches demonstrated more robust and consistent performance across internal and external validations compared to the CNN model, although DeLong tests confirmed that no model reached a statistically significant improvement over the established TLG-only biomarker. The CNN model achieved comparable AUC values in the internal cohort, yet it exhibited reduced generalizability and greater sensitivity to the staging composition in the external dataset. Notably, the conventional PET-derived biomarker TLG demonstrated prognostic performance comparable to that of both the multivariate radiomics and deep learning models. This finding highlights that simple metrics, such as TLG, retain substantial clinical value, offering reliable prognostic insight without the complexity or computational burden associated with high-dimensional modeling approaches. Because this study focused on technical performance rather than clinical implementation, the models were not adjusted for clinical variables or evaluated for calibration.
