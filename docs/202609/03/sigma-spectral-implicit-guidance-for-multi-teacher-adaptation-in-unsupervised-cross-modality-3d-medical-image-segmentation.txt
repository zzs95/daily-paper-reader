# SIGMA: spectral-implicit guidance for Multi-Teacher adaptation in unsupervised cross-modality 3D medical image segmentation 

Jiaqi Zheng 1 · Jing Lian 1,2  · Huaikun Zhang 2 · Jizhao Liu 2 · Wenjuan Zhou 3

> Received: 12 May 2026 / Accepted: 20 July 2026 © The Author(s) 2026

Abstract 

Unsupervised domain adaptation (UDA) for three-dimensional (3D) cross-modality medical image segmentation is affected by domain shifts arising from heterogeneous imaging physics and acquisition protocols. Existing methods remain lim-ited by ringing artifacts from hard spectral truncation, unreliable pseudo-label supervision, and fixed single-pass inference. This paper presents Spectral-Implicit Guidance for Multi-Teacher Adaptation (SIGMA), a unified integration framework composed of Ringing-suppressed Soft Spectral Alignment (RSSA), Reliability-aware Multi-Teacher Fusion (RMTF), and Cognitive Implicit Refinement Adapter (CIRA). RSSA performs smooth low-frequency appearance transfer while preserving high-frequency anatomy; RMTF generates uncertainty-aware soft pseudo-labels from heterogeneous teacher disagreement; and CIRA conducts lightweight inference-time refinement over high-risk regions with the segmentation backbone frozen. Experiments on Brain Tumor Segmentation 2021 to Brain Tumor Segmentation 2023 Sub-Saharan Africa (BraTS2021-to-BraTS2023 SSA) and Multi-Modality Whole Heart Segmentation 2017 (MM-WHS 2017) magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)-to-computed tomography (CT) show that SIGMA achieves the highest average mean Dice among the evaluated meth-ods and competitive boundary/volume performance. For MM-WHS, the observed differences are interpreted as numerical trends because the paired tests do not reach statistical significance with the limited evaluation cohort. The results suggest that spectral guidance, reliability-aware multi-teacher supervision, and localized implicit refinement provide complementary functions within a unified cross-modality 3D adaptation pipeline.

pathological information through distinct physical mecha-nisms. For example, computed tomography (CT) primarily reflects tissue-dependent X-ray attenuation, whereas mag-netic resonance imaging (MRI) captures soft-tissue contrast through sequence-dependent magnetic resonance signals. Such differences are further amplified by variations in acqui-sition protocols, scanner vendors, imaging parameters, and clinical populations. Consequently, a model trained in one domain may produce fragmented contours, missing small structures, or unreliable lesion delineation in another domain, especially in low-contrast or anatomically complex cases. A direct solution is to annotate target-domain data and retrain or fine-tune the model. However, high-quality 3D voxel-wise annotation is costly, time-consuming, and depen-dent on experienced clinical experts. This burden becomes more severe for multi-class volumetric segmentation, where small lesions, thin structures, and ambiguous boundaries require careful slice-wise inspection. Unsupervised domain adaptation (UDA), which leverages labeled source data together with unlabeled target data, has therefore become an important strategy for reducing annotation dependence and improving cross-domain generalization in medical image segmentation (Ben-David et al. 2010; Ganin et al. 2016; Long et al. 2015b; Saito et al. 2018; Tzeng et al. 2017). Despite substantial progress, existing UDA methods remain insufficiently reliable for complex 3D medical scenar-ios. First, image-level adaptation may compromise anatom-ical fidelity. Generative adversarial network (GAN)-based methods can translate source images into a target-like appear-ance (Chen et al. 2019; Dou et al. 2018; Goodfellow et al. 2014; Hoffman et al. 2018; Huo et al. 2019; Kamnitsas et al. 2017; Zhu et al. 2017), but they may entangle modality-specific style with anatomical structure, leading to boundary distortion, disruption of thin structures, topological inconsis-tency, and degraded inter-slice continuity. Frequency-domain adaptation offers a more interpretable alternative by manip-ulating spectral components related to image appearance (Yang and Soatto 2020). However, conventional Fourier-based spectrum exchange typically relies on hard cutoff win-dows, whose abrupt spectral transitions can introduce Gibbs ringing artifacts during reconstruction (Cooley and Tukey 1965; Harris 1978; Oppenheim and Schafer 2009). These artifacts contaminate high-frequency edges and local textures that are essential for accurate anatomical boundary modeling. Second, pseudo-label-based self-training is vulnerable to unreliable pseudo-label supervision on unlabeled target data. A teacher model is usually used to generate pseudo-labels for unlabeled target images, and a student model is then optimized with these predictions. The effectiveness of this paradigm is bounded by pseudo-label quality. Existing meth-ods often rely on single-model confidence or entropy for pseudo-label filtering (Grandvalet and Bengio 2005; Hin-ton et al. 2015; Lee 2013; Sohn et al. 2020; Tarvainen and Valpola 2017; Xie et al. 2020), but these criteria are insuffi-cient to distinguish aleatoric uncertainty caused by imaging ambiguity from epistemic uncertainty caused by limited model knowledge (Gal and Ghahramani 2016; Kendall and Gal 2017; Lakshminarayanan et al. 2017; Ovadia et al. 2019). In 3D multi-class segmentation, this limitation is amplified by severe class imbalance, small targets, long-tailed structures, and ambiguous boundaries. Once erroneous high-confidence predictions are repeatedly reinforced, con-firmation bias accumulates and restricts further improvement in clinically important regions. Third, most UDA methods adopt a static inference paradigm. After training, model parameters are fixed, and each test volume is segmented by a single forward pass. However, real clinical test streams often contain unseen anatomical variations, low-contrast boundaries, imaging arti-facts, and hard cases that are not fully covered during adaptation. Without test-time adaptation, the model cannot adjust its decision boundary according to the current input (Karani et al. 2021; Niu et al. 2022; Sun et al. 2020; Wang et al. 2021, 2022), which may lead to discontinuous con-tours, missing thin structures, or over-smoothed predictions in high-risk regions. This paper presents Spectral-Implicit Guidance for Multi-Teacher Adaptation (SIGMA), a unified integration frame-work for unsupervised cross-modality 3D medical image segmentation. The acronym SIGMA reflects the coordinated design of the framework: spectral guidance is implemented by RSSA, implicit guidance is realized by CIRA, and multi-teacher adaptation is achieved through RMTF. SIGMA inte-grates spectral-level data alignment, reliability-aware cross-domain pseudo-label supervision, and inference-time case-specific refinement within one target-label-free adaptation pipeline. At the data level, RSSA transfers modality-related low-frequency appearance while preserving high-frequency anatomical structures. At the supervision level, RMTF con-verts heterogeneous teacher disagreement into reliability-aware soft supervision. At the inference level, CIRA per-forms lightweight refinement over high-risk regions while keeping the segmentation backbone frozen. The contributions of this work lie in the coordinated design and evaluation of the following components within a unified adaptation framework: 

• Within the unified pipeline, Ringing-suppressed Soft Spectral Alignment (RSSA) uses a 3D Gaussian soft mask for smooth low-frequency appearance transfer and combines it with dual-stream spectral consistency to pre-serve volumetric structure.

## 2 Related work 

Unsupervised domain adaptation aims to transfer knowl-edge from labeled source-domain data Ds = {(xs , ys )}

to unlabeled target-domain data Dt = {xt } under dis-tribution shifts caused by differences in imaging physics, acquisition protocols, scanners, and patient populations (Ben-David et al. 2010; Ganin et al. 2016). For cross-modality 3D medical image segmentation, UDA is crucial for reducing target-domain annotation burden and improv-ing clinical generalization. Existing studies mainly involve domain alignment, pseudo-label learning, uncertainty-aware supervision, and test-time adaptation. Meanwhile, recent medical segmentation and neuro-oncology computer-aided diagnosis studies have expanded the design space beyond conventional encoder–decoder segmentation by exploring hierarchical graph attention, multimodal disentanglement, prompt-guided collaborative learning, modality-and-slice memory, and cross-stage feature aggregation (Xu et al. 2026; Chen et al. 2026; Xu et al. 2025; Luo et al. 2025; Liu et al. 2026). These recent paradigms provide useful context for structural modeling and multimodal representation learn-ing, while SIGMA focuses on the complementary problem of unsupervised cross-domain adaptation without target-domain labels. 

2.1 Domain alignment 

Feature-level alignment reduces source-target discrepan-cies in latent representations through adversarial learning, moment matching, or statistical regularization (Ganin et al. 2016; Long et al. 2015b; Saito et al. 2018; Tsai et al. 2018; Tzeng et al. 2017; Vu et al. 2019). Although effec-tive in learning domain-invariant features, such methods provide limited explicit control over fine anatomical bound-aries, local geometry, and topological consistency, which are essential for 3D voxel-wise segmentation. Image-level align-ment further narrows modality gaps by translating source images into target-like appearances (Chen et al. 2019; Dou et al. 2018; Hoffman et al. 2018; Huo et al. 2019; Zhu et al. 2017). Cycle-consistent translation and related methods can improve visual similarity, but in strongly heteroge-neous settings such as MRI-to-CT adaptation, appearance transfer may entangle modality-specific style with anatom-ical structure, causing boundary distortion, thin-structure alteration, and inter-slice inconsistency. Frequency-domain adaptation provides a more efficient and interpretable alter-native by manipulating spectral components associated with image appearance (Yang and Soatto 2020). However, most existing methods rely on hard low-frequency replacement, whose abrupt spectral transitions may introduce Gibbs ring-ing artifacts and contaminate high-frequency anatomical edges (Harris 1978; Oppenheim and Schafer 2009). Reli-able cross-modality 3D adaptation requires smooth spectral transfer while preserving structural fidelity. Recent multi-modal learning methods further highlight the importance of explicitly modeling modality-specific and modality-shared cues. For example, optimal-transport-guided multimodal dis-entanglement has been used to separate complementary diagnostic factors in leptomeningeal metastasis analysis (Chen et al. 2026), and modality-and-slice memory with category-agnostic prompting has been introduced for multi-modal brain tumor segmentation (Luo et al. 2025). Different from these supervised or task-specific multimodal designs, RSSA targets unlabeled source-to-target appearance align-ment while preserving the source anatomical phase structure. 

2.2 Pseudo-label learning and uncertainty-aware supervision 

Self-training is widely used for target-domain learning in medical UDA (Grandvalet and Bengio 2005; Lee 2013; 

# 123 773 Page 4 of 29 Journal of King Saud University Computer and Information Sciences (2026) 38:773 

Sohn et al. 2020; Tarvainen and Valpola 2017; Xie et al. 2020). A teacher model generates pseudo-labels for unla-beled target images, and a student model is optimized through teacher-student consistency. Mean-teacher strategies, con-fidence filtering, multi-scale consistency, and structural regularization have been introduced to improve pseudo-label stability. Beyond self-training, prompt-guided collaborative segmentation has recently shown that mutual prompts and cross-task guidance can strengthen contextual consistency across versatile medical segmentation tasks (Xu et al. 2025). However, such collaborative learning is usually developed under supervised or prompt-available settings, whereas UDA requires reliable supervision to be inferred from unlabeled target-domain volumes. Pseudo-label reliability remains a key bottleneck. Most methods rely on single-model confi-dence, entropy, or fixed thresholds, which are insufficient to distinguish aleatoric uncertainty caused by imaging ambi-guity from epistemic uncertainty caused by limited model knowledge. This issue is amplified in 3D multi-class seg-mentation, where anatomical structures and lesions are often imbalanced in size, morphology, and occurrence frequency. A single teacher may produce overconfident but erroneous predictions for small targets, long-tailed classes, or ambigu-ous boundaries. These errors can be repeatedly reinforced and evolve into confirmation bias. Multi-teacher disagree-ment offers a more reliable cue for epistemic uncertainty (Gal and Ghahramani 2016; Kendall and Gal 2017; Lak-shminarayanan et al. 2017; Ovadia et al. 2019), yet how to jointly exploit teacher consensus and disagreement for robust pseudo-label supervision on unlabeled target volumes remains underexplored. 

2.3 Test-time adaptation and implicit refinement 

Most UDA methods adapt the model during training and keep it fixed during inference. However, clinical test streams often contain unseen anatomical variations, low-contrast bound-aries, artifacts, and hard cases that are not fully covered during adaptation. Test-time adaptation (TTA) addresses this issue by updating model statistics or optimizing unsuper-vised objectives on incoming test samples (Karani et al. 2021; Niu et al. 2022; Sun et al. 2020; Wang et al. 2021, 2022). Although promising, updating the full segmentation network during inference can be computationally expen-sive and may cause parameter drift, unstable predictions, or structural collapse. Moreover, global TTA objectives usu-ally lack explicit anatomical constraints for local high-risk regions. Recent architectural advances have also improved structural representation through attention-based reasoning, including hierarchical graph attention networks for robust brain MRI lesion analysis (Xu et al. 2026) and cross-stage attention residuals that aggregate encoder–decoder features across multiple scales (Liu et al. 2026). These methods improve feed-forward feature modeling, whereas they do not directly address per-case unlabeled refinement after adaptation. Implicit neural representations (INRs) provide a potential solution for local refinement owing to their continuous coordinate-based modeling and high-frequency representation capacity (Mildenhall et al. 2020; Rahaman et al. 2019; Sitzmann et al. 2020; Tancik et al. 2020). Existing INR-based studies mainly focus on reconstruction, inter-polation, or shape modeling, while their use for unlabeled test-time segmentation refinement remains limited. 

2.4 Positioning of this work 

Existing medical UDA studies have improved either domain alignment, pseudo-label learning, or test-time robustness, but these components are typically developed in isolation. For cross-modality 3D segmentation, this separation leaves three coupled issues insufficiently addressed: ringing-suppressed appearance transfer, reliable pseudo-label supervision on unlabeled target volumes, and case-specific correction of residual local errors. SIGMA addresses this gap by coordi-nating RSSA, RMTF, and CIRA within a unified integration framework.

3.1 Ringing-suppressed soft spectral alignment and dual-stream distillation 

RSSA targets appearance differences represented primarily in low-frequency amplitudes while retaining source phase and high-frequency anatomical information. Conventional rectangular low-frequency replacement can produce sinc-like spatial responses and Gibbs ringing around strong edges. As shown in Fig. 2, RSSA instead applies a 3D fast Fourier transform (FFT) and a Gaussian soft mask to blend target-domain low-frequency amplitude into the source amplitude while preserving the source phase (Harris 1978; Oppenheim and Schafer 2009). Here, “ringing-suppressed” means that the smooth transition is designed to reduce, rather than eliminate, hard-cutoff-induced oscillatory arti-facts. 3.3 Cognitive implicit refinement adapter with spectral anchoring 

CIRA is an inference-time module for spatially localized residual errors. As shown in Fig. 3, the RMTF disagreement map U identifies high-risk regions, from which region-of-interest (ROI) patches and normalized 3D coordinate grids in

With the student backbone frozen, CIRA performs a small number of online updates that map coordinates to local feature-modulation parameters rather than directly modifying discrete labels. RSSA uses the 3D Gaussian mask M(k) and stochastic mix-ing coefficient λ ∼ Beta (α, β) . RMTF derives U from inter-teacher disagreement, generates the gated soft pseudo-label ¯P, and maps U to voxel-wise loss weights. At inference,

## 4 Experiments 

SIGMA is evaluated on two representative unsupervised 3D medical image segmentation adaptation tasks: BraTS2021-to-BraTS2023 SSA for brain tumor MRI adaptation across 

# 123 Journal of King Saud University Computer and Information Sciences (2026) 38:773 Page 11 of 29 773 

scanners, years, and patient cohorts, and MM-WHS 2017 MRI-to-CT for cross-modality cardiac multi-structure seg-mentation. The former assesses robustness under complex clinical distribution shifts, whereas the latter evaluates adap-tation under substantial differences in imaging physics. All experiments follow a strict unsupervised 3D domain adap-tation protocol, where only labeled source-domain data and unlabeled target-domain data are available during adaptation. Target-domain labels are used solely for final evaluation and are never involved in training, hyperparameter tuning, val-idation, or model selection. The stage-wise use of source training data, source validation data, and unlabeled target data follows Algorithm 1. The evaluation includes quantitative comparison, qualita-tive analysis, efficiency assessment, and ablation study. Main results are reported on both target tasks and compared with representative methods covering source-only training, adver-sarial domain alignment, pseudo-label-based self-training, and cross-modality structural modeling. Component-level ablations examine the individual and joint effects of RSSA, RMTF, and CIRA, as well as the trade-off among segmenta-tion accuracy, structural fidelity, and deployment cost. 

4.1 Datasets 

SIGMA is evaluated on two public 3D medical image seg-mentation benchmarks, covering clinically realistic MRI distribution shifts and strong cross-modality cardiac adap-tation. For the BraTS2021-to-BraTS2023 SSA task, BraTS2021 is used as the labeled source domain, containing 500 multi-modal brain tumor MRI volumes (Bakas et al. 2017; Menze et al. 2015), while BraTS2023 SSA is used as the unla-beled target domain for unsupervised adaptation and final testing. This task involves scanner-level, temporal, and population-level distribution shifts, thereby providing a real-istic evaluation of clinical domain shift caused by imaging platform variation, year-to-year protocol changes, and cohort heterogeneity. Each case contains four MRI modalities. After unified preprocessing, all volumes share consistent spatial attributes, with a volume size of 240 × 240 × 155, spacing of 1 .0 × 1.0 × 1.0, and aligned affine information. Following the standard BraTS evaluation protocol, segmentation per-formance is reported on three tumor regions: whole tumor (WT), tumor core (TC), and enhancing tumor (ET), where WT={1,2,3}, TC={1,3}, and ET={3}. For the MM-WHS 2017 MRI-to-CT task, the second evaluation setting focuses on cardiac multi-structure segmen-tation under substantial cross-modality shift. The MM-WHS 2017 dataset contains 20 MRI cases and 20 CT cases (Zhuang et al. 2019). MRI is used as the labeled source domain and CT as the unlabeled target domain. Unlike BraTS, where domain shift mainly arises from scanner, temporal, and cohort differences, MM-WHS emphasizes appearance and struc-tural representation discrepancies caused by fundamentally different imaging mechanisms. This task is therefore suitable for evaluating spectral modeling, pseudo-label robustness, and anatomical consistency preservation. Five label cate-gories are used: background (BG), ascending aorta (AA), left atrium cavity (LAC), left ventricle cavity (LVC), and myocardium (MYO). For both tasks, case-level splits are used for training, val-idation, and testing. Source-domain labels are available only for supervised source training and validation, whereas target-domain labels are strictly withheld during adaptation and model selection and are used solely for final reporting. For BraTS, stratified sampling is adopted to reduce imbalance across tumor sizes and difficult cases. 

4.2 Implementation details 

All experiments are implemented in PyTorch and conducted on a single NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 graphics processing unit (GPU). Unless otherwise specified, the batch size is set to 2, mixed precision training is used to improve memory efficiency, and the reference implementation uses Seed 42. The BraTS multi-seed analysis additionally evaluates Seeds 2024, 3407, and 2026 under the same data split, preprocess-ing, and evaluation protocol. For preprocessing, all volumes are normalized using non-zero z-score normalization, where the mean and standard deviation are computed only over the non-zero foreground region to avoid bias from empty background voxels. Full-volume input is used during both training and testing without additional organ-level cropping, preserving global 3D con-text and avoiding structural truncation caused by local crop-ping. For BraTS, the network input size is 4 ×240 ×240 ×155. The student segmentation network is implemented as a lightweight 3D U-Net (Çiçek et al. 2016) with a base chan-nel number of 32. Adam is used as the optimizer, with an initial learning rate of 1 × 10 −3, weight decay of 1 × 10 −5,and a total training length of 150 epochs. The learning rate is scheduled using CosineAnnealingLR with a minimum learn-ing rate of 1 ×10 −5. For BraTS, model selection is performed on the labeled source-domain validation split, and the vali-dation score is defined as the average regional Dice: Score = WT + TC + ET 3 . (22) For MM-WHS, the best checkpoint is selected according to the mean Dice over the four foreground cardiac structures on the source-domain validation split. The use of the source validation split is confined to checkpoint and hyperparameter selection under the standard UDA setting; these validation

labels are not used for gradient updates, and no target-domain labels are used for training, validation, or model selection. Table 1 summarizes the teacher, student, and CIRA configurations used in SIGMA. The teacher committee is heterogeneous at the construction level but produces proba-bility maps in a shared label space. The style teacher and the deployment student use the same lightweight 3D U-Net family for fair backbone control, whereas the structure teacher uses an independent residual 3D encoder–decoder proxy to provide complementary anatomical priors. The diversity teacher is generated from existing checkpoints and lightweight perturbation variants rather than from a sepa-rately trained full network. During target-domain student adaptation, the student net-work takes only unlabeled target-domain samples as input and is optimized using detached fused soft pseudo-label supervision and dual-stream spectral consistency constraints. Source-domain labels are used for source-supervised teacher construction and source validation, but they are not included in the student adaptation mini-batches or adaptation loss. For MM-WHS, the reported metrics are AA, LAC, LVC, MYO, mean Dice, mean average surface distance (ASD), and RAVD. Together, these metrics provide a multi-metric evaluation of regional accuracy, boundary stability, and vol-ume consistency in 3D cross-domain segmentation.

4.8 Ablation and sensitivity analysis 

Module-level ablation studies quantify the individual con-tributions of the core components in SIGMA. All abla-tion settings use the same backbone, optimizer, learning-rate schedule, training epochs, and inference configura-tion unless a component is explicitly modified. Five vari-ants are evaluated: (1) w/o reliability-aware pseudo-label supervision, where fused cross-domain pseudo-labels and uncertainty-modulated loss on unlabeled target volumes are removed; (2) single-teacher pseudo-labeling, where cross-domain pseudo-label supervision on unlabeled target volumes is preserved but the reliability-aware multi-teacher gated fusion is replaced by a single pseudo-label source; (3) w/o RSSA, where the ringing-suppressed soft spec-tral alignment module is removed while the cross-domain pseudo-label supervision pathway is retained; (4) w/o CIRA, where inference-time high-risk ROI refinement is disabled; and (5) the full SIGMA model. Here, pseudo-label super-vision is generated by source/RSSA-trained teachers and applied to unlabeled target volumes; no target-domain anno-tations are used for teacher training, student adaptation, model selection, or any ablation setting. The “w/o reliability-aware pseudo-label supervision” variant is a lower-bound supervision ablation because it removes both fused soft pseudo-labels generated by the teacher commit-tee and the uncertainty-modulated loss applied to unlabeled target volumes, rather than only changing the fusion rule. Their integration yields a closed-loop adaptation framework that improves seg-mentation accuracy and boundary stability under complex 3D domain shifts. 

4.9 RSSA Window-function and bandwidth analysis 

The ringing-suppression behavior of RSSA is evaluated by comparing the proposed Gaussian soft mask with an FDA-style rectangular hard cutoff and two classical tapered windows, Hann and Kaiser. The rectangular hard-cutoff variant performs abrupt low-frequency spectrum replace-ment and serves as the hard-mask spectral exchange control, whereas Gaussian RSSA uses a continuous soft transi-tion between injected low-frequency target appearance and preserved high-frequency source structure. All window functions are evaluated under the same frequency band-width, source–target pairing, amplitude-mixing coefficient, and reconstruction protocol. The controlled setting isolates the effect of the spectral transition profile from other training and data-processing factors. Reconstruction quality is eval-uated using four indicators: ringing index (RI), edge-region reconstruction error (EdgeErr), structural similarity (SSIM), and peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR). RI measures oscilla-tory artifacts around high-gradient regions and is reported in 10 −2 scale; EdgeErr measures local error in edge-dominant regions and reflects boundary contamination; SSIM and PSNR evaluate global structural and intensity fidelity. The Gaussian bandwidth σ is then analyzed to characterize the balance between target-style reconstruction and high-frequency structural preservation. Slice-wise 2D FFT RSSA and volumetric 3D FFT RSSA are also compared to examine whether spectral alignment is better performed indepen-dently on each slice or jointly in the 3D frequency domain. As shown in Table 15, Gaussian RSSA achieves the lowest RI (2 .01 × 10 −2) and EdgeErr (1.91), while also obtain-ing the highest SSIM (0.83) and PSNR (27.49). Compared with the FDA-style rectangular hard cutoff, Gaussian RSSA reduces RI from 2 .48 × 10 −2 to 2 .01 × 10 −2 and EdgeErr from 2.47 to 1.91, indicating lower ringing intensity and less edge-region contamination. The rectangular hard cutoff preserves more high-frequency energy than overly tapered windows but produces a discontinuous spectral transition, which increases oscillatory artifacts around sharp anatom-ical edges. Hann and Kaiser windows provide smoother transitions than the rectangular cutoff, but in this setting they attenuate boundary-relevant high-frequency compo-nents more aggressively, leading to larger edge errors and lower PSNR. In contrast, the Gaussian mask provides a smooth isotropic decay from low to high frequencies, thereby reducing hard-cutoff-induced artifacts while preserving suf-ficient high-frequency anatomical information.

A very small bandwidth, such as σ = 0.05, imposes a restrictive spectral constraint and minimizes high-frequency leakage, but it under-transfers target-domain appearance, resulting in lower SSIM and PSNR. Slice-wise 2D FFT performs spec-tral alignment independently for each axial slice, whereas volumetric 3D FFT models in-plane and through-plane fre-quency components jointly. This distinction is relevant for 3D medical segmentation because slice-wise processing may create inconsistent spectral transfer across adjacent slices, especially near thin or oblique anatomical boundaries. The 2D variant obtains higher SSIM, suggesting that independent slice-wise spectral alignment can preserve certain in-plane structural similarity. However, this comes with larger ringing vari-ability, higher edge-region error, and weaker through-plane consistency. ## 5 Conclusion 

Spectral-Implicit Guidance for Multi-Teacher Adaptation (SIGMA) is a unified integration framework for unsuper-vised cross-modality 3D medical image segmentation. It coordinates RSSA for smooth spectral appearance align-ment, RMTF for reliability-aware cross-domain pseudo-label fusion, and CIRA for optional high-risk ROI refinement at inference time. The methodological contribution lies in coordinating these complementary mechanisms within a con-sistent target-label-free pipeline. On BraTS2021-to-BraTS2023 SSA, the paired analysis supports higher overlap and improved volume consistency relative to the principal paired references, while HD95 differ-ences are interpreted according to their reported significance levels. On MM-WHS 2017 MRI-to-CT, SIGMA obtains the highest average mean Dice among the evaluated methods and competitive boundary accuracy, but the paired comparisons do not reach statistical significance because of the limited evaluation cohort. Case-wise analysis, multi-seed evaluation, internally implemented diagnostic controls, and failure-case inspection provide complementary evidence and clarify the remaining sensitivity to small separated components and hard outlier cases. The RSSA, RMTF, and CIRA analyses further characterize the roles and operating trade-offs of the three integrated components. Several limitations remain. Disagreement-based uncer-tainty may fail when all teachers make the same high-confidence error, because such collective errors produce low inter-teacher disagreement. In addition, CIRA is designed for local ROI refinement under a plausible coarse predic-tion and is not a full re-segmentation mechanism; severe global topological errors in the initial prediction may not be fully corrected by the low-frequency anchored refinement objective. Future work will investigate adaptive uncertainty estimation, more diverse teacher construction, dynamic spec-tral anchoring, and integration with pretrained volumetric foundation models for cross-domain clinical deployment.
