Title: Understanding and Mitigating Distribution Shifts in Volumetric Lung Nodule CAD Using a 3D Vision-Language Framework

URL Source: https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10278-026-02196-4

Published Time: Tue, 23 Jun 2026 02:06:19 GMT

## Abstract

As artificial intelligence becomes increasingly integrated into medical imaging practice, its robustness across heterogeneous real-world settings remains a major challenge. We quantified the effect of real-world distribution shifts on three-dimensional AI models for lung nodule analysis on CT and, motivated by these shifts, developed and evaluated MedStyle-3DG, an open-source 3D vision-language framework for domain generalization. This retrospective multicenter study assembled 2679 chest CTs acquired from 2010 to 2025, with institutional review approval and waiver of informed consent. The study was designed in two stages: first, to quantify the extent of performance degradation from in-distribution (ID) to out-of-distribution (OOD), under clinically realistic distribution shifts; and second, to test whether a domain generalization strategy can mitigate these gaps. Data were split into training, validation, test-ID, and test-OOD to model three shifts: exposure variations, device manufacturer, and geographic changes. We then proposed MedStyle-3DG, a 3D vision-language domain generalization framework combining feature statistics mixing, stochastic weight averaging, vision-language alignment, and a three-branch ensemble. OOD F1 score and the gaps between test-ID and test-OOD were the primary outcomes. The 3D ResNet50 baseline showed consistent ID-OOD degradation across all shifts, largest for exposure (F1, 0.630 vs 0.521; gap, 10.9 percentage points; _P_< .001). MedStyle-3DG improved exposure OOD F1 to 0.601 and reduced the gap to 7.7 percentage points, with similar improvements for manufacturer and geographic shifts. Across all three factors, MedStyle-3DG achieved state-of-the-art performance in mean OOD F1 (_P_< .001) and reduction of the F1 generalization gap (_P_< .041). Real-world protocol, vendor, and population shifts substantially degrade volumetric lung nodule CAD performance. MedStyle-3DG reduces generalization gaps and its code is available at[https://github.com/RafaelMedelean/MedCLIP-3DG](https://github.com/RafaelMedelean/MedCLIP-3DG).

2.   2.

3.   3.

## Materials and Methods

### Data

Since no public benchmark currently exists for the 3D medical imaging domain generalization, we curated an intentionally heterogeneous dataset of 2679 chest CTs (Fig.[1](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10278-026-02196-4#Fig1)). We chose this hybrid approach for multiple reasons. Public datasets alone lacked sufficient diversity or key metadata for in-depth exploration of domain generalization. Second, proprietary data enabled controlled distribution shifts necessary for our experimental design. Third, all data had to be annotated by a cohesive radiology team, minimizing confounding from concept (label-definition) shift, ensuring that observed differences reflect and isolate a covariate distribution shift rather than labeling inconsistencies.

### Generalization Setup

We explored generalization using a rigorous four-way data split framework designed to test real-world deployment scenarios, illustrated in Fig.[2](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10278-026-02196-4#Fig2). This mixed-to-test approach, based on WILDS [[25](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10278-026-02196-4#ref-CR25 "P. W. Koh, S. Sagawa, H. Marklund, S. M. Xie, M. Zhang, A. Balsubramani, W. Hu, M. Yasunaga, R. L. Phillips, I. Gao, T. Lee, E. David, I. Stavness, W. Guo, B. A. Earnshaw, I. S. Haque, S. Beery, J. Leskovec, A. Kundaje, E. Pierson, S. Levine, C. Finn, P. Liang, WILDS: A Benchmark of in-the-Wild Distribution Shifts, arXiv [cs.LG] (2020). 
                http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.07421
                
              .")], separates data into training, validation, test-in-distribution (test-ID), and test-out-of-distribution (test-OOD) subsets. While training and validation represent the source distribution (seen during development), the test-ID and test-OOD represent a never-seen data distribution (e.g., a different country). The framework enables a two-stage evaluation:

1.   I.
First, we train a model on the training subset while monitoring and choosing the best checkpoint on the validation set. The best threshold for computing the F1 score is also tuned on the validation set. We then measure the performance on test-OOD, which is sampled from the target distribution—this will tell the absolute performance of the model as impacted by distribution shift.

2.   II.
Second, we merge training and test-ID data for model retraining. This way, we inject the target data distribution into training, eliminating the generalization obstacle. All domains are now in-distribution domains. We then measure the performance on the same test-OOD—this will be the performance of an equivalent model, not impacted anymore by the previous shift.

**Fig.2**

Mixed-to-test evaluation protocol for domain generalization. Training and validation subsets comprise multiple source domains. Test-OOD is drawn from an unseen target domain to measure out-of-distribution performance. To contextualize OOD performance, we retrain with the target-domain training subset (test-ID) merged into training while maintaining the same validation set, then re-evaluate on the same test-OOD set to estimate an in-distribution reference and compute the generalization gap. All splits are patient-disjoint

This two-stage approach reveals critical insights. The first stage shows pure out-of-distribution performance, while the second stage sets an in-distribution reference—putting OOD performance in context. The difference between the two is the generalization gap, which is now quantifiable.

We composed individual splits for each shift factor we planned to study: geography, exposure, and device (Fig.[3](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10278-026-02196-4#Fig3)). Within each split, all subsets maintained strict patient-level separation to avoid patient leakage.

**Fig.3**

Exploring three domain shift factors through separate data splits. The diagram illustrates the main data splits used to explore model performance under three distinct distribution shifts. (Experiment **A**) The exposure shift evaluates models trained on standard-higher-mAs (mean 152.7 mAs) against lower-mAs (mean 34.4 mAs). (Experiment **B**) The manufacturer shift separates vendors, training on Siemens, Neusoft, and Canon scanners while testing on unseen GE and Philips systems. (Experiment **C**) The geographic shift tests generalization from European populations (training) to US populations (testing), encompassing a natural drop in nodule prevalence from 13.78 to 6.02%. This design isolates specific generalization gaps by maintaining strict patient-level separation and controlled distribution shifts

**The exposure split** evaluates whether changes in tube current–time product affect model generalization. The training and validation sets contained higher-exposure scans as defined by mAs (60–465 mAs, mean 152.7 mAs), whereas test-ID and test-OOD contained lower-exposure scans as defined by mAs (15–50 mAs, mean 34.4 mAs), corresponding to an approximately 77% reduction in tube current–time product. Because CTDIvol and DLP were not consistently available, this split is interpreted as an mAs-defined exposure shift rather than a direct CTDIvol/DLP-based radiation-dose comparison.

**The manufacturer split** evaluates whether a CAD maintains accuracy across different scanner brands. The training and validation sets included three manufacturers (i.e., Siemens, Neusoft, Canon), while test-ID and test-OOD used the other two (i.e., GE HealthCare, Philips).

**The geographic split** tests whether models generalize across different patient populations, crucial for health equity and global deployment. The training and validation sets comprised European examinations from five countries (Romania, Germany, Croatia, Italy, Greece), while test-ID and test-OOD included only the USA, creating a transcontinental shift. Unlike the exposure and manufacturer experiments, which isolate individual factors under maximally contrasted conditions, this composite split preserves the naturally co-occurring variation encountered in transcontinental deployment: differences in patient demographics, a reduction in nodule prevalence, and variation in scanner fleet and acquisition protocols across different healthcare systems. These co-occurring factors were preserved intentionally to complement the single-factor experiments with a realistic multi-factor evaluation, noting that compound shifts do not necessarily produce additive degradation, as overlapping factors may interact in complex ways.

Because the false-positive reduction task operates on pre-extracted candidate patches as a binary classification problem rather than as scan-level localization, we adopted the F1 score as the primary evaluation metric. F1 captures the precision-recall trade-off at a fixed operating point, penalizing both missed nodules and unnecessary false alarms equally, both of which carry clinical consequences. Importantly, the primary quantity of interest in this study is not absolute F1 performance but the generalization gap \(\left(\Delta =F{1}_{\left\{ID\right\}}-F{1}_{\left\{OOD\right\}}\right)\) which measures how much a model’s performance degrades when exposed to an unseen distribution. This relative measure helps contextualize the effect of domain shift by comparing the same model under matched versus unmatched conditions, and is a standard evaluation approach in domain generalization benchmarks.

For all distribution shift analyses, we used a 3D ResNet50 [[26](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10278-026-02196-4#ref-CR26 "K. He, X. Zhang, S. Ren, J. Sun, “Deep residual learning for image recognition” in 2016 IEEE Conference on Computer Vision and Pattern Recognition (CVPR) (IEEE, 2016), pp. 770–778.")] baseline, with more details in the Online Resource.

### MedStyle-3DG: Training Framework for 3D Medical Imaging Generalization

To combat distribution shifts, we developed a generalization training framework that integrates Contrastive Language-Image Pre-training (CLIP) [[27](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10278-026-02196-4#ref-CR27 "A. Radford, J. W. Kim, C. Hallacy, A. Ramesh, G. Goh, S. Agarwal, G. Sastry, A. Askell, P. Mishkin, J. Clark, G. Krueger, I. Sutskever, Learning transferable visual models from natural language supervision, arXiv [cs.CV] (2021). 
                http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.00020
                
              .")] with specialized medical imaging domain generalization techniques. The framework operates through a three-phase sequential training pipeline, with each phase addressing complementary aspects of domain generalization.

Phase 1 trains a 3D vision encoder from scratch using style mixing [[28](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10278-026-02196-4#ref-CR28 "K. Zhou, Y. Yang, Y. Qiao, T. Xiang, Domain Generalization with MixStyle, arXiv [cs.CV] (2021). 
                http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.02008
                
              .")] for feature-level domain augmentation and a dense and overfit-aware stochastic weight sampling strategy, as proposed by [[29](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10278-026-02196-4#ref-CR29 "J. Cha, S. Chun, K. Lee, H.-C. Cho, S. Park, Y. Lee, S. Park, SWAD: Domain generalization by seeking flat minima, arXiv [cs.LG] (2021). 
                http://arxiv.org/abs/2102.08604
                
              ."), [30](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10278-026-02196-4#ref-CR30 "P. Izmailov, D. Podoprikhin, T. Garipov, D. Vetrov, A. G. Wilson, Averaging weights leads to wider optima and better generalization, arXiv [cs.LG] (2018). 
                https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.1803.05407
                
              .")], aiming for robust feature extraction that handles appearance variations from scanner differences and imaging protocols. Phase 2 aligns the Phase 1 frozen vision backbone with a frozen pretrained medical text encoder (BiomedVLP-CXR-BERT [[31](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10278-026-02196-4#ref-CR31 "B. Boecking, N. Usuyama, S. Bannur, D. C. Castro, A. Schwaighofer, S. Hyland, M. Wetscherek, T. Naumann, A. Nori, J. Alvarez-Valle, H. Poon, O. Oktay, “Making the most of text semantics to improve biomedical vision–language processing” in Lecture Notes in Computer Science (Springer Nature Switzerland, Cham, 2022)Lecture notes in computer science, pp. 1–21.")]) using template-based synthetic prompts (e.g., “A lung nodule is present in this CT scan”) rather than free-text clinical reports. Neither encoder is fine-tuned; only the projection layers mapping features to a shared 512-dimensional latent space are trained contrastively. Beyond its direct contribution to classification, this alignment serves an architectural role: by creating a joint vision-language embedding space, it enables Phase 3 to apply text-based style removal methods to visual representations. Phase 3 employs a style generation and removal module inspired by [[32](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10278-026-02196-4#ref-CR32 "32. Y. Tang, Y. Wan, L. Qi, X. Geng, DPStyler: Dynamic PromptStyler for source-free domain generalization. IEEE Trans. Multimedia 27, 120–132 (2025).")] to dynamically mix frozen domain descriptor embeddings grounded in actual training domain characteristics. Figure[4](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10278-026-02196-4#Fig4)illustrates the three-phase architecture, which is further described in more detail:

**Fig.4**

MedStyle-3DG training framework. The schematic depicts the three-phase sequential training pipeline. (Phase 1) A 3D ResNet50 visual encoder is trained from scratch using MixStyle for feature-level domain augmentation and stochastic weight averaging (SWAD) to identify robust minima. (Phase 2) The pretrained visual encoder is frozen and aligned with a frozen medical text encoder (BiomedVLP-CXR-BERT) using a contrastive loss to create a joint semantic embedding space. (Phase 3) A style removal module is trained to suppress domain-specific artifacts along with a classifier following DPStyler

### Phase 1—Visual encoder training

MixStyle performs feature-level domain augmentation by mixing channel-wise statistics (mean and standard deviation) between patch-level training samples. MixStyle is applied only during the training and is disabled during validation and testing. Next, the SWAD optimization strategy identifies and averages model weights corresponding to flat regions of the loss landscape, which correlate with better generalization to distribution shifts. Unlike standard stochastic weight averaging, which averages a fixed set of checkpoints, SWAD performs dense model averaging once training enters a convergence valley. The combination of MixStyle and SWAD addresses orthogonal aspects of domain generalization: MixStyle enables the model to learn representations robust to scanner manufacturer differences, imaging protocol variations, and exposure-related appearance shifts through feature-level augmentation, while SWAD ensures robust model selection by averaging weights from flat loss landscape regions rather than selecting a single checkpoint, reducing sensitivity to validation set characteristics.

### Phase 2—Vision-language alignment

Using the first phase’s pretrained 3D ResNet50 visual feature extractor, the second phase combines it with a pretrained BiomedVLP-CXR-BERT to create the multimodal CLIP-style joint embedding space through a frozen encoder strategy. This is to preserve feature representation power and to train the model faster and with fewer computational resources, without requiring a massive CT-text pair collection. Both encoders remain frozen while the projection layers that map features to a shared 512-dimensional latent space are trained contrastively. This strategy leverages the complementary strengths of domain-invariant visual features from the first phase and rich medical semantic space from the pretrained text encoder, which theory and recent empirical evidence [[33](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10278-026-02196-4#ref-CR33 "T. Wang, P. Isola, Understanding contrastive representation learning through alignment and uniformity on the hypersphere, arXiv [cs.LG] (2020). 
                https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2005.10242
                
              ."),[34](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10278-026-02196-4#ref-CR34 "M. Maniparambil, R. Akshulakov, Y. A. D. Djilali, S. Narayan, A. Singh, N. E. O’Connor, Harnessing frozen unimodal encoders for flexible multimodal alignment, arXiv [cs.CV] (2024). 
                https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2409.19425
                
              ."),[35](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10278-026-02196-4#ref-CR35 "Z. Wang, Z. Wu, D. Agarwal, J. Sun, MedCLIP: Contrastive learning from unpaired medical images and text. Proc. Conf. Empir. Methods Nat. Lang. Process. 2022, 3876–3887 (2022).")] suggest improved robustness.

Training data consists of cropped 3D CT patches paired with generated text prompts. For patches containing nodules (positive samples), simple, synthetic prompts provide basic statements such as “A lung nodule is present in this CT scan,” while complex prompts incorporate radiological attributes: “A lung nodule is present in this CT scan with solid texture, spiculated margin, and no calcification.” For patches without nodules (negative samples), both simple and complex prompts state “A lung nodule is not present in this CT scan.” Text augmentation was applied during training to increase prompt diversity through formulation variations, phrase replacements, and synonym substitutions.

### Phase 3—Style removal module

After Phase 2 creates the vision-language joint space, we go one step forward and add a style removal module by fitting a Style-SE (_32_) adapter and classifier on top of the frozen text encoder. Leveraging the aligned embedding space, we simulate domain shifts by synthetically injecting various styles (e.g., “Reduced-Exposure,” “Siemens”) into the nodule text prompts. The objective combines ArcFace Loss on the nodule label with an additional domain-uncertainty loss. The uncertainty loss minimizes the mutual information between the feature and the style definitions, forcing the Style-SE output to look equally likely across the split’s style descriptors. Crucially, while this module is trained on stylized text embeddings, it learns to suppress style-correlated channels in the joint embedding space. After training, the Style-SE adapter and classifier are transplanted to the image encoder for inference.

To further maximize robustness, we employ a three-branch ensemble at inference time (Fig.[5](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10278-026-02196-4#Fig5)). This fuses complementary decision boundaries: a stable semantic baseline, a texture-regularized visual classifier, and a diversity-aware consensus mechanism. The final prediction is a weighted integration of these three pathways.

**Fig.5**

Three-branch inference ensemble. A 3D CT candidate patch is encoded into a shared embedding space. Branch 1 computes cosine similarity to canonical positive/negative prompts (semantic anchor). Branch 2 applies the trained Style-SE module to suppress domain-correlated channels before classification (style-suppressed visual branch). Branch 3 evaluates similarity against _K_ = 20 source-consensus style-injected prompts sampled from mixtures of training domain descriptors (source-consensus branch). Final probability is a weighted combination with weights selected on the validation set

### Base visual-language branch (\({{\varvec{\alpha}}}_{{\varvec{b}}{\varvec{a}}{\varvec{s}}{\varvec{e}}}\)**)**

The first branch serves as a stable baseline designed to be invariant across all experimental settings. It utilizes the frozen text encoder to compute the cosine similarity between the test image embedding and a fixed set of canonical positive and negative prompts (e.g., “A lung nodule is present” vs. “No nodule is present”). Unlike the adaptive branches described below, this branch employs strictly prompts derived from the vision-language alignment phase. By excluding trainable parameters, style injection, or adapter modules, this branch provides a stable but reliable “semantic anchor.”

### Style-suppressed visual-only branch (\({{\varvec{\alpha}}}_{{\varvec{v}}{\varvec{i}}{\varvec{s}}{\varvec{u}}{\varvec{a}}{\varvec{l}}}\))

The second branch focuses on extracting non-spurious signals by actively suppressing domain-related artifacts. The nodule patch is encoded by the frozen 3D ResNet50 and projection module. This embedding is then processed by the Style-SE module trained in Phase 3. The Style-SE mechanism works as a channel-wise attention filter, dynamically down-weighting feature channels that correlate with domain-specific spurious cues (such as the specific noise texture of a reduced-exposure scan or a manufacturer's reconstruction kernel). The filtered embedding is passed to the classifier to produce a probability score, promoting non-spurious cues rather than acquisition characteristics.

### Source-consensus visual-language branch (\({{\varvec{\alpha}}}_{{\varvec{s}}{\varvec{t}}{\varvec{y}}{\varvec{e}}}\))

The third branch addresses the “unseen domain” problem by evaluating the test image against the diverse distribution of known source domains. We sample _K_ = 20 style descriptors that represent randomized combinations of the known source domains found in the training set. For example, in the manufacturer experiment, these descriptors are not limited to single vendors but are sampled as mixtures of Siemens, Canon, and Neusoft characteristics. These combinatorial descriptors are injected into the prompt template “A lung nodule in a [STYLE] style” and encoded into text embeddings. We calculate the similarity between the test image and each of these diverse source-style prompts, averaging the resulting probabilities to obtain a source-consensus score. This logic ensures that a test sample is classified as a positive nodule if it aligns semantically with the nodule concept across the robust range of training domain variations.

To account for the differing dynamic ranges between the classifier logits (Branch 2) and the cosine similarity distributions (branches 1 and 3), the weighting coefficients are determined via a grid search on the validation set for each specific experimental split (using the validation subset only). This empirical optimization ensures the ensemble is calibrated to the specific difficulty of the distribution shift, balancing the stability of the anchor with the specificity of the adaptive branches.

The final model is composed using a greedy model soup [[36](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10278-026-02196-4#ref-CR36 "M. Wortsman, G. Ilharco, S. Y. Gadre, R. Roelofs, R. Gontijo-Lopes, A. S. Morcos, H. Namkoong, A. Farhadi, Y. Carmon, S. Kornblith, L. Schmidt, Model soups: averaging weights of multiple fine-tuned models improves accuracy without increasing inference time, arXiv [cs.LG] (2022). 
                http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.05482
                
              .")] made of the five best checkpoint candidates (i.e., highest F1 scores) of each single run. The final probability _P_ is computed as the weighted sum of these three decision boundaries:

$${P}_{final}={\alpha}_{base}{P}_{base}+ {\alpha}_{visual}{P}_{visual}+ {\alpha}_{style}{P}_{style}$$

### Statistical Analysis

For each method and each distribution shift split, we evaluated five seed-level runs. The primary endpoint was out-of-distribution F1 score. The secondary endpoint was the ID-OOD F1 generalization gap. The F1 operating threshold was selected on the validation set and then kept fixed for test-ID and test-OOD evaluation. For each method, metrics were summarized as the mean across the five seed-level runs.

Uncertainty was estimated using a nonparametric two-level bootstrap with 10,000 replicates. In each bootstrap replicate, patients were resampled with replacement separately within the test-ID and test-OOD subsets, and seed-level runs were resampled with replacement from the five available seeds. The same patient and seed resampling draws were applied across all methods within each split, preserving the paired structure of method comparisons. For each replicate, patient-level true positives, false positives, and false negatives were aggregated before recomputing F1. AUROC and AUPRC (in [Appendix](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10278-026-02196-4#MOESM1)) were also recomputed using patient-level bootstrap weights. The mean across the three shift experiments was computed within each bootstrap replicate by averaging the corresponding replicate-level estimates across the geographic, manufacturer, and exposure splits.

To assess whether the baseline 3D ResNet50 model exhibited significant degradation under distribution shift, we estimated 95% bootstrap confidence intervals for the ID-OOD F1 gap in each split and for the mean gap across the three shifts. Two-sided bootstrap _P_ values were calculated.

Primary method comparisons were prespecified for MedStyle-3DG against the 3D ResNet50 baseline and the three strongest competing vision-language methods: PromptTA, DPStyler, and PromptStyler. For OOD F1, the treatment effect was defined as the difference in mean OOD F1 between MedStyle-3DG and the comparator. For generalization-gap reduction, the treatment effect was defined as \({\Delta}_{comparator}-{\Delta}_{MedStyle-3DG}\) so that positive values indicate a smaller ID-OOD gap for MedStyle-3DG. Bootstrap confidence intervals and two-sided bootstrap _P_ values were calculated for each comparison. Holm correction was applied across the eight primary comparisons. Statistical significance was defined as Holm-adjusted \(P<.05\). Analyses were performed in Python using NumPy, pandas, and custom bootstrap code.

### Exposure shift (standard-exposure to reduced-exposure)

In the most challenging setting, MedStyle-3DG achieved OOD F1 = 0.601 (0.678 on test-ID), improving OOD F1 by 0.080 over the baseline and by 0.017–0.027 over the strongest prompt/DG competitors. ### Geographic shift (Europe to the USA)

MedStyle-3DG reached an OOD F1 of 0.519 (0.564 on test-ID), outperforming the plain ResNet50 by 0.073 in OOD F1 and surpassing the strongest prompt-based baseline by about 0.026–0.028. The ID-OOD gap was reduced to 0.045 compared with 0.09 for the baseline, indicating improved transfer under combined prevalence and population differences.

## Discussion

In this study, we address a practical deployment problem in volumetric lung nodule CAD: performance can drop materially when a model is moved across clinically common distribution shifts, scanner vendor, acquisition protocol, and patient population, even when the underlying task is unchanged. We therefore (i) quantified these generalization failures in a controlled, clinically realistic way across three shifts (exposure, manufacturer, geography) and (ii) propose a concrete mitigation strategy. Specifically, we introduce MedStyle-3DG, a domain generalization framework designed to reduce sensitivity to acquisition and site-specific style without requiring site-specific retraining, by combining robust feature learning, vision-language alignment, and an inference-time consensus mechanism. This synergistic approach substantially reduced the generalization gap.

### Susceptibility to distribution shifts

Our systematic investigation isolates the exposure shift (higher-mAs to lower-mAs) as the most severe source of performance degradation in volumetric medical imaging. As quantified in our results, the drop in exposure induces a drastic covariate shift. Consequently, the 3D baseline model exhibited the largest generalization gap. This vulnerability is particularly critical given the pervasive nature of exposure variance. This shift is not limited to macroscopic differences between geographic regions or distinct hospital protocols; it manifests acutely within single institutions between diagnostic and screening cohorts, and could even emerge as a temporal drift on identical scanning equipment when acquisition parameters are adjusted by technical staff over time. The manufacturer shift, while producing a smaller gap, similarly indicates that augmentations and standardization alone do not eliminate vendor-specific reconstruction signatures from learned representations. Unlike the exposure and manufacturer experiments, which isolate individual shift factors under maximally contrasted conditions, the geographic split captures the compound variation inherent in cross-site deployment, including differences in patient demographics, disease prevalence, scanner fleet, and acquisition practices. Because co-occurring shifts may partially overlap or interact non-additively, a composite scenario does not necessarily produce a larger generalization gap than a controlled single-factor experiment designed to maximize contrast along one axis. This design reflects the practical question facing CAD deployment: whether a model trained in one clinical ecosystem can sustain performance when transferred to another, where multiple factors shift simultaneously. That MedStyle-3DG reduced the generalization gap from 0.090 to 0.045 in this multi-factor scenario suggests the framework provides robustness that extends beyond controlled single-factor settings.

### Overcoming the performance-robustness trade-off via multimodal alignment

Addressing the exposure shift did not result in the “robustness-performance trade-off” frequently observed in domain generalization literature, where invariance constraints typically dampen performance on the source distribution. Instead, our framework demonstrates that MedStyle-3DG can yield holistic performance gains. Our proposed framework not only compressed the generalization gap on the exposure split but also elevated In-Distribution performance.

### Algorithmic regularization vs. data scaling: implications for sample efficiency

Our findings challenge the prevailing assumption that simply scaling dataset size is sufficient to mitigate these distributional shifts. Paradoxically, our scaling experiments reveal that tripling the training data volume caused the generalization gap of the ResNet baseline to widen. This could hint that without explicit regularization against domain-specific styles, standard models tend to overfit the specifics of the larger source dataset. Conversely, MedStyle-3DG successfully leveraged the additional data to reduce the gap. This leads to a critical observation regarding sample efficiency: our proposed framework achieved superior OOD robustness with 1 × data than the standard model achieved with 3 × data, hinting that robustness is a prerequisite for effective data scaling.

### Limitations and future research

A limitation of the current framework is its reliance on template-based prompts during vision-language alignment, which provide useful semantic anchors but do not capture the richness and variability of real radiology language; future work shall explore integrating de-identified clinical reports to better encode nuanced descriptors (e.g., morphology and interval change) and potentially improve performance. Additionally, the study did not discriminate between the F1 cutoff calibration drift and representational drift as the upstream source of performance degradation. Both could be factors involved in real-world deployment, contributing to generalization problems in currently unknown degrees. Another apparent limitation is that we focus on domain generalization (DG) rather than explicitly benchmarking domain adaptation (DA) approaches; however, while DA can be attractive in controlled settings, it typically presupposes access to target-domain and repeated recalibration, which is often operationally and regulatorily impractical across large clinical networks. In that context, DA experiments remained out of scope. Finally, future research should examine the transferability of the proposed DG framework to other 3D imaging tasks beyond pulmonary nodules (e.g., lymph node detection, MRI, segmentation).

## Conclusions

This study demonstrates that clinically realistic distribution shifts, including acquisition protocol, scanner manufacturer, and geographic population differences, can substantially degrade the performance of 3D lung nodule CAD models. Among the evaluated shifts, reduced-exposure CT produced the largest generalization gap, highlighting the need for systematic robustness testing beyond aggregate performance on static test sets.

To address this challenge, we introduced MedStyle-3DG, a 3D vision-language domain generalization framework that combines robust visual feature learning, vision-language alignment, style suppression, and ensemble-based inference. Across all evaluated shifts, MedStyle-3DG achieved the best mean OOD F1 and mean F1 generalization gap, while threshold-independent metrics showed broadly similar discrimination.

In summary, MedStyle-3DG addresses the critical lack of systematic training frameworks for robust cross-domain generalization in 3D medical imaging. By effectively mitigating the performance degradation caused by scanner, protocol, and population shifts, the method offers a practical pathway towards developing more robust CAD systems. To foster reproducibility and accelerate the development of domain-robust medical AI, we will make the complete MedStyle-3DG framework and training code publicly available on GitHub upon article acceptance.

## Ethics declarations

### Ethics Approval

This retrospective study used de-identified chest CT examinations from public repositories and private multicenter clinical datasets. Public datasets were accessed and used in accordance with their respective data-use terms. Private clinical data were de-identified before analysis and were processed in compliance with applicable institutional and data protection requirements. Institutional review board or ethics committee approval was obtained where required by the contributing institutions, and the requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective design and use of de-identified data. No prospective recruitment, patient contact, or intervention was performed.

### Consent to Participate

The requirement for informed consent was waived by the Institutional Review Boards of all participating institutions due to the retrospective design of the study and the use of de-identified imaging data.

### Consent for Publication

All patient images included in the manuscript and supplementary material are fully de-identified. The Institutional Review Boards of the participating institutions waived the requirement for additional consent for publication.
