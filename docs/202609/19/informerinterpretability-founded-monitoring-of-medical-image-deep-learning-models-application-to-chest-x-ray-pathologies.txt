Title: INFORMER—Interpretability-Founded Monitoring of Medical Image Deep Learning Models: Application to Chest X-ray Pathologies

Published Time: Tue, 23 Jun 2026 02:06:19 GMT

## Abstract

Deep learning has demonstrated strong performance in medical imaging. However, its limited interpretability remains a major barrier to clinical trust and safe deployment. This limitation is particularly relevant in multi-label classification, where quality control methods are still underdeveloped and commonly rely only on model outputs, without incorporating gradient-level information that may better reflect prediction reliability. In this study, we propose a quality control framework for multi-label medical image classification that improves both reliability and interpretability. The framework includes a graph-based class-distinctiveness method that analyzes saliency-derived information to identify unreliable predictions, as well as a retrieval-based extension that provides case-based explanations for flagged outputs. The proposed methods were evaluated on the CheXpert dataset and compared with established output-based quality control approaches. Robustness was assessed using bootstrapped test sets, and differences in ranking across bootstrap samples were analyzed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. The proposed framework outperformed baseline methods, achieving a higher mean F1 score (0.574 vs. 0.563), while the best-performing variant showed higher sensitivity (0.752 vs. 0.700). In bootstrapped analyses, it achieved better mean ranks than the baseline approaches. Averaged across input-image noise levels of 0.001–0.005, under IxG-based evaluation, the proposed framework showed improvements in bootstrapped F1 over the baseline, with the retrieval-based variant achieving a 21.92% improvement and the corresponding non-retrieval variant achieving a 13.53% improvement. Clinicians further evaluated the retrieved examples to determine their relevance for interpreting flagged predictions. These findings indicate that gradient-level and graph-based analysis can enhance the effectiveness, transparency, and clinical applicability of quality control in multi-label medical image classification.

## Methods

This section introduces our proposed quality control framework for deep learning–based multi-label medical image classification. The central goal of our method is to assess the reliability of model predictions by leveraging interpretability signals beyond standard confidence scores. An overview of the framework is presented in Fig.[1](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10278-026-02257-8#Fig1), while the proposed method is illustrated in Fig.[2](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10278-026-02257-8#Fig2). The individual components are described in the following subsections.

**Fig. 2**

**Illustration of the proposed methods.**INFORMER computes pairwise cosine distances between class-specific saliency maps to construct a vector \(v_i\) for each disease _i_, where \(d_{ij}\) denotes the cosine distance between the saliency maps of diseases _i_ and _j_, for \(i, j \in \mathcal {C}\). The norm of each vector, denoted \(d_i\), serves as a measure of class distinctiveness. These values are aggregated into the final case-level representation vector _D_, which is used for quality control. In RE-INFORMER, \(I_{\text {test}}\) and \(I_{\text {retrieval}}\) denote the test image and its most similar image retrieved from a reference database (e.g., training data), respectively. Saliency maps for each class are generated for both images, and the correlation between their respective cosine distance vectors, \(v^T_i\) and \(v^R_i\), is computed as \(\rho _i\). This correlation score is used to assess the reliability of the prediction for disease _i_

### Interpretability Class-Distinctiveness (INFORMER)

INFORMER introduces the concept of using class-level interpretability patterns to assess the reliability of deep learning predictions in multi-label medical image classification. Specifically, we compute saliency maps (also known as heatmaps) for each patient for all classes \(i \in C\) using a chosen attribution method. These heatmaps capture which image regions contribute most to each class’s model output.

To quantify how separable each class is from each other, we compute the pairwise cosine distance between saliency maps, forming the **_Interpretability Class-Distinctiveness Vector_** (ICDV), denoted as \(v_i\) for class _i_, as illustrated in Fig.[2](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10278-026-02257-8#Fig2). Each element in this vector \(v_i\) is defined as follows:

$$\begin{aligned} d_{ij} = 1 - \text {cos\_sim}(S_i, S_j), \end{aligned}$$

(1)

where \(S_i\) and \(S_j\) are the saliency maps for classes _i_ and _j_, respectively. \(j\in \textit{C}\) and \(i\ne j\). For each disease _i_, we compute the norm of its ICDV, denoted as \(d_i=||v_i||\), to represent the distinctiveness of that class _i_ for patient _k_.

These scalar values \(\{d_i\}_{i=1}^{|C|}\) are concatenated into a final case-level representation vector _D_, which serves as input to a quality control module. This module does quality control of model predictions based on the hypothesis that a confident model should produce saliency maps that are distinct across classes. In contrast, lower class separability, i.e., similar interpretability patterns across different classes, may reflect model uncertainty or ambiguity in the prediction.

### Retrieval-Based Interpretability Class-Distinctiveness (RE-INFORMER)

RE-INFORMER extends the core idea of INFORMER by incorporating a retrieval mechanism to condition interpretability-based uncertainty estimation on semantically similar cases from a chosen database, e.g., training distribution. Rather than evaluating the distinctiveness of saliency maps in isolation, RE-INFORMER compares these interpretability patterns between the test image and a retrieved image from the training distribution. The underlying intuition is that a reliable prediction should exhibit a strong correlation between the test image and the most similar counterpart in a reference database (e.g., the training set), in terms of inter-class separability and class-specific characteristics, as reflected by the similarity of their pairwise interpretability maps.

Given a test image \(I_{\text {test}}\), we first encode it into a latent embedding \(e_{\text {test}}\) using an image encoder _E_ (e.g., RAD-DINO [[24](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10278-026-02257-8#ref-CR24 "Pérez-García F, Sharma H, Bond-Taylor S, Bouzid K, Salvatelli V, Ilse M, Bannur S, Castro DC, Schwaighofer A, Lungren MP, Wetscherek MT, Codella N, Hyland SL, Alvarez-Valle J, Oktay O: Exploring scalable medical image encoders beyond text supervision. Nat Mach Intell 7:119–130, 
                https://doi.org/10.1038/s42256-024-00965-w
                
              , 2025.")]), although any suitable image encoder may be used. The retrieval database of images are all similarly embedded into \(e_{\text {train}} = \{E(I_j)\}_{j=1}^N\). Based on the model’s multi-label prediction \(P = [c_0, c_1, c_2, c_3, c_4]\), where \(c_i \in \{0,1\}\) denotes the predicted presence or absence of disease _i_, we filter the retrieval database of images to include only samples with the same positive labels (\(c_i = 1\)), ensuring consistency with the potential disease complexity. This selection retains only the most relevant samples under the assumption that the prediction of the test image is correct, such that the model is expected to exhibit similar inter-class dissimilarity with samples of comparable conditions. This contrasts with a general image-retrieval setting, where retrieval is performed unconditionally and without constraints on label correspondence, with the primary goal being to return visually similar images rather than to support quality control. We then compute the cosine distance between \(e_{\text {test}}\) and each filtered training embedding to rank the images by similarity and retrieve the most similar image, \(I_{\text {retrieval}}\). The lower the cosine distance score indicates greater similarity to the test image.

Saliency maps are generated for all classes for both the test and retrieved images. Based on these saliency maps, we compute the ICDV for each image as described previously. Then, for each disease _i_, we calculate the Pearson correlation coefficient \(\rho _i\), between the ICDVs of the test images(_T_) and retrieved(_R_) images, where

$$\begin{aligned} \rho _i = \frac{\sum _{k, k\ne i}^{\textit{C}} (d^T_{ik} - \bar{d}_i^T)(d^R_{ik} - \bar{d}_i^R)}{\sqrt{\sum _{k, k\ne i}^{\textit{C}} (d_{ik}^T - \bar{d}_i^T)^2} \sqrt{\sum _{k, k\ne i}^{\textit{C}} (d_{ik}^R - \bar{d}_i^R)^2}}. \end{aligned}$$

(2)

These correlation values are aggregated into the final case-level representation vector _D_, which is used as input to the quality control module.

### Quality Control Mechanism

We compute the final case-level representation vector _D_ for both INFORMER and RE-INFORMER. Subsequently, we determine a class-specific threshold \(\tau _i\) for each class \(i \in \mathcal {C}\) based on the dataset’s performance after it has passed through the quality control system. Predictions for classes whose corresponding elements in the representation vector exceed this threshold are flagged, whereas those below the threshold are considered to have passed quality control. The thresholds are selected using the validation set and applied during inference on the test set. During inference, for INFORMER, low class-distinctive images are flagged, and the average performance is subsequently evaluated. For RE-INFORMER, images with low correlation (\(\rho \)) are flagged.

### Explanation Strategy for RE-INFORMER

The retrieved image \(I_{retrieval}\) associated with the model’s predicted label is displayed together with a retrieved sample from the flipped (inverted) class to provide an explanation to the quality control system. In a multi-label setting, each class is treated as an independent binary decision, which allows the inverted class to be defined simply by flipping the predicted label. In a flagged case, the system can present a side-by-side comparison of the two retrieved images, while suggesting that the inverted label may be more plausible. This comparative visualization provides a human-interpretable rationale for questioning the model’s output and forms the explanation strategy in RE-INFORMER.

### Computational Complexity

The computational cost of INFORMER is dominated by saliency map generation, which requires one forward and one backward pass per class. Its cost scales linearly with the number of classes and also depends on the model size and input resolution. Since the class-wise backward passes are independent, they can be batched on a single GPU or distributed across multiple GPUs. The case-level representation vector _D_ is constructed from the inter-class difference vectors (ICDVs). For each class, the ICDV contains its pairwise differences with the other classes, and its norm forms the corresponding entry of _D_. Excluding self-comparisons and counting each pair only once yields \(\frac{C(C-1)}{2}\) distinct comparisons. Treating each saliency map as a vector of _Z_ elements, each comparison costs \(\mathcal {O}(Z)\), giving an overall complexity of \(\mathcal {O}(C^{2}Z)\). Because _D_ is computed using lightweight vector operations on the CPU, its cost scales linearly with the saliency map size _Z_ and remains inexpensive even for high-resolution maps.

RE-INFORMER adds a CPU-based retrieval step that compares the current image with a set of precomputed reference descriptors and retrieves the top-1 match. Its cost scales with the size of the reference set but consists primarily of efficient vectorized similarity operations. Thus, the dominant GPU cost is governed mainly by the number of classes, whereas the additional CPU cost introduced by RE-INFORMER depends primarily on the number of reference cases.

### Experiments

#### Dataset and Preprocessing

We conducted our experiments using the CheXpert dataset [[20](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10278-026-02257-8#ref-CR20 "Stanford Center for Artificial Intelligence in Medicine & Imaging: CheXpert: Chest X-rays. 
                https://doi.org/10.71718/y7pj-4v93
                
              . Accessed 21 July 2026.")], a large public chest radiograph dataset containing 224,316 images from 65,240 patients. The dataset is divided into 223,414 training images, 234 validation images, and 668 test images. The validation and test sets were labeled by board-certified radiologists, with each image annotated independently by three experts. Following the CheXpert challenge setup, we focus our analysis on five target pathologies selected based on their clinical importance and prevalence: _atelectasis_, _cardiomegaly_, _consolidation_, _edema_, and _pleural effusion_. Each image in the training set is annotated based on radiology reports, with one of four possible labels: present, not present, uncertain, or not mentioned. Our experiments exclude patients with uncertain labels for any of the five target pathologies. Additionally, we group the not present and not mentioned labels as negative samples. To ensure focus on relevant pathology cases and prevalence, we further excluded patients with none of the five target pathologies. This results in a filtered dataset comprising 90,839 training images, 128 validation images, and 284 test images, with per-pathology composition summarized in Table[1](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10278-026-02257-8#Tab1). To evaluate the robustness and variance of our methods, we generated 30 bootstrapped versions of the test set. Each bootstrapped set is constructed by sampling with replacement from the original test set, preserving the original size of 284 images per set.

#### Baselines Uncertainty Estimation Methods

To benchmark our proposed method, we compare it against two widely used uncertainty-based quality control techniques: _predictive entropy_ and _test-time augmentation (TTA)_.

The predictive uncertainty for class _i_ is measured using the binary **predictive entropy**:

$$\begin{aligned} H(\hat{p_i}) = -\hat{p_i} \log _2 \hat{p_i} - (1 - \hat{p_i}) \log _2 (1 - \hat{p_i}), \end{aligned}$$

(3)

where higher entropy values indicate greater uncertainty. As a binary classification task in multi-label classification settings, each class is treated independently. For each class _i_, the model produces a probability prediction \(p_i \in [0, 1]\). \(\hat{p_i}\) is the estimated probability of the disease. Following [[10](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10278-026-02257-8#ref-CR10 "Carneiro G, Pu LZCT, Singh R, Burt A: Deep Learning Uncertainty and Confidence Calibration for the Five-Class Polyp Classification from Colonoscopy. Medical Image Analysis 62:101653, DO
                https://doi.org/10.1016/j.media.2020.101653
                
              , 2020.")], which uses predictive entropy for uncertainty estimation, we selected predictive entropy as one of the baseline approaches.

### Test-Time Augmentation (TTA)

estimates uncertainty by measuring the variability in model outputs across multiple augmented versions of the same input[[25](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10278-026-02257-8#ref-CR25 "Wang G, Li W, Aertsen M, Deprest J, Ourselin S, Vercauteren T: Aleatoric uncertainty estimation with test-time augmentation for medical image segmentation with convolutional neural networks. Neurocomputing 338:34–45, DO
                https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neucom.2019.01.103
                
              , 2019.")]. Specifically, a set of _N_ transformations \(\mathcal {T}\) is applied to the input image _X_, yielding transformed samples \(X' = T(X)\) for each \(T \in \mathcal {T}\). The variance of the output logits from model _M_ across the set of transformed inputs \(\{X'\}^\mathcal {T}\), denoted by \(\textrm{Var}(M(\{X'\}^\mathcal {T} ))\), is then computed. A higher variance indicates greater prediction instability, which is interpreted as increased uncertainty.

These two approaches serve as baselines by using the model’s logit outputs for confidence estimation. All methods can be computed with a single forward pass and require no modifications to the model architecture nor sampling at inference time. This enables a fair comparison with the effectiveness of our interpretability-driven quality control framework.

For INFORMER, the threshold \(\tau _i\), described in Section [Quality Control Mechanism](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10278-026-02257-8#Sec5), was selected via grid search from 0 to the maximum possible score with a step size of 0.001. For RE-INFORMER, TTA, and entropy-based methods, the threshold search, \(\tau _i\) for class _i_, was conducted in the range [0,1] with a step size of 0.001. For TTA and entropy, predictions with logit variance or entropy higher than the threshold \(\tau _i\) for the corresponding method and class _i_ are flagged accordingly.

Multi-label classification is treated as multiple binary classification problems. Flagged predictions are those with a high probability of being misclassified. To evaluate the quality control system in terms of \(F_1\), sensitivity, and specificity, we inverted the flagged predictions and assessed the resulting post-processed model performance using the \(F_1\) score. We also showed the sensitivity and the specificity of the prediction for the test set. This approach quantifies the effectiveness and impact of the uncertainty estimation on improving prediction quality. Since we evaluate model performance after predictions are made using fixed thresholds, multi-threshold metrics are not applicable in this setting. ### Clinical Relevance Application Evaluation

To evaluate the usefulness of the retrieval component of RE-INFORMER in supporting clinicians’ understanding of the flagging system, we conducted a clinical relevance assessment. In this study, clinicians were presented with images flagged by the quality control system, along with retrieval examples generated based on the model’s predictions (positive or negative), as inherently retrieved by RE-INFORMER, and conditionally different retrievals based on the suggested inverted labels corresponding to the flagged cases (retrieval conditions 1 and 2). The model’s predictions themselves were not displayed. Only the retrieval samples and the target class to be evaluated were shown.

The clinician was asked to compare the test image under the two retrieval conditions and determine which condition, anonymously representing either the predicted or the suggested label, appeared more consistent with the flagged case. The true labels of the images were withheld to prevent bias from classification performance or other factors irrelevant to the evaluation of the retrieval conditions. A total of seven sets of samples were evaluated. Among these, the clinician agreed with the flagging system in six cases, indicating that the pathology associated with the suggested class was more consistent with the condition implied by the predicted class. The examples shown to clinicians are provided in Appendix [B](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10278-026-02257-8#Sec22).

Furthermore, the clinician was asked to comment on how the retrieval examples contributed to their trust and understanding of the quality control system. The clinicians agreed that the retrieval-based explanations enhanced their trust in the system’s quality control mechanism and provided greater transparency regarding the rationale behind the flagging decisions.

### Sensitivity Analysis

We determine high-uncertainty predictions using a class-specific threshold \(\tau \), as mentioned in Section [Implementation](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10278-026-02257-8#Sec11), selected via grid search on the validation set. To assess the sensitivity of each method to threshold variation, we perform a sensitivity analysis by perturbing the original threshold \(\tau \) by ±5%. The impact of these changes on model performance is shown in Fig.[4](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10278-026-02257-8#Fig4).

The results indicate that the performance of the entropy-based quality control method is more sensitive to threshold variation, as shown in Fig.[4](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10278-026-02257-8#Fig4), particularly when the threshold is decreased by 5%. In contrast, both INFORMER and RE-INFORMER exhibit greater robustness to threshold shifts, maintaining stable performance under minor changes.

### Test-Time Noisy Image

To evaluate the robustness of the proposed quality control system, we conducted experiments using noisy input images that simulate test-time corruptions. Gaussian noise with zero mean and unit standard deviation was added to the test images, scaled across varying proportions of the image intensity range to generate different noise levels. The performance of the system was then assessed after applying the quality control procedures, allowing us to quantify its ability to maintain accuracy in the presence of input degradation. As shown in Fig.[5](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10278-026-02257-8#Fig5), INFORMER consistently outperforms baseline methods across all noise levels and with both interpretability techniques, maintaining higher overall F1 scores. RE-INFORMER demonstrates a more pronounced advantage over entropy-based quality control under noise test image conditions. Beyond strong quantitative performance, both INFORMER and RE-INFORMER provide interpretable explanations for why specific predictions are flagged, offering valuable insights to support clinical decision-making.

## Discussion

In this section, we discuss the clinical significance of the findings, as well as provide a general discussion of the study and its limitations.

### Clinical Evaluation

In this section, we translate the impact of the quality control system’s improvement in model predictions into a clinical context and assess its potential value. We observe that \(\text {INFORMER}_{\text {ixg}}\) and \(\text {RE-INFORMER}_{\text {lrp}}\) demonstrate improved sensitivity compared to the baseline models. For example, in the case of atelectasis, both methods achieve higher sensitivity (SENS), although this comes with a slight decrease in specificity (SPEC). This trend is similarly reflected in cardiomegaly and consolidation. Clinically, this trade-off is acceptable and even desirable. Atelectasis can stem from various reversible causes and is often managed with non-invasive interventions such as breathing exercises, repositioning, or mobilization. These treatments are generally safe and beneficial for most hospitalized patients, regardless of whether true atelectasis is present. As a result, prioritizing higher sensitivity in atelectasis detection, even at the expense of specificity, aligns with clinical best practices.

For consolidation detection, RE-INFORMER with both interpretability methods achieves the higher SPEC at 0.518 and 0.522 without a reduction in sensitivity. The INFORMER\(_\text {lrp}\) method outperforms baseline approaches in sensitivity (SENS: baselines 0.690, \(\text {INFORMER}_\text {lrp}\) 0.724), with a comparable specificity (SPEC: predictive entropy 0.514, \(\text {INFORMER}_\text {lrp}\) 0.510). Community-acquired pneumonia (CAP), commonly presenting with radiographic consolidation, is a leading cause of hospitalization, with an estimated 1.5 million unique adult admissions annually in the USA alone [[31](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10278-026-02257-8#ref-CR31 "Ramirez JA, Wiemken TL, Peyrani P, Arnold FW, Kelley R, Mattingly WA, Nakamatsu R, Pena S, Guinn BE, Furmanek SP, Persaud AK, Raghuram A, Fernandez F, Beavin L, Bosson R, Fernandez-Botran R, Cavallazzi R, Bordon J, Valdivieso C, Schulte J, Carrico RM, University of Louisville Pneumonia Study Group: Adults Hospitalized With Pneumonia in the United States: Incidence, Epidemiology, and Mortality. Clin Infect Dis 65:1806–1812, 
                https://doi.org/10.1093/cid/cix647
                
              , 2017.")]. To put these results in perspective, assuming all 1.5 million patients undergo chest X-ray (CXR) evaluation, a method with 0.690 sensitivity (baseline) would correctly identify approximately 1,035,000 cases. In comparison, \(\text {INFORMER}_\text {lrp}\) (SENS: 0.724) would detect around 1,086,000 cases, capturing an additional 51,000 patients. Early detection of these cases could be critical in preventing the progression of CAP to severe outcomes such as respiratory failure or sepsis. All models’ sensitivity and specificity values are provided in Supplementary Tables[5](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10278-026-02257-8#Tab5) and [7](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10278-026-02257-8#Tab7).

### General Discussion

We propose a novel quality-control approach that leverages model gradients to assess prediction uncertainty in multi-label classification. Unlike existing approaches that rely solely on output probabilities, our method, INFORMER, achieves a superior \(F_1\) score on the CheXpert dataset. Beyond performance, it provides gradient-based explanations for why specific predictions are flagged, while RE-INFORMER adds case-based interpretability by retrieving reference examples for each flagged instance. Importantly, the proposed framework is not disease-specific, as it relies on saliency- and embedding-derived information rather than pathology-specific features, which supports its potential applicability across different target pathologies. Although RE-INFORMER does not surpass INFORMER in terms of quality-control performance alone, it remains above the predictive-entropy baseline and, in the I\(\times \)G setting, is more robust to input perturbations than INFORMER and the baseline. INFORMER’s stronger performance on Cardiomegaly may reflect its relatively consistent anatomical location and geometric characteristics, which may be better captured through class-distinctive aggregation than through correlations with retrieved cases. In the clinical evaluation, the retrieved examples were judged to enhance transparency and foster trust in the flagging process. This is particularly relevant when quality control operates on top of a black-box model, where transparency and interpretability are important for clinical trust and adoption [[32](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10278-026-02257-8#ref-CR32 "Kim C, Gadgil SU, Lee SI: Transparency of Medical Artificial Intelligence Systems. Nat Rev Bioeng 4:11–29, 
                https://doi.org/10.1038/s44222-025-00363-w
                
              , 2026."), [33](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10278-026-02257-8#ref-CR33 "Amann J, Blasimme A, Vayena E, Frey D, Madai VI, the Precise4Q consortium: Explainability for Artificial Intelligence in Healthcare: A Multidisciplinary Perspective. BMC Med Inform Decis Mak 20:310, 
                https://doi.org/10.1186/s12911-020-01332-6
                
              , 2020.")]. Both methods further maintain stable performance across varying decision thresholds.

Most existing studies on uncertainty-based quality control focus on analyzing model outputs, such as predictive entropy or variations in class probabilities. However, valuable information embedded within the model, particularly in its gradients, remains underutilized and poorly understood. The proposed methods demonstrate the potential of leveraging model gradients to achieve competitive uncertainty estimation performance while also providing inherent interpretability of the model’s behavior. We also emphasize the clinical importance of sensitivity in pathology detection. Even a small reduction in false negatives can yield substantial clinical benefits by preventing missed diagnoses and improving patient safety. Our approach improves the sensitivity of model predictions, which in our hypothetical scenario could enable the detection of an additional 51,000 patients with consolidation.

In our retrieval implementation, images are retrieved conditioned on belonging to the same class as the predicted label. This is intended to mimic the similarity between the test image and class-specific examples previously seen by the model, enabling class-distinctive evaluation. However, this constraint can be relaxed and adapted for different retrieval strategies, such as alternative image comparisons or counterfactual illustrations.

The underlying intuition of RE-INFORMER is that a reliable prediction should maintain interpretability consistency with similar cases previously encountered by the model. Discrepancies in the interpretability structure between the test and retrieved images may indicate unusual patterns or uncertainty, thereby justifying the need for human review. The performance of RE-INFORMER is inherently influenced by the quality of the retrieval database. In particular, the more semantically similar the retrieved image is to the test image, the more reliable the resulting quality control signal. This is closely tied to out-of-domain detection and distributional shifts between the test image and the training data. Therefore, the retrieval database should contain semantically relevant images with accurate and trustworthy labels.

Currently, most studies on multi-label medical classification report only the area under the curve (AUC), which reflects model performance across multiple decision thresholds. However, in real-world clinical settings, decisions are typically made using fixed thresholds. Therefore, we encourage future work to report additional metrics, such as the \(F_1\) score and sensitivity, to better capture model behavior at deployment. In multi-label classification, each class is treated as an independent binary classification task, for which the use of \(F_1\) score and sensitivity is recommended, as outlined in[[30](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10278-026-02257-8#ref-CR30 "Maier-Hein L, Reinke A, Godau P, Tizabi MD, Buettner F, Christodoulou E, Glocker B, Isensee F, Kleesiek J, Kozubek M, Reyes M, Riegler MA, Wiesenfarth M, Kavur AE, Sudre CH, Baumgartner M, Eisenmann M, Heckmann-Nötzel D, Rädsch T, Acion L, Antonelli M, Arbel T, Bakas S, Benis A, Blaschko MB, Cardoso MJ, Cheplygina V, Cimini BA, Collins GS, Farahani K, Ferrer L, Galdran A, van Ginneken B, Haase R, Hashimoto DA, Hoffman MM, Huisman M, Jannin P, Kahn CE, Kainmueller D, Kainz B, Karargyris A, Karthikesalingam A, Kofler F, Kopp-Schneider A, Kreshuk A, Kurc T, Landman BA, Litjens G, Madani A, Maier-Hein K, Martel AL, Mattson P, Meijering E, Menze B, Moons KGM, Müller H, Nichyporuk B, Nickel F, Petersen J, Rajpoot N, Rieke N, Saez-Rodriguez J, Sánchez CI, Shetty S, van Smeden M, Summers RM, Taha AA, Tiulpin A, Tsaftaris SA, Van Calster B, Varoquaux G, Jäger PF: Metrics reloaded: recommendations for image analysis validation. ### Limitation and Further Work

There are limitations to these quality control methods. The proposed methods may be less effective when co-occurring findings occupy substantially overlapping regions of the image, making their class-specific saliency patterns less distinguishable. A separate class-dependent behavior was observed for edema and pleural effusion, where the underlying classifier already achieved high sensitivity for these findings, and the evaluated quality-control configurations generated no additional alerts. The retrieval database uses the CheXpert training set, whose labels were automatically extracted from radiology reports and not verified through manual image-level review. Despite excluding uncertain labels, possible label inconsistencies may affect the quality-control signal and clinicians’ interpretation of the retrieved examples. A missing alert does not confirm a prediction, and an incorrect alert may lead to unnecessary review. All alerts should require expert interpretation.

We also experimented with Grad-CAM as the underlying saliency method for computing class-distinctiveness. However, the performance was suboptimal compared to LRP and input-times-gradient methods. We attribute this to the coarseness of Grad-CAM, which is also observed in [[34](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10278-026-02257-8#ref-CR34 "Chattopadhay A, Sarkar A, Howlader P, Balasubramanian VN: Grad-cam++: Generalized gradient-based visual explanations for deep convolutional networks. In: 2018 IEEE winter conference on applications of computer vision (WACV), IEEE, pp. 839–847, DO
                https://doi.org/10.1109/WACV.2018.00097
                
              , 2018."), [35](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10278-026-02257-8#ref-CR35 "Selvaraju RR, Cogswell M, Das A, Vedantam R, Parikh D, Batra D: Grad-CAM: Visual Explanations From Deep Networks via Gradient-Based Localization. In: Proceedings of the IEEE International Conference on Computer Vision (ICCV), pp. 618–626, 2017.")], potentially obscuring subtle class distinctions crucial for our framework. We employ a post hoc interpretability method for our experiments, which differs from approaches that analyze models, such as transformer architectures, based on their inherent attention mechanisms. Adapting the system to transformer-based models is a promising direction for future work. Incorporating GRAM-style [[36](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10278-026-02257-8#ref-CR36 "Yang Y, Wang P, He X, Zou D: GRAM: An Interpretable Approach for Graph Anomaly Detection Using Gradient Attention Maps. Neural Netw 178:106463, 
                https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neunet.2024.106463
                
              , 2024.")] methods to capture correlations between classes may further enhance the construction of the underlying graph structure. Additionally, while our current focus is on X-ray imaging, extending the approach to other modalities is also worth investigating.

Advanced uncertainty estimation methods, such as deep ensembles and Monte Carlo (MC) dropout, while widely used in the uncertainty qualification literature, are not directly aligned with the objectives of this work, which focuses on a lightweight flagging mechanism for detecting potentially unreliable predictions rather than producing full Bayesian uncertainty estimates. Deep ensembles [[37](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10278-026-02257-8#ref-CR37 "Lakshminarayanan B, Pritzel A, Blundell C: Simple and Scalable Predictive Uncertainty Estimation Using Deep Ensembles. In: Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems, Vol 30, pp. 6402–6413, 2017.")] require training multiple independently initialized models and often rely on diversity-enhancing strategies [[38](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10278-026-02257-8#ref-CR38 "Kofler F, Ezhov I, Fidon L, Pirkl CM, Paetzold JC, Burian E, Pati S, El Husseini M, Navarro F, Shit S, Kirschke J, Bakas S, Zimmer C, Wiestler B, Menze BH: Robust, Primitive, and Unsupervised Quality Estimation for Segmentation Ensembles. Front Neurosci 15:752780, 
                https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2021.752780
                
              , 2021."), [39](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10278-026-02257-8#ref-CR39 "Wen Y, Tran D, Ba J: BatchEnsemble: an Alternative Approach to Efficient Ensemble and Lifelong Learning. In: International Conference on Learning Representations, 2020.")], resulting in substantial computational cost and a large hyperparameter space. Likewise, MC dropout [[40](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10278-026-02257-8#ref-CR40 "Gal Y, Ghahramani Z: Dropout as a bayesian approximation: Representing model uncertainty in deep learning. In: international conference on machine learning, PMLR, pp. 1050–1059, 2016.")] involves architectural modification and many stochastic forward passes, with performance highly sensitive to dropout configuration [[41](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10278-026-02257-8#ref-CR41 "Verdoja F, Kyrki V: Notes on the Behavior of MC Dropout. In: ICML Workshop on Uncertainty & Robustness in Deep Learning, 2021.")]. These properties impose substantial training, storage, and configuration overhead, making both methods heavier to deploy than a post hoc flagging mechanism that operates on a single, trained model. For these reasons, we employ simple, architecture-agnostic baselines, predictive entropy and test-time augmentation, that match the minimal-overhead design constraints of our approach, aiming not to compete with full quality control methods but to evaluate an efficient, deployable strategy for identifying potentially erroneous outputs in real clinical settings.

In Appendix Fig.[6](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10278-026-02257-8#Fig6), we present examples of the retrieval samples shown to the clinician. We presented this work to an additional senior radiologist. They agreed that such retrieval examples can help establish trust in the quality control system, although such examples do not yet fully explain how the system operates. The clinicians also suggested that these retrievals could support clinical decision-making, particularly for junior residents, if the images are accompanied by localized pathology annotations, such as bounding boxes. Further refinement and expansion of the clinical evaluation will be addressed as future work.

In future work, we plan to extend our quality-control framework to additional pathologies and multimodal settings, such as those combining medical images and text reports, to further assess its generalizability and enhance model trustworthiness in clinical applications. We also plan to explore the graph structure in greater depth to better capture inter-class relationships, which may lead to further methodological improvements. In addition, we aim to enhance transparency while closing the small gap in quality-control performance relative to INFORMER.

## Conclusion

This study shows that gradient-based quality control can improve both the reliability and interpretability of multi-label medical image classification. By incorporating gradient-level information rather than relying solely on output probabilities, INFORMER and RE-INFORMER achieved improved performance, robust behavior across perturbations and thresholds, and higher sensitivity for clinically important findings. The retrieval-based extension further strengthened interpretability by providing case-based explanations for flagged predictions, thereby supporting transparency and human oversight. Taken together, these results highlight the potential of gradient-informed quality control frameworks to enhance trust, safety, and practical deployment of artificial intelligence systems in clinical imaging.
