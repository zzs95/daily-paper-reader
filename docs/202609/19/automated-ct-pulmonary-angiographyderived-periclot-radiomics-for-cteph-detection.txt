Title: Automated CT Pulmonary Angiography‐Derived Periclot Radiomics for CTEPH Detection

URL Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13551595/

Markdown Content:
## [](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13551595/)Abstract

CTEPH remains underdiagnosed and can be difficult to differentiate on routine CTPA from acute PE and from patients without pulmonary thromboembolism. We developed and evaluated a fully automated CTPA‐based machine learning algorithm combining vascular blood volume metrics with clot and periclot imaging features. In this retrospective multicenter study, intraparenchymal pulmonary vessels were segmented using a scale‐space particle approach, deep learning models estimated vessel radius, clot probability, and clot area from cross‐sectional patches along the vessel axis, and subject‐level radiomic and blood volume features were used to train an XGBoost classifier on a Northwestern University (NU) cohort (89 CTEPH; 176 controls). The trained model was then applied (without retraining) to held‐out NU data, an independent acute PE cohort (152 patients) as an exploratory generalization test, and an external cohort from Brigham and Women's Hospital (23 CTEPH; 34 controls). Among 474 participants (mean age, 51 years ± 16; 299 men), the model achieved an AUC of 0.87 (95% CI: 0.79, 0.94) for CTEPH versus controls in internal testing, 0.74 (95% CI: 0.61–0.86) in external testing, and 0.89 (95% CI 0.83–0.93) for CTEPH versus acute PE. Excluding periclot features reduced discrimination, particularly for CTEPH versus acute PE (AUC 0.71; 95% CI: 0.63, 0.79). These findings support the feasibility of automated CTPA‐derived vascular, clot, and periclot features for differentiating CTEPH from acute PE and controls without evidence of pulmonary thromboembolism, with further validation needed to assess generalizability across institutions.

**Keywords:**automated clot detection, blood volume metrics, Pulmonary vascular pruning, SHAP interpretability, small vessel remodeling

1
Chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH) is a rare but life‐threatening form of pulmonary hypertension (PH) and the most severe chronic manifestation of pulmonary thromboembolic disease. It is characterized by persistent obstruction of the pulmonary arteries due to unresolved thromboemboli and progressive fibrotic vascular remodeling. Unlike idiopathic pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH), CTEPH is potentially curable with pulmonary endarterectomy, which targets proximal and segmental vessels [[1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13551595/#pul270406-bib-0001)]. When surgery is not feasible, balloon pulmonary angioplasty, directed at more distal, subsegmental branches, offers an alternative treatment option [[2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13551595/#pul270406-bib-0002)]. Therefore, its treatment differs substantially from that of other forms of PH and acute pulmonary embolism (PE). Early diagnosis is critical, yet CTEPH remains underrecognized because early symptoms are nonspecific and overlap considerably with those of both other PH subtypes and acute PE, while characteristic clinical signs often emerge late [[3](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13551595/#pul270406-bib-0003), [4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13551595/#pul270406-bib-0004)]. In addition, limited familiarity with CTEPH‐specific imaging features outside expert centers frequently leads to misdiagnosis as acute PE or alternative PH subtypes, which drives delayed treatment, increased mortality, and higher health care costs [[5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13551595/#pul270406-bib-0005), [6](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13551595/#pul270406-bib-0006), [7](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13551595/#pul270406-bib-0007)].

Both imaging suggestive of chronic clot and elevated mean pulmonary pressures (mPAP > 20 mmHg) and pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR ≥ 2 WU) are needed for the diagnosis of CTEPH. However, even without meeting the criteria for PH, the presence of chronic clot. termed chronic thromboembolic disease (CTED), can lead to symptoms and is often treated similarly as CTEPH [[8](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13551595/#pul270406-bib-0008), [9](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13551595/#pul270406-bib-0009), [10](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13551595/#pul270406-bib-0010)]. Thus, the presence of chronic clot is one key pathophysiologic differentiator between CTEPH/CTED and acute PE, as well as other forms of PH. While current guidelines recommend a multimodal diagnostic approach [[11](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13551595/#pul270406-bib-0011), [12](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13551595/#pul270406-bib-0012), [13](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13551595/#pul270406-bib-0013), [14](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13551595/#pul270406-bib-0014), [15](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13551595/#pul270406-bib-0015)], computed tomography pulmonary angiography (CTPA) is typically performed first to evaluate suspected acute PE, presenting a key opportunity to detect the presence of chronic thromboembolism.

While CTEPH is associated with distal vascular abnormalities and perfusion defects on CT imaging [[5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13551595/#pul270406-bib-0005), [16](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13551595/#pul270406-bib-0016)], many of these microvascular changes – particularly in vessels below 10mm 2 cross‐sectional area (CSA) and in the sub‐millimeter microvasculature – are subtle and difficult to quantify reliably in clinical practice and overlap with those found in pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) and acute PE [[17](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13551595/#pul270406-bib-0017)].

In the progression from acute thromboembolism to chronic thromboembolism, the clot undergoes substantial remodeling, evolving from an acute thrombus composed of platelets, erythrocytes, and inflammatory cells into an organized, fibrin‐rich fibrotic lesion that becomes incorporated into the vessel wall [[18](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13551595/#pul270406-bib-0018), [19](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13551595/#pul270406-bib-0019)]. The increasing availability of chest CTPA, together with advances in computational imaging, presents an opportunity to develop reproducible CT‐derived biomarkers that capture the differences between acute and chronic thromboembolism in ways not readily appreciated by visual assessment.

Radiomics enables high‐throughput extraction of quantitative image features and when combined with machine learning, has supported disease classification and risk prediction across cardiothoracic and oncologic imaging applications [[20](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13551595/#pul270406-bib-0020), [21](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13551595/#pul270406-bib-0021), [22](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13551595/#pul270406-bib-0022), [23](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13551595/#pul270406-bib-0023), [24](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13551595/#pul270406-bib-0024)]. In CTEPH, chronic thrombi undergo substantial organization, fibrosis, and incorporation into the vessel wall, inducing local microstructural changes that extend beyond the immediate lumen that are not present in acute thromboembolism [[18](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13551595/#pul270406-bib-0018), [19](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13551595/#pul270406-bib-0019)]. We therefore hypothesized that tissue immediately surrounding clot‐positive vessels (the periclot region) would carry distinguishing information about thromboembolic chronicity, detectable as quantifiable attenuation heterogeneity (e.g., variance, skewness, kurtosis) rather than morphologic change alone. Such radiomic approaches may help quantify not only small vessel thrombotic burden but also periclot tissue signatures associated with chronic vascular remodeling. In this study, we developed and evaluated an automated CT‐based algorithm that integrates deep learning, radiomics, and machine learning to differentiate patients with CTEPH from patients without evidence of pulmonary thromboembolism and from patients with acute PE using standard CTPA. The workflow (presented in Figure[1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13551595/#pul270406-fig-0001)) detects intraparenchymal vascular clot candidates, characterizes and quantifies features reflecting clot burden, vascular characteristics, and periclot morphology (with associated blood volume metrics), and trains a gradient‐boosted decision tree classifier (XGBoost [[25](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13551595/#pul270406-bib-0025)]). Performance was assessed on internal test data, an independent acute PE cohort, and an external validation cohort.

Schematic illustration of the automated pipeline integrating deep learning, radiomics, and machine learning to differentiate chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH) from controls without evidence of pulmonary thromboembolism and acute pulmonary embolism (PE). The workflow consists of seven sequential stages applied to contrast‐enhanced chest CT scans: (1) automated vascular segmentation using scale‐space particle detection; (2) extraction of standardized 32 × 32 pixel vessel patches (3–50 mm 2 cross‐sectional area, CSA); (3) three convolutional neural networks for clot detection, clot area estimation, and vessel radius prediction (all trained on 7 million synthetic patches); (4) radiomic feature extraction from vessel lumen, clot, and periclot regions (10 × 10 × 10 voxels); (5) quantification of blood volume (BV) metrics normalized by total lung volume (BV1, BV5, BV5‐20, TBV); (6) compilation of comprehensive feature vectors; and (7) XGBoost classification with SHAP‐based feature importance analysis. This pipeline is fully automated and requires no manual annotation. An important novel aspect includes the integration of periclot radiomic features (stage 4), which capture tissue heterogeneity in the immediate vicinity of thrombi. ## [](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13551595/)Methods

2
### Study Design, Setting, and Participants

2.1
This retrospective, multicenter, HIPAA‐compliant study was approved by the institutional review boards at Northwestern University Feinberg School of Medicine (Chicago, IL) (NU) and Brigham and Women's Hospital (Boston, MA) (BWH); informed consent for this specific analysis was waived. After initial cohort assembly, all CTPA images and clinical variables were de‐identified prior to analysis.

At NU, the CTEPH cohort represents consecutive patients with a diagnosis of CTEPH who underwent pulmonary thromboendarterectomy between 2016 and 2022. PE and control participants were selected from the pool of all patients who underwent CTPA between 2020 and 2023, with subsets chosen to match the CTEPH cohort on age, sex and body mass index (BMI). The acute PE cohort included patients with confirmed acute PE. The control cohort comprised patients who underwent CTPA for suspected PE but were definitively negative, presenting with normal pulmonary vascular anatomy and no evidence of pulmonary thromboembolism. Our model development strategy trained the classifier to distinguish CTEPH from these controls to isolate baseline signatures of chronic vascular remodeling. Distinguishing CTEPH from acute PE was then designed as an exploratory generalization experiment to assess the clinical utility of the learned chronic signature.

At BWH, participants were selected from patients receiving invasive hemodynamic assessment and CTPA within 1 year of each other between 2011 and 2018 [[26](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13551595/#pul270406-bib-0026)]. Within this cohort, patients with a diagnosis of CTEPH and adequate imaging were included as cases. Control participants were selected from individuals who underwent invasive cardiopulmonary exercise testing and had normal pulmonary function testing and normal imaging findings, provided CTPA quality was adequate. Participants younger than 18 years were excluded.

For both institutions, CTPA examinations were excluded for inadequate image quality, defined as absence of contiguous slices covering the entirety of both lungs. CTPA examinations were retrospectively identified from routine clinical care at each institution rather than acquired using a standardized research imaging protocol. Studies were performed on multiple multidetector CT scanner platforms over the study period, including both conventional and dual‐energy CT systems, using institution‐specific acquisition protocols. All examinations were intravenous contrast‐enhanced CTPA studies reconstructed with standard clinical algorithms. Images were reconstructed at thin‐section thicknesses ranging from approximately 0.5 to 1.5 mm, consistent with routine clinical practice for pulmonary embolism evaluation.

### Image Processing and Vessel Sampling

2.2
The automated analysis algorithm consisted of vascular tree segmentation, extraction of standardized vessel cross‐sectional patches orthogonal to the vascular axes, deep learning‐based estimation of vessel radius and clot‐related measures, subject‐level feature aggregation (radiomic and BV metrics), and supervised classification (Figure[1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13551595/#pul270406-fig-0001)).

Intraparenchymal pulmonary vessels were segmented using a scale‐space particle detection algorithm that identifies tubular structures across spatial scales using Hessian‐based image features [[27](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13551595/#pul270406-bib-0027)]. For each detected vessel cross‐section, a 32 × 32‐pixel axial patch centered on the estimated cross‐sectional plane was extracted and resampled to a uniform in‐plane resolution. Only intraparenchymal vessels with estimated CSA 3–50 mm 2 were retained for clot detection, radiomic feature extraction, and subject‐level feature aggregation. This range was chosen because vessels below 3 mm 2 approach the spatial resolution limit of routine CTPA and 32 × 32 patch sampling, precluding reliable clot delineation due to severe partial volume artifacts in smaller microvasculature, while vessels above 50 mm 2 correspond to proximal/lobar arteries where thromboembolic material is more reliably detected by visual assessment and where our synthetic training data (matched to segmental/subsegmental caliber) may not generalize well. This window targets the segmental‐to‐subsegmental vessels most relevant to CTEPH‐specific remodeling and where automated detection of early remodeling and microvascular thrombotic burden is most clinically needed. Blood volume metrics, by contrast, were derived from the complete segmented intraparenchymal vascular tree and were computed independently of the clot‐detection pipeline.

### Deep Learning‐Based Clot Measures

2.3
Two convolutional neural networks (CNNs) were used for (1) clot presence classification and (2) clot area percentage regression. Both CNNs were trained on synthetically generated 32 × 32 grayscale vessel patches with known ground‐truth parameters [[18](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13551595/#pul270406-bib-0018)] (Figure[2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13551595/#pul270406-fig-0002)). Synthetic patch generation varied lumen geometry (circular or elliptical), vessel size, adjacent anatomic structures, and CT‐like blur and noise [[28](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13551595/#pul270406-bib-0028)]. Approximately 80% of patches contained a simulated clot with variable eccentricity and clot burden, defined as percentage of lumen area occlusion (30%–80%). More than 7 million patches were generated for training. Quality control of the synthetic patches was maintained by enforcing strict geometric constraints during generation, while the ground‐truth parameters inherently mitigated bias by ensuring a uniformly distributed prevalence of clot morphologies and occlusion severities.

Synthetic clot‐occluded vessel patch generation pipeline. Synthetically generated vessel patches were created by first generating a high‐resolution geometric vessel model with an embedded simulated clot (step 1; 50 µm), where clot type and severity were randomly sampled from a predefined morphology library (examples shown below, including wall‐adherent and central clots). The geometric model was then resampled to the target CT‐like resolution (step 2), blurred by convolving with a point‐spread‐function (PSF) model to emulate system/reconstruction blur (step 3), and corrupted with additive noise to emulate CT image noise (step 4), producing the final synthetic vessel patch used for CNN training (step 5; 0.50 mm). An example of an in‐vivo vessel patch is shown for qualitative comparison with the final synthetic appearance (right). Synthetic patch generation was designed to span variability in lumen geometry (circular/elliptical), vessel size, adjacent anatomic structures (such as connected arteries), and CT‐like blur/noise, and to provide known ground‐truth clot parameters (e.g., clot presence and clot area percentage) for supervised training.

For clot detection and clot area regression, we used a shared dual‐path CNN architecture [[29](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13551595/#pul270406-bib-0029)] (Figure[3](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13551595/#pul270406-fig-0003)) trained with binary cross‐entropy loss (classification) or mean squared error loss (regression), using Adam optimization, dropout, and early stopping.

Architecture of the convolutional neural networks used for clot detection (binary classification) and clot area estimation (regression) from 32×32‐pixel cross‐sectional axial vessel patches. Each network processes input patches through two parallel convolutional paths (six layers each) with distinct kernel sizes, enabling capture of both fine local textures and broader contextual features. Outputs from both paths are flattened, passed through fully connected layers, concatenated, and processed in a shared dense layer before the task‐specific output head: sigmoid activation for clot presence or linear activation for clot area percentage. Networks were trained on synthetic vessel patches (~ 80% with simulated clots) using Adam optimization (initial learning rate, 1×10‐4) with early stopping and dropout regularization based on validation loss. This architecture has been adapted from a previously proposed neural network originally designed for subvoxel vessel wall thickness estimation in intracranial arteries19. Conv2D: 2D convolutional layer; PReLu: parametric rectified linear unit; Batch Norm: batch normalization.

At inference, patches with predicted clot probability ≥ 0.80 (selected using training/validation data) were retained. Predicted clot area percentage was stored for downstream feature computation.

### CNN Performance and Robustness Evaluation

2.4
Performance of both CNNs was evaluated on a synthetic test set (_n_ = 200,000 patches) not used for training nor hyperparameter tuning. Because expert‐annotated real CTPA vessel cross‐sections with known clot ground truth were not available, we additionally assessed robustness through stratified and perturbation‐based analyses on synthetic data. Performance was stratified by vessel CSA (3–10, 10–25, and 25–50 mm 2) and clot occlusion burden (low, 30%–45%; moderate, 45%–65%; high, 655–80%). To approximate image‐quality variability encountered in clinical CTPA, we additionally evaluated performance under three levels of simulated Gaussian noise (σ n = 10, 25, and 40 HU, added to patch intensities) and three levels of simulated Gaussian blur (σ s = 0.4, 0.7, and 0.9 mm, corresponding to approximately 0.6–1.4 voxel widths at the isotropic 0.625 mm voxel spacing used for patch resampling), generating 100 synthetic patches per condition per vessel CSA group, with the non‐varied parameter held at its medium level (σ n = 25 HU; σ s = 0.7 mm). Because CTPA reconstruction blur in clinical practice arises from a combination of reconstruction kernel and slice thickness rather than a single reported Gaussian parameter, the simulated blur levels were used as a controlled stress test of network robustness relative to patch resolution, rather than a direct mapping to specific clinical acquisition protocols.

### Feature Extraction

2.5
For each retained clot candidate, radiomic features were computed from three regions: vessel lumen, detected clot, and a periclot neighborhood defined as a 10 × 10 × 10‐voxel cubic region centered on the clot‐positive vessel cross‐section (Figure[4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13551595/#pul270406-fig-0004)), after resampling to an isotropic voxel spacing of 0.625 mm using cubic interpolation. Primary per‐vessel measures included vessel lumen attenuation, clot area percentage, and periclot attenuation. Subject‐level descriptors were computed by aggregating each per‐vessel measure across selected vessels using first‐order statistics (mean, standard deviation, skewness, kurtosis). To reduce inter‐scan variability, vessel lumen density was normalized by the mean attenuation in the main pulmonary artery, automatically detected using TotalSegmentator‐v1.5 [[30](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13551595/#pul270406-bib-0030)].

### Blood Volume Metrics

2.6
Pulmonary blood volume (BV) metrics were computed from the full segmented intraparenchymal vascular tree [[31](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13551595/#pul270406-bib-0031)]. For each vessel element, CSA and segment length were estimated using previously described methods [[27](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13551595/#pul270406-bib-0027), [28](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13551595/#pul270406-bib-0028)] and integrated across vessel sizes to obtain BV1 (< 1 mm 2), BV5 (< 5 mm 2), BV5‐20 (5–20 mm 2), and total blood volume (TBV) [[27](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13551595/#pul270406-bib-0027), [28](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13551595/#pul270406-bib-0028), [31](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13551595/#pul270406-bib-0031)]. BV metrics were normalized by total lung volume derived from CT‐based lung segmentation.

### Model Development, Evaluation, and Interpretability

2.7
An XGBoost [[25](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13551595/#pul270406-bib-0025)] model was trained to classify CTEPH using the combined radiomic and BV feature vector. Model development used only NU data (89 CTEPH; 176 controls) with a stratified 70%/30% train‐test split preserving class distribution. Hyperparameters were optimized within the 70% training set using five‐fold cross‐validation. The operating threshold was selected using the Youden index computed on training‐only outputs and was held fixed across all three subsequent evaluation scenarios (internal test set, CTEPH vs acute PE, and external replication).

Without further re‐training, the fixed model was evaluated in three prespecified scenarios: (1) Internal test set: NU CTEPH and control patients held out from the internal train/test split (27 CTEPH, 53 controls), used to evaluate the model on the task for which it was trained and calibrated. (2) CTEPH vs acute PE: the 27 NU held‐out CTEPH patients combined with 23 BWH CTEPH patients (50 total), compared against 152 NU acute PE patients not used in training, used to apply the fixed model on an exploratory generalization task. (3) External replication: the full BWH cohort (23 CTEPH; 34 controls), entirely independent of model training, used to evaluate the CTEPH‐vs‐control task at a new site. Scenarios 2 and 3 share the same 23 BWH CTEPH patients, and Scenario 2 shares the same 27 NU CTEPH patients as Scenario 1. These overlaps preclude treating the three scenarios as fully independent comparisons. Performance metrics included area under the ROC curve (AUC), and sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy at the fixed threshold; 95% CI were computed using 1,000 bootstrap resamples per test set. A prespecified ablation analysis repeated all evaluations after excluding periclot‐derived features, to test our hypothesis that periclot tissue heterogeneity contributes incremental discriminative information beyond luminal and clot‐based measures. Feature importance was assessed using Shapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) [[32](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13551595/#pul270406-bib-0032)]. Stability was evaluated by repeating stratified 70%/30% splits 5000 times within NU and recording the top five SHAP‐ranked features per iteration.

All analyses were performed on a single NVIDIA RTX 5000 Ada GPU workstation without transfer to external computing facilities. Mean per‐subject processing time, from CTPA input to classifier output, was approximately 10 min per subject.

### Cohort Characteristics

3.1
Participant demographics and baseline characteristics are provided in Table[1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13551595/#pul270406-tbl-0001), including age, sex, BMI (available for NU cohorts), and race categories. Race was recorded by self‐report in the electronic health record. The cohort selection process is shown in Figure[5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13551595/#pul270406-fig-0005). Of 488 subjects assessed for eligibility across both institutions, 14 were excluded due to inadequate CT quality (_n_ = 11) or age younger than 18 years (_n_ = 3), resulting in 474 participants included in the final analysis. The final cohort comprised 474 participants (mean age, 51 years ± 16; 299 men), including 417 from NU and 57 from BWH (Figure[5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13551595/#pul270406-fig-0005)).

The BWH cohort included 23 CTEPH participants and 34 control participants, with no significant differences in age (_p_ = 0.79), and sex distribution (_p_ = 0.11) between CTEPH and controls.

#### CTEPH Versus Acute PE (Independent Test Cohort)

3.2.3
As described in Methods, the fixed CTEPH‐versus‐control classifier was applied without retraining to 152 acute PE cases from NU and 50 CTEPH cases (27 from the NU test set and 23 from the BWH dataset), for a total of 202 subjects. The full‐feature model achieved an AUC of 0.89 (95% CI: 0.83–0.93), sensitivity of 0.78 (39/50; 95% CI: 0.66–0.89), specificity of 0.86 (130/152; 95% CI: 0.80–0.91), and accuracy of 0.84 (169/202; 95% CI: 0.79–0.89).

#### External Replication: CTEPH Versus Controls Without Pulmonary Thromboembolism (BWH)

3.2.4
External replication was performed on the independent BWH cohort (23 CTEPH, 34 controls). The full‐feature model achieved an AUC of 0.74 (95% CI: 0.61–0.86), sensitivity of 0.78 (18/23; 95% CI: 0.62–0.95), specificity of 0.71 (24/34; 95% CI: 0.55–0.86), and accuracy of 0.74 (42/57; 95% CI: 0.63–0.84).

#### Comparison With a Simpler Baseline Model

3.2.5
To contextualize the incremental value of the full deep‐learning and radiomics pipeline, we trained a logistic regression model using BV metrics alone (BV1, BV5, BV5‐20, TBV), without clot or periclot radiomic features, and compared its performance to the full model using paired bootstrap testing within each evaluation scenario.

For CTEPH versus controls (NU internal test set), the full model outperformed the BV‐only baseline (AUC 0.875 vs. 0.629; ΔAUC = 0.246, 95% CI: 0.120–0.376; _p_< 0.001). For CTEPH versus acute PE, the full model outperformed the baseline (AUC 0.886 vs. 0.568; ΔAUC = 0.319, 95% CI: 0.227–0.414; _p_< 0.001). For CTEPH versus controls in external replication (BWH), the BV‐only baseline numerically exceeded the full model (AUC 0.767 vs. 0.738), but this difference was not statistically significant (ΔAUC = −0.029, 95% CI: −0.132 to 0.062; _p_ = 0.358).

## [](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13551595/)Discussion

4
In this multicenter study, we developed a fully automated CTPA‐based algorithm that integrates deep learning, radiomics, and machine learning to differentiate CTEPH from patients without evidence of pulmonary thromboembolism and from patients with acute PE. This study demonstrates the feasibility of combining vascular blood‐volume metrics with clot and periclot radiomic descriptors from routine CTPA to distinguish CTEPH from acute PE and controls in a fully automated pipeline. The model showed discrimination capabilities for CTEPH versus controls (AUC 0.87; 95% CI: 0.79, 0.94) and for CTEPH versus acute PE (AUC 0.89; 95% CI: 0.83, 0.93). The lower external performance at a second institution's dataset (AUC 0.74; 95% CI: 0.61, 0.86) indicates that generalizability remains constrained by inter‐site differences in acquisition, reconstruction, and cohort composition. Importantly, excluding periclot features did not significantly reduce discrimination in either the internal (AUC: 0.87–0.84; _p_ = 0.23) or external (AUC: 0.74–0.66; _p_ = 0.21) CTEPH‐versus‐control test sets, and confidence intervals for both differences crossed zero. By contrast, exclusion of periclot features significantly reduced discrimination in the CTEPH‐versus‐acute‐PE comparison (AUC: 0.89–0.71; _p_< 0.001). As the model was neither trained nor threshold‐calibrated for this scenario, this finding should be interpreted as exploratory evidence that periclot features contribute to distinguishing chronic from acute thromboembolic disease, rather than as confirmation of incremental value for the CTEPH‐versus‐control task for which the model was developed. Confirmation of these findings will require dedicated studies incorporating pathological correlation and adequately powered, purpose‐designed cohorts.

A key exploratory and hypothesis‐generating finding is the contribution of periclot radiomic features, which quantify intensity‐distribution heterogeneity in tissue immediately surrounding clot‐positive vessels. The marked ablation‐related performance reduction – particularly for distinguishing chronic from acute thromboembolic disease, for which the model was not trained or calibrated – suggests that periclot characteristics capture disease‐related signals not adequately represented by luminal density or clot area alone. These findings are consistent with the known pathology of CTEPH, where chronic thrombus organization, fibrosis, and vessel‐wall remodeling may alter local tissue composition and microstructure in ways that differ from acute embolic obstruction. However, confirmation in a study specifically designed and calibrated for that clinical comparison is required.

Model interpretability analyses further supported this multiscale signature. Normalized BV1 emerged as the most influential feature, consistent with the concept that distal vascular loss and pruning are central to chronic thromboembolic remodeling and may be detectable as reduced small‐vessel blood volume. However, BV1 should be interpreted as a global vascular morphometric marker derived from the full segmented intraparenchymal vascular tree rather than as a surrogate for the clot‐centered analysis. By contrast, the periclot radiomic features were extracted locally around clot‐positive vessel cross‐sections and were designed to capture lesion‐adjacent attenuation heterogeneity that may reflect chronic thrombus organization, fibrosis, and vessel‐wall remodeling. These feature families therefore operate at different spatial scales and are likely complementary rather than redundant. This interpretation is supported by the ablation analysis, in which excluding periclot features reduced discrimination from 0.89 to 0.71 for CTEPH versus acute PE, from 0.87 to 0.84 in the internal CTEPH‐versus‐control test set, and from 0.74 to 0.66 in the external test set. The higher discriminative weight of BV1 relative to BV5 likely reflects the preferential obliteration of sub‐millimeter vessels in CTEPH, where progressive fibrotic occlusion disproportionately affects the smallest vascular compartments. BV5, encompassing a broader vessel size range, may capture intermediate‐caliber disease that partially overlaps with acute PE, reducing its specificity as a standalone discriminator. Texture‐based measures (including density kurtosis and periclot variance, skewness, and kurtosis) were also among the highest‐impact predictors, indicating that microstructural heterogeneity within and around clot‐positive regions contributes meaningfully to classification. The consistent importance of clot area distribution features and intermediate vessel‐volume measures (e.g., BV5‐20) suggests that the model leverages complementary information spanning small‐vessel remodeling, thrombus burden, and broader vascular distribution.

The stability analysis across 5,000 random train/test splits demonstrated that the same subset of predictors repeatedly ranked among the top contributors, supporting robustness of feature attribution within the NU development cohort. This is important because explainability methods can be sensitive to sampling variation, correlated predictors, and model tuning; repeated resampling provides evidence that the model's reliance on BV1 and periclot/density heterogeneity is not driven by a single partition of the data. Taken together, these findings align with prior work linking CT‐derived blood‐volume metrics (including reductions in BV5 and BV5/TBV) to pulmonary vascular disease and perfusion abnormalities [[18](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13551595/#pul270406-bib-0018), [33](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13551595/#pul270406-bib-0033), [34](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13551595/#pul270406-bib-0034)], and they extend that framework by highlighting periclot radiomics as an additional, discriminative signal.

Comparison to a blood‐volume‐only logistic regression baseline showed statistically significant incremental discrimination for the full pipeline (incorporating clot and periclot radiomics) in the NU internal comparisons (CTEPH vs controls and CTEPH vs acute PE; ΔAUC = 0.246 and 0.319, respectively; both _p_< 0.001). In external replication, however, the BV‐only baseline achieved a numerically superior, though not statistically significant, performance relative to the full model (ΔAUC = −0.029, 95% CI: −0.132‐0.062; _p_ = 0.358). These results indicate that the added value of clot and periclot radiomics observed internally does not fully generalize to the smaller external cohort, and may reflect inter‐site differences in acquisition, reconstruction, or cohort‐composition. Therefore, prospective multicenter validation with protocol diversity and harmonization strategies is needed to determine whether the full radiomic pipeline offers consistent incremental diagnostic value across settings.

Clinically, an automated CTPA‐based approach could support earlier recognition of CTEPH by flagging patients whose vascular and perivascular signatures are more consistent with chronic thromboembolic disease, including in settings where CTEPH expertise or adjunct modalities are limited. Such a tool could function as a triage mechanism to prompt confirmatory evaluation (e.g., V/Q scintigraphy, hemodynamic assessment, or referral to a CTEPH center) rather than as a standalone diagnostic test. The observed drop in external performance underscores the need for additional multicenter testing, protocol diversity, and potential harmonization strategies prior to translation into routine clinical workflows.

This study has several limitations. First, the analysis was retrospective and derived from two academic institutions without standardized research imaging protocols, which may limit generalizability across scanners, reconstruction kernels, and contrast‐timing practices. At NU, CTEPH cases were accrued between 2016 and 2022, while control and acute PE cases were accrued between 2020 and 2023. This largely non‐overlapping temporal window introduces a risk of scanner‐ or protocol‐driven batch effects. Because first‐order radiomic texture features are sensitive to variations in acquisition platforms and reconstruction parameters, this temporal non‐overlap represents a potential, unquantified threat to internal validity that cannot be fully excluded in the present analysis. Prospective, protocol‐harmonized data collection is therefore an important priority for future validation work. Second, our dataset features an artificially enriched prevalence of CTEPH compared to routine clinical practice, where the disease is exceedingly rare among the general screening population undergoing CTPA. Consequently, the positive predictive value in a real‐world clinical screening context would likely be lower than our reported metrics. Third, the model was evaluated exclusively against acute PE and patients without evidence of pulmonary thromboembolism; it was not tested against other clinically important causes of pulmonary hypertension, such as primary pulmonary arterial hypertension or left heart disease, limiting the assessment of its diagnostic specificity across the broader pulmonary hypertension spectrum. Fourth, the internal CTEPH cohort was subject to selection bias, as it predominantly comprised patients undergoing surgical pulmonary thromboendarterectomy. Because these patients likely represent more severe, central disease, the algorithm's applicability for detecting earlier, non‐operable, or predominantly distal disease remains to be evaluated. Fifth, the clot‐related neural networks were trained and validated exclusively on synthetic data. Real, expert‐annotated CTPA vessels were not available to evaluate performance against clinical ground truth. Therefore, we performed stratified evaluation across vessel CSA and clot occlusion burden on synthetic patches and assessed robustness under simulated noise and blur exceeding the training distribution. Performance under baseline conditions was high (AUC ≥ 0.93, RE ≤ 3.4%) across CSA groups but decreased with higher noise or blur (AUC to 0.86–0.87, RE up to 7.1% at the most severe perturbations). The decline was numerically largest in the smallest vessel CSA group. While these synthetic robustness analyses are consistent with the expected sensitivity of small‐vessel edge and texture detection to image degradation, they do not substitute for validation on real annotated clinical data, and residual synthetic‐to‐clinical domain shift cannot be excluded. Acquisition of a real annotated validation dataset is an important priority for future work. Moreover, our radiomic feature set relied on first‐order intensity statistics from lumen, clot, and periclot regions, and did not directly encode established morphologic descriptors of chronic thromboembolic disease (e.g., clot eccentricity, clot‐wall angle, wall thickening, or vessel tapering). Although our synthetic generator includes structural clot morphology (e.g., wall‐adherent _vs._ central clot position), these were not used as supervised outputs or downstream features in the present analysis. Explicit geometric segmentation of clot boundaries is susceptible to partial‐volume artifacts in small‐caliber vessels and may be unreliable for routine clinical CTPA. We hypothesize that certain radiomic texture measures may partially and indirectly reflect such morphologies (e.g., wall‐adherent clots could plausibly alter local density skewness). However, this correspondence was not tested in this study. The lack of direct mapping between our radiomic features and semantic visual descriptors is a limitation and incorporating or formally testing such descriptors is an important extension for future work. Sixth, we did not incorporate clinical variables or hemodynamics, which are critical components of a holistic CTEPH diagnosis in clinical workflows. We also did not benchmark model performance against routine or expert radiologist interpretation. While a formal multi‐reader comparison was beyond the scope of this work, it is an important next step to establish incremental clinical value over expert visual assessment. Seventh, while external validation is a methodological strength, the external cohort was relatively small (23 CTEPH cases and 34 controls), underscoring the need for larger, multicenter prospective validation to confirm generalizability. Notably, a baseline model restricted to macroscopic blood‐volume metrics demonstrated comparable, and numerically superior, performance to the full radiomic pipeline within this external cohort. This finding underscores that the incremental diagnostic value of clot and periclot radiomics remains preliminary, necessitating further validation in larger, independent multicenter studies. Finally, the statistically significant periclot‐ablation effect was observed only in the CTEPH versus acute‐PE comparison, for which the model was not trained nor threshold‐calibrated. Whether the corresponding non‐significant findings in the CTEPH‐versus‐control comparisons (both internal and external) reflect a true absence of periclot contribution to that task, or insufficient statistical power given the smaller sample sizes (_n_ = 80 and _n_ = 57, respectively), cannot be determined from the present data. Adequately powered, purpose‐designed studies are needed to resolve this distinction.

In summary, this study demonstrates that combining vascular blood‐volume metrics, clot‐related measures, and periclot radiomic descriptors from routine CTPA can differentiate CTEPH from controls and acute PE, with evidence of external generalizability. Future work should include larger multisite validation with broader protocol variability, assessment of calibration and clinical decision thresholds, and integration of clinical and hemodynamic data and longitudinal imaging to evaluate diagnostic utility, monitoring capability, and real‐world impact.
