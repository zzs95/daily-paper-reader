Abstract: 

While considerable effort has been devoted to examining how variations in study protocols, acquisition settings, and annotations 

influence radiomics features, the role of feature selection (FS) methods largely remains unexplored. This study investigates a self-

supervised deep sparse autoencoder ensemble (ensembleAE) and a novel Bayesian variant (bayesianAE) for radiomics FS in a 

small, class-imbalanced framework. Using a cohort of 100 prostate cancer patients under active surveillance, these models were 

benchmarked against eight classical FS methods. FS stability was assessed both globally and locally in a soft data-perturbation 

setting. While global stability measured consistency in overall feature ranking, local stability quantified the agreement in selecting 

top-ranked feature subsets. Among classical methods, wrappers were the least stable, while the filter-based Wilcoxon test (WLCX) 

exhibited high local stability and performance with moderate global stability. Conversely, bayesianAE achieved the highest global 

stability while matching WLCX’s local stability and performance. Furthermore, although bayesianAE yielded local stability 

comparable to ensembleAE, it surpassed ensembleAE in terms of global stability while significantly reducing computational 

burden (95x faster and consumes 84% less memory). These findings position bayesianAE as a robust alternative to classical FS 

methods that could support the development of reproducible radiomics signatures.

Autoencoder, Radiomics, Feature Selection, Stability, Prostate Cancer, Multiparametric MRI 

Introduction: 

Radiomics features are known to be susceptible to variations due to the changes in the study protocol 1,2 , acquisition 

parameters 3,4 , and region-of-interest segmentation 5,6 , to name a few, which have been extensively studied in recent years for 

the development of robust signatures. These factors may be considered “external”, given that they are beyond the direct 

influence of the choices involved in the core analytic components ( feature extraction, and analysis ) of the radiomics workflow 7.

On the contrary, a factor that may be considered “internal” yet frequently overlooked can be attributed to the role of the 

underlying feature selection (FS) algorithm and the classifier that are involved in signature development and validation 8. Although 

these factors may be treated separately, the choice of the selection algorithm and the subsequent use of a classifier are often 

interdependent. Together, they constitute the machine learning (ML) pipeline. An FS algorithm mines and selects a small subset 

of features, known as a radiomics signature, from a large pool of quantitative imaging features 7. This is carried out to enhance 

model performance by mitigating the common challenges posed by the curse of dimensionality, limited sample size, and model 

overfitting, all while improving interpretability 9–11 . Classifiers leverage these signatures to approximate a function that maps the 

features and the target 12 . In this context, the FS algorithm plays a pivotal role in radiomics signature development, and its stability, 

i.e., the consistency of the selected features under slight perturbations 13 , is a crucial factor that requires further investigation. 

The instability of radiomics signature may stem from several factors. An obvious difference may be attributed to the choice of 

the selection algorithm 14 , but factors such as small sample size, redundancy (or correlation) of features, optimization metric, 

distribution shifts owed to the partitioning of training/test folds, and, class imbalance also contribute to instability even within 

## ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT ARTICLE IN PRESS ARTICLE IN PRESS features selected by the same underlying FS algorithm 15–20 . Unstable selection results raise concerns about the trustworthiness 

of these signatures. Although external validation can assess the robustness of these selected features 21,22 , it is typically impractical 

for clinical applications involving rare diseases, treatments, or new protocols. In such cases, researchers rely on internal cross-

validation (CV) to estimate a robust performance metric of the signature without a separate test set 23 . However, signatures 

identified in this framework are typically selected using FS algorithms optimized to improve the performance metric over the 

entire dataset 24 . Otherwise, variations in the training/validation fold could yield different selection outputs for each fold, 

especially in a class-imbalanced low-shot data setting. This is a classic example of data leakage that produces over-optimistic CV 

performance estimates. In fact, it has been reported that such incorrect use of FS could lead to a bias of up to 0.15 in the area 

under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) 25 , a popular performance metric used in the radiomics literature 21 .

FS methods used in radiomics generally fall into three categories: filter, embedded, and wrapper methods. Filter algorithms 

typically rank or select features by evaluating the statistical properties of the data (supervised or unsupervised) independent of 

the downstream classification algorithm. While they often evaluate features in isolation (eg., t-test and variants), multivariable 

filter methods (eg., variance inflation factor [VIF]) also exist. On the other hand, embedded and wrapper methods account for 

feature dependence and directly incorporate the classifier's performance during feature selection, making them both inherently 

supervised. The former implicitly rank or select features based on importance assigned during sample classification (e.g., least 

absolute shrinkage and selection operator [LASSO]) while the latter recursively exploit this behavior with the help of an iterative 

greedy search (e.g., sequential forward/backward selection [SFS/SBS], recursive feature elimination [RFE]) or population-based 

meta-heuristic optimization algorithms (e.g., genetic algorithms). 

Only a handful of studies have examined the impact of feature selection algorithms in the context of radiomics analysis 15,26–28 .

These studies typically limit their focus on the filter 15 or embedded 26 methods to offset computational complexity, although, in 

practice, wrapper FS methods are also popular in radiomics signature development 29–33 . Furthermore, these studies generally 

focus on the predictive performance of signatures returned by the FS methods rather than their stability. On the other hand, 

several attempts have been made to assess the stability of FS methods in the context of biomolecular data 13,20,34–38 . This may be 

attributed to the fact that selected features there often represent biological descriptors such as interesting genes, proteins, or 

single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), which may be investigated further to identify a causal relationship, which, as of now, is 

not clearly defined in radiomics. New FS methods have also been explored in this domain 39–41 . Particularly, we would like to point 

out a filter-based FS method that uses a self-supervised (or unsupervised) deep learning approach involving deep sparse 

autoencoder (AE) ensembles 42 to identify SNPs associated with toxicity after radiotherapy for prostate cancer 40 . This method 

leverages the concept of anomaly or outlier detection to approximate the distribution shift associated with the majority and 

minority classes. Although this algorithm may fall into the category of filter FS methods, unlike most, it is unsupervised and can 

model feature dependence and non-linear behaviors, all while being robust to class imbalance. 

In this study, we explored the aforementioned deep sparse autoencoder ensemble method (ensembleAE) 42 for FS in a real-world 

radiomics application. A standard (i.e., standalone) deep sparse autoencoder (singleAE) was included as a reference. To offset 

the high computational cost associated with the ensemble approach, we also propose a novel Bayesian formulation 43 of the 

singleAE, which we call “bayesianAE”. The stability, predictive performance and the computational cost of all these AE variants 

were then compared with popular classical FS methods. This included (i) three filter-based approaches: Wilcoxon rank sum (WLCX, 

a.k.a Mann-Whitney U-test), mutual information maximization (MIM), and minimum redundancy maximum relevance (MRMR); 

(ii) one embedded method: LASSO; and finally, (iii) wrapper methods built on top of the iterative greedy SBS (a.k.a backward 

elimination) using four inductive algorithms: logistic regression (LR; SBS+LR) , linear-support vector machine (L-SVM; SBS+L-SVM), 

random forest (RF; SBS+RF), and multi-layer perceptron (MLP; SBS+MLP). To quantify their stability in a practical setting, all FS 

algorithms were applied to baseline whole-prostate multiparametric MR (mpMR) radiomics features to select subsets predictive 

of Gleason score upgrade (GS > 3+3; i.e., grade reclassification) in a cohort of 100 prostate cancer patients undergoing active 

surveillance (AS). Note that, our goal was not to derive a radiomics signature for the clinical endpoint, but rather to benchmark 

the stability of AE-based FS methods relative to classical approaches, while also contextualizing their behavior using secondary 

metrics such as computational cost, predictive performance and signature similarity.

Table 1 summarizes the mean stability estimates associated with the eight classical FS methods and the three autoencoder 

variants investigated in this study. A random FS algorithm was also included as a baseline to aid in the interpretation of the results. 

The stability of FS algorithms was assessed both globally and locally. The Spearman rank index was used to quantify the overall 

(global) feature ranking stability, whereas measures such as the Dice index, Jaccard index, Kuncheva overlap index, and maximum 

weighted matching (MWM) cost, evaluated at various top-k feature selections (k = 5, 10, 15, 20, 25), were used to quantify local 

stability. While Table 1 reports all these metrics, the results presented in subsequent paragraphs focus on three key stability 

measures: the global Spearman rank index, the Kuncheva overlap index, and the MWM cost. Of these, both local metrics were 

evaluated at k = 5. Figure 1 presents a simplified illustration of the stability characteristics of all FS algorithms across these three 

key measures, whereas a detailed distribution plot is provided in the supplementary materials (SM; see Figure S1). Note that, 

while the MWM cost may appear disproportionally larger in magnitude compared to the Kuncheva index, it should be interpreted 

in relation to the random selection baseline, whereas the Kuncheva index inherently adjusts for such chance selections. Finally, 

it is important to emphasize that, in practice, the local stability of an FS algorithm is far more critical than global stability, since 

radiomics signatures usually only constitute the top-k selections. 

Among classical methods, the embedded method, LASSO yielded the highest definitive Spearman rank index of 0.94, followed by 

two filter methods MIM, and WLCX, with values of 0.73, and 0.53, respectively. Of these, the WLCX method also demonstrated 

high local stability (Kuncheva index = 0.39; MWM cost = 0.57), making it the most robust classical method. On the other hand, 

both LASSO and MIM demonstrated low top-5 feature selection stability. For LASSO, the Kuncheva index was observed to be 

0.23, whereas this metric was as low as 0.15 for MIM. Similarly, their respective MWM costs were 0.48 and 0.43, representing 

marginal improvements of 0.13 and 0.08 over the random selection baseline (0.35). This discrepancy between global and local 

stability indicates that their high Spearman rank indices do not represent consistent feature ranking across runs. Rather for LASSO 

this stems from its consistency in shrinking a large fraction (55%) of feature coefficients to exactly zero. While for MIM, this may 

be attributed to the nearly 27% of features for which, on average the mutual information value was zero. Indeed, if evaluated 

solely based on the Spearman rank index of their non-zero coefficients, the stability of these methods drop significantly (LASSO 

vs. MIM: 0.26 vs. 0.21; see SM Figures S2-S3). MRMR also followed a similar trend regarding global (Spearman index = 0.52) and 

local stability (Kuncheva index = 0.19; MWM cost = 0.49). Conversely, the SBS-based wrapper methods consistently yielded low 

global (Spearman index range: 0.11–0.22) and local stability (Kuncheva index range: 0.08–0.13; MWM cost range: 0.40–0.43). 

Conversely, all autoencoder variants showed balanced performance with consistent global and local stability. Among these, 

bayesianAE demonstrated the highest global stability (Spearman index = 0.72), followed by ensembleAE (0.65), and finally 

singleAE (0.53). Concerning local stability, both bayesianAE and ensembleAE behaved very similarly, with their top-5 Kuncheva 

indices and MWM costs clustering around ~0.36 and ~0.57, respectively. Specifically, ensembleAE scored marginally higher on 

the Kuncheva index (ensembleAE vs bayesianAE: 0.38 vs. 0.36), whereas bayesianAE performed slightly better on the MWM cost 

(0.57 vs. 0.58). In both cases, singleAE demonstrated slightly lower performance (Kuncheva index = 0.30; MWM cost = 0.52). 

Finally, although bayesianAE outperformed ensembleAE by a higher margin on the global stability (Δ Spearman index = 0.07, p < 

0.0001), their differences in terms of average local top-5 selection stability measures were negligible yet statistically significant 

(Δ Kuncheva index = 0.02, p < 0.0001; Δ MWM cost = 0.01, p = 0.01), 

When comparing the selection stability of classical FS methods with AE variants, the AE methods demonstrated balanced global 

and local stability. They outperformed SBS wrapper methods across all metrics, while displaying better or comparable 

performance to the most stable classical FS method, WLCX. In terms of global stability, the best-performing AE method, 

bayesianAE (Spearman index = 0.72), outperformed WLCX (0.53) by a significant margin (Δ Spearman index = 0.19, p < 0.0001). 

Conversely, for the local top-5 Kuncheva index, the best-performing methods, ensembleAE (0.38) and WLCX (0.39) demonstrated 

no statistically significant difference in stability (Δ = 0.01, p = 0.83), whereas with bayesianAE (0.36), although the mean difference 

was negligible, it was significant (Δ = 0.03, p < 0.0001). For MWM cost, bayesianAE (0.58) and WLCX (0.57) also yielded marginally 

close mean stability estimates, yet they were statistically different (Δ = 0.01, p < 0.0001), whereas as WLCX with ensembleAE 

(0.57) demonstrated no statistical difference (Δ = 0.0, p = 0.99). Of note, while WLCX remains competitive at top-5 selections, 

both bayesian and ensemble AE variants demonstrate better scalability at larger top-k selections. For example, at k = 25,

Signature Analysis 

The radiomics signature associated with each FS method was derived by applying the method to the full available dataset and 

selecting the top-5 features. These signatures were selected in the context of predicting Gleason grade reclassification (GS > 6) 

among 100 prostate cancer patients undergoing active surveillance. The resulting signatures are presented in SM Figure S4. Note 

that our objective was to examine the relationships among the signatures generated by different FS methods, rather than to 

propose an optimal radiomics signature for clinical use. 

While no single radiomics feature was unanimously selected across all FS algorithms, distinct consistency patterns were observed 

within and across FS method families. Specifically, all classical filter methods reached a consensus on exactly one radiomics 

feature, with a stronger localized agreement between WLCX and MRMR, which shared two of their top-5 selected features. 

Likewise, all AE variants shared one common feature, with a strong pairwise agreement between singleAE and ensembleAE, which 

shared four features. On the other hand, bayesianAE shared two features with singleAE but only one with ensembleAE. Crucially, 

there was absolutely no feature overlap between the signatures selected by classical filter and AE method families. The wrapper 

family exhibited no overall internal consensus, though its linear variants (SBS+LR and SBS+L-SVM) shared three features, while 

non-linear variants shared none. Finally, the embedded LASSO method shared three common features with SBS+L-SVM and one 

feature each with MRMR and ensembleAE. 

In terms of correlations, the signatures derived from both single and ensemble AE variants demonstrated the strongest positive 

correlation (Pearson = 0.83; Spearman = 0.83). In contrast, correlations between bayesianAE and other AE methods were only 

low-to-moderate (Pearson range: 0.47–0.53; Spearman range: 0.47–0.53). Similarly, within wrapper SBS methods, the linear 

variants showed high positive correlation (0.78; 0.74), whereas the non-linear variants exhibited substantially weaker correlation 

(0.34; 0.28). On the other hand, the classical filter methods demonstrated low-to-moderate correlations amongst themselves 

(0.39–0.63; 0.37–0.60). Across FS families, the embedded LASSO method displayed low-to-moderate correlations with SBS 

variants (0.46–0.65; 0.48–0.64), while exhibiting weak associations both with the classical filters (0.32–0.43; 0.25–0.48) and AE 

variants (0.25–0.42; 0.26–0.43). Finally, AE family demonstrated low cross-family correlation with wrapper methods (0.22–0.47; 

0.22–0.40), while exhibiting slightly higher, though relatively weak correlation with classical filter methods (0.40–0.47; 0.32– 

0.50). SM Figure S5 presents the correlation heatmaps associated with the signatures selected by all investigated FS methods. 

Performance Analysis 

To approximate the unbiased predictive performance of the signature, we used micro-averaged AUC. During each perturbation 

run, a multivariable logistic regression model was trained on the training partition using only the top-5 features selected by the 

FS algorithm for that specific run. The model’s predictions on the corresponding test partitions were then aggregated across all 

stability runs to estimate the micro-averaged AUC. Note that this metric does not reflect the performance associated with any 

specific signature but rather quantifies the expected performance of signatures generated by a particular FS algorithm. 

Nevertheless, it can be used as the proxy performance measure of the signature derived from the entire dataset. The results are 

summarized in SM Table S1. 

Overall, the predictive performance of signatures was low to near-moderate (AUC range: 0.52–0.59). Among these, classical filter 

methods demonstrated relatively better AUCs (0.56–0.59), closely followed by AE variants (0.55–0.58). Conversely, the classical 

wrapper (0.54–0.56) and embedded (0.52) methods consistently yielded low AUCs. The top performers in each FS method family 

followed the order: WLCX (filter family, AUC = 0.59) > bayesianAE (AE variants, 0.58) > SBS+RF (wrapper, 0.56) > LASSO 

(embedded, 0.52). DeLong’s test revealed that the difference in predictive performance between the best classical method and 

the best AE variant were not statistically significant (WLCX vs. bayesianAE: 0.59 vs. 0.58, Δ = 0.01, p = 0.18). 

Computational Cost 

## ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT ARTICLE IN PRESS ARTICLE IN PRESS SM Table S2 provides the system specifications used to conduct all experiments. For each FS algorithm, the execution time and 

memory usage were tracked individually across 100 perturbation runs, and the resulting distributions are presented in Figure 2. 

While execution time represents the cumulative duration until the completion of a full perturbation run, for memory usage, only 

peak memory was recorded. This is because, memory is dynamically reused across iterations without accumulating. Note that 

the memory usage of the AE variants was estimated as the combined total of RAM and GPU memory, whereas classical FS 

methods only consumed RAM. 

The computational cost associated with each feature selection algorithm varied substantially, revealing clear resource trade-offs 

between FS families. The embedded LASSO method and classical filters (WLCX, MIM, and MRMR) demonstrated exceptional 

computational efficiency with all completing a single run in under 4 seconds and consuming minimal peak memory (< 3.2 MiB). 

Of these, LASSO was the fastest at 0.04 seconds and utilized the least amount of memory at 0.22 MiB whereas MRMR took the 

longest (3.4 seconds) and utilized the most memory (3.1 MiB). A similar computational efficiency was also observed with the 

simple AE variants (singleAE and bayesianAE), requiring roughly 10 seconds and less than 1.5 MiB of memory. On the other hand, 

the ensemble variant (ensembleAE) drastically increased the computational burden to approximately 16 minutes and 8.9 MiB. 

Overall, the wrapper SBS methods proved to be the most resource-intensive FS family, with costs scaling alongside the complexity 

of the underlying classifier. While linear wrapper variants (SBS+LR and SBS+L-SVM) incurred moderate computational cost 

(execution time range = 3.1–4.8 minutes; memory usage = 4.0–10.2 MiB), the non-linear variants exerted the highest 

computational overhead (17.4–19.4 minutes; 16.4–22.7 MiB). Among these, SBS+RF recorded the longest average execution time 

(19.4 minutes), while SBS+MLP exerted the largest memory footprint, peaking at 22.7 MiB. 

Discussion: 

We present self-supervised deep sparse autoencoders as a technique for feature selection in radiomics analysis. Three variants 

were explored—a standalone AE (singleAE), its collection (ensembleAE), and finally a novel Bayesian formulation (bayesianAE) 

that allows a singleAE to approximate uncertainty like an ensemble method but with significantly less time and computational 

cost. We evaluated the selection stability of these methods against classical filter, embedded and wrapper FS algorithms, 

considering consistency in both global feature ranking and top-k subset selection. In doing so, we presented a general framework 

for FS stability analysis in radiomics studies. These evaluations were conducted in the context of predicting GS upgrade (GS > 6) 

using baseline radiomics in a cohort of 100 prostate cancer patients undergoing active surveillance. Importantly, this study was 

not designed to propose a clinical radiomics signature, but rather to systematically benchmark the stability, signature 

composition, performance, and computational cost of AE-based FS methods against classical approaches. 

Our findings suggest that self-supervised AE methods, particularly bayesianAE offers a highly robust, computationally efficient, 

and stable alternative to classical FS methods for radiomics signature development in small, class-imbalanced datasets, especially 

in the absence of external validation. However, as deep-learning models, the AE family requires specialized hardware supporting 

GPU acceleration. In this context, the WLCX filter method, being the top-performer among classical methods, could serve as a 

potential alternative. Although WLCX yielded a relatively low global stability compared to bayesianAE, it maintained a 

comparable, if not, marginally better local stability and predictive performance. In practice, local stability is far more critical than 

global stability because clinical datasets typically contain a low incidence of events, meaning only the top five or fewer features 

truly constitute the final radiomics signature. SBS methods on the other hand, exhibited major shortcomings across all benchmark 

measures, rendering them suboptimal for signature development in such contexts. Finally, while the embedded LASSO method 

was the most computationally efficient algorithm with the highest global stability, it demonstrated exceeding poor local stability 

and predictive performance. 

A critical finding in our study is the importance of quantifying the stability of FS algorithms using both global and local stability 

metrics. In particular, relying solely on global metrics like the Spearman rank index may yield overly optimistic estimates for FS 

methods that assign the same ranking to a large fraction of features. For instance, in our study, while LASSO demonstrated the 

highest Spearman rank index of 0.94, its local top-5 selection stability was exceedingly low (Kuncheva index = 0.23; MWM cost = 

0.48). This discrepancy was identified to be an algorithmic artifact, as LASSO consistently shrank a large fraction of feature 

coefficients to exactly zero. In fact, when evaluated solely on its non-zero coefficients, its global stability dropped significantly 

(Spearman index = 0.26). Note that, this observation does not undermine the utility of LASSO or the metric. On the contrary, it 

## ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT ARTICLE IN PRESS ARTICLE IN PRESS suggests that although the relative ranking that LASSO assigns to non-zero coefficients is highly inconsistent, the algorithm still 

holds value for its ability to consistently identify and eliminate non-informative features. MIM also demonstrated a similar 

pattern. 

Among AE methods, bayesianAE demonstrated the highest global FS stability, followed by ensembleAE, and singleAE. Conversely, 

local stability was comparable between bayesianAE and ensembleAE. The ability of such uncertainty-aware models to perform 

better than standalone methods is well-known in machine learning 20,44 . By aggregating multiple outputs, these models can offset 

the variance or uncertainty present in individual predictions, leading to a more reliable and stable behavior. However, training a 

large number of autoencoder models end-to-end using stochastic gradient descent to construct an ensemble is computationally 

expensive. The bayesianAE variant proposed in this study is a far superior alternative for stable feature selection. Importantly, in 

addition to demonstrating better global stability, it is nearly 95-fold faster and consumes roughly 84% less memory compared to 

ensembleAE. 

The low stability observed for sequential selection-based wrapper methods is not new and is well documented in the literature 

of biomolecular data analysis. In fact, Dunne et al 34 first introduced similarity-based stability measures to quantify the instability 

inherent to these methods, specifically in forward and backward elimination techniques. Other popular wrapper methods have 

also been widely criticized for their low stability, especially in microarray analysis. For example, Yu et al 37 observed that the 

stability measured based on Dice overlap in RFE-based L-SVM was nearly zero between the top-k genes selected by the algorithm 

on the entire microarray dataset with those chosen from each of its partitions. Davis et al 45 also observed similar low stability for 

the L-SVM-based wrapper method in their study; however, they quantified stability based on the frequency with which a gene 

was selected repeatedly among the top-ranked genes in a soft-perturbation setting rather than overlap. Haury et al 36 , while 

assessing the stability of many classical FS methods, including wrapper methods, also observed a similar behavior in soft and hard 

(no overlap between data partitions) perturbation settings. They further highlighted the dependence of selection results on the 

underlying FS method. Davis et al 45 , and Haury et al 36 also included filter-based FS alongside wrapper methods that demonstrated 

relatively high stability in their analysis. 

Only a handful of studies have investigated FS stability in the context of radiomics, but their findings are consistent with those of 

biomolecular data analysis. For instance, Demircioğlu et al 27 reported that the selection of radiomics features was strongly 

dependent on the underlying FS method, with embedded methods such as LASSO and ExtraTrees exhibiting low stability 

compared to class-agnostic filter methods. Our findings are to a certain degree consistent with their observation, where LASSO 

indeed demonstrated low local stability compared to WLCX. However, compared to MIM and MRMR, LASSO either performed 

marginally better or worse depending on the metric considered for local stability quantification. Demircioğlu et al 27 also noted 

that radiomics models with statistically similar predictive performance (based on DeLong’s test) might rely on different, though 

potentially correlated feature subsets and urged caution when reporting features associated with the best-performing model as 

candidate biomarkers. In contrast, our results demonstrate that methods with statistical similarity do not necessarily return highly 

correlated feature subsets. Specifically, when we compared the top-5 selected features returned by best methods: WLCX and 

bayesianAE, despite having statistically similar predictive performance (DeLong’s p = 0.18), they showed a low signature 

correlation (Pearson = 0.41; Spearman = 0.38). Of note, unlike Demircioğlu et al 27 , we removed all highly correlated features 

before applying any of the FS methods. This suggests that the observations of Demircioğlu et al 27 may likely apply in situations 

where signatures are derived without eliminating highly correlated features. Finally, Parmar et al 15 , performed stability analysis 

solely on filter-based methods and highlighted the Wilcoxon test-based FS method as being relatively high in stability and 

performance. Our findings are consistent with this observation. Nevertheless, these studies limited their investigation to 

embedded and/or filter-based FS methods, excluding the commonly employed wrapper methods in radiomics analysis 29–33 ,

mainly to avoid the high computational complexity. This prompted our investigation on the stability of classical wrapper-based 

FS methods, in addition to filter and embedded methods in radiomics analysis. 

Unlike popular stability metrics in radiomics studies 46 , such as the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) or concordance 

correlation coefficient (CCC), the interpretations of FS stability measures are not well defined and are relative. However, as a rule 

of thumb higher positive values indicate better stability. Nonetheless, the interpretation of these similarity measures can be 

misleading without an understanding of what the measure signifies in relation to a reference method. This is what drove us to 

## ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT ARTICLE IN PRESS ARTICLE IN PRESS incorporate a random FS method to serve as a reference, and we strongly recommend its inclusion in future stability studies. 

Ideally, all FS algorithms should return a mean stability value at least as good as the random FS algorithm. 

It is important to recognize that high FS stability does not always translate to high predictive performance 47 . However, it can be 

indicative of a more consistent and/or realistic performance that can be expected from the predictive model. This is more 

apparent in studies comparing the performance of signatures selected by filter-based methods with those derived from complex 

embedded 28 or wrapper approaches, where filter methods yield relatively low yet consistent performance compared to the 

latter 34 . While, this may hold true in certain aspects, in this study, the filter method WLCX demonstrated higher predictive 

performance than embedded and wrapper methods. A major drawback of univariable filter methods such as the WLCX, is their 

inability to account for feature relationships. Variants based on multivariable statistics can address some of these limitations but 

tend to be slower and less stable compared to their univariable counterparts 13 , and are rarely used in practice. This encouraged 

our exploration of self-supervised autoencoder variants in the context of radiomics. In our study, the bayesianAE-derived 

signature demonstrated AUC comparable to that of the top-performing WLCX method with no statistical difference (bayesianAE 

vs. WLCX: 0.58 vs. 0.59, Delong’s p = 0.18). This suggests that ‘unsupervised’ AE variants can select signatures with performance 

that is in par with the best-supervised classical methods while maintaining high stability. Although our evaluation of the predictive 

power of AE signature was limited to a small dataset, Massi et al 42 , when proposed ensembleAE for feature selection, empirically 

investigated the predictive performance on several benchmark datasets against classical filter (Chi-squared test), and wrapper 

methods (RFE-based L-SVM). They observed that it either outperformed or achieved comparable performance in nearly all 

experiments, regardless of the classifier used for performance evaluation. Our work may be viewed as an extension of theirs, 

with a clear focus on feature selection stability, while also proposing a Bayesian alternative to ensembleAE. 

Developing and validating a robust, stability-aware clinical signature from a small, imbalanced dataset would require a 

substantially different analytical framework than the one utilized here, which was exclusively designed for stability quantification. 

Ideally, such a framework should incorporate reproducibility studies, including inter-observer segmentation variability, test-retest 

reliability, or phantom-based assessments (when available), to offset the unwanted influence of external factors on radiomics 

features. At the same time, the ML pipeline should be carefully designed to avoid the risk of optimization bias. This is particularly 

important when multiple design choices (such as alternative FS methods and/or predictive models) are explored. Selecting the 

final configuration solely based on predictive performance can lead to substantial overestimation, a phenomenon commonly 

referred to as the “winner’s curse” 48 . In such situations, objectively quantifying the true unbiased out-of-sample performance 

requires rigorous methodological procedures like stratified nested cross-validation. For detailed guidance on signature 

development in such contexts, readers are referred to relevant studies 19,48 . However, implementing a fully nested pipelines 

across large number of design choices can be extremely complex. In this context, stability analysis offers a pragmatic alternative 

to exclude unstable FS algorithms before applying it for signature development. 

We identified several limitations in this study that could benefit from further investigation and/or open new research directions. 

First, we focused exclusively on the stability of feature selection methods in radiomics analysis. This was done to emphasize the 

often-overlooked aspect of radiomics signature variations due to the methodological choices rather than to propose a definitive 

clinical biomarker. Accordingly, for simplicity, the input feature space was restricted to original radiomics features extracted 

without the use of any image preprocessing filters. Similarly, no reproducibility studies were performed to remove features 

sensitive to external sources of variation. Second, the stability of FS methods was evaluated on a relatively small, single-center, 

class-imbalanced dataset targeting a very specific clinical endpoint. Moreover, to further isolate the effects of FS algorithms, 

predictive performance was only assessed using a simple multivariable logistic regression model. Future studies incorporating 

multiple benchmark datasets across diverse clinical endpoints, and a broader range of classifiers are therefore needed to assess 

the generalizability of our findings. Another limitation is associated with the data perturbation setting that we utilized in the 

study. Although a soft-perturbation framework may be preferred while working with a limited sample size to avoid a huge data 

distribution shift, a hard-perturbation setting is a better proxy for a conservative stability quantification across a wide variety of 

use cases 36 . Finally, while bayesianAE variant emerged as the most stable method across all FS methods investigated in this study, 

it is worth exploring ensemble of classical methods or hybrids, or the ones that possess provable statistical properties in yielding 

consistent results, such as stability-path-based approaches 49 , for a more comprehensive analysis. These methods are not popular 

in radiomics literature; hence, we did not explore them. 

## ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT ARTICLE IN PRESS ARTICLE IN PRESS Radiomics, leveraging both handcrafted and deep features, is a rapidly growing field. Yet, unlike the field of biomolecular data 

analysis, relatively few studies have examined the stability of feature selection methods during signature development. This is 

surprising considering that both fields share the same goal of identifying robust biomarkers. The radiomics quality score (RQS) 

introduced in 2017 (RQS 1.0) 21 established a foundational framework for best practices, which can be used to grade radiomics 

studies. While it recommended removing features sensitive to external sources of variability (such as segmentation differences, 

scanner/vendor differences, and longitudinal imaging variations), reliability was largely evaluated through external validation, 

which was viewed as the primary safeguard of robustness and was generously rewarded. In contrast, the updated RQS 2.0 

(2025) 50 now explicitly also rewards studies that include reproducibility analysis, particularly with respect to external factors, 

recognizing their importance in ensuring robustness. Nevertheless, even RQS 2.0 does not fully address the importance of 

carefully designing the internal ML pipeline to avoid optimization bias, nor does it explicitly evaluate the stability of the feature 

selection methods , which, if low, can substantially alter the selected radiomics signature. Thus, the stability of feature selection 

strategies for robust signature development remains an open challenge in radiomics research. This issue is particularly relevant 

in rare treatment protocols or diseases, where acquiring an external dataset, let alone a large enough training dataset, is often 

infeasible. In such situations, in addition to assessing the impact of external factors on stability, evaluating FS stability due to 

small changes in methodological choices may be an effective strategy to propose robust signatures. Our study may be considered 

a small step forward in that direction. 

Methods: 

Dataset 

100 patients diagnosed with very low-risk prostate cancer from a population of mixed active surveillance (AS) protocols were 

utilized in this study. These protocols include the institutional AS cohort (Sorveglianza Attiva Istituto Nazionale Tumori - SAINT) 

defined in 2005, the “Prostate Cancer Research International: Active Surveillance” (PRIAS) protocol 51 since 2007, and personalized 

AS protocol. Although active surveillance started as early as 2005, the use of multiparametric magnetic resonance images 

(mpMRI) was introduced much later, in 2013. We identified 100 patients who underwent at least one prostate mpMRI along with 

subsequent surveillance biopsies. Patients were reclassified based on whether they experienced a Gleason score (GS) upgrade 

(GS > 6) during any of the surveillance biopsies. 8-year follow-up records were considered for censoring. This binary outcome 

formed the basis of our stability study. For each patient, we utilized the earliest available mpMRI scan. The study protocols were 

approved by the local ethical committee (INT 46/07, and INT 95/11), and written informed consent was obtained from all the 

patients. A detailed description of the patient population, study protocols, grade reclassifications, and analyses of treatment-free 

survival are provided in a previously published study 52 .

Image Acquisition and Processing 

MR images were acquired using an “Ingenia” 1.5 T (Philips Medical Systems, Best, The Netherlands) machine equipped with 32-

channel phased-array and spine coils, in combination with an endorectal receiver coil. MR sequences comprised turbo spin echo 

and gradient echo sequences, always including a projection with axial slicing, according to the PI-RADS v2.1 53 recommendations. 

The acquisition protocol (see Table S3 in SM) was standardized: every set of images included T2-weighted (T2w) images, and two 

functional MRI sequences: diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), dynamic contrast-enhanced (DCE) imaging. DWI and DCE images 

were processed further to generate apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) and subtraction (SUB) maps. An experienced radiologist 

(E.G.) delineated the entire prostate gland on T2w images, and the resulting segmentations were propagated to other sequences 

through spatial-resampling. The details of image preprocessing steps are outlined in a previous study 5. In short, intensity-

normalized sequences including T2w, ADC map, and DCE-derived subtraction maps encompassing wash-in (SUB-win) and wash-

out (SUB-wout) phases, along with whole prostate segmentations, were made available for subsequent analysis. It is also 

important to emphasize that the image dimensions were resampled to have a common isotropic voxel size of 2 x 2 x 2 mm 3, with 

third-order B-spline interpolation applied on the in-plane dimension and nearest neighbor interpolation on the out-of-plane 

dimension. 

Radiomics Features 

## ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT ARTICLE IN PRESS ARTICLE IN PRESS The Pyradiomics (v.3.1.0) 54 feature extraction package, a popular open-source tool, was employed for the extraction of unfiltered 

(original) 3D radiomics features from the prostate delineations, resulting in 72 features per sequence (or map). The extracted 

features fall in the category of first-order (n = 18) and texture features (n = 54). Texture features included gray level co-occurrence 

matrix (GLCM, n = 22), gray level size zone matrix (GLSZM, n = 16), and gray level run length matrix (GLRLM, n = 16). For details 

on feature definitions, we urge the reader to refer to the Pyradiomics documentation ( https://pyradiomics.readthedocs.io ). Size 

and shape features were excluded, since there is conflicting evidence in terms of the relation between prostate gland morphology 

and Gleason score upgrade 55–57 . After normalization, a bin-width discretization strategy with a bin size of 5 was selected to ensure 

that the resulting number of bins falls in the desired range of 30 to 130. In literature 58 , this range is shown as having good 

performance and reproducibility. Additionally, we posit that opting for such a smaller bin width may capture fine-grained tissue 

information within the whole prostate gland since nearly 50% of patients in AS do not exhibit MR visible lesions 59–61 . For simplicity, 

we chose not to incorporate radiomics features obtained after subjecting the image to numerous filtering strategies. Our 

objective is not to propose or establish a radiomics signature but rather to investigate the stability of feature selection strategies. 

Collectively, 288 mpMR whole prostate quantitative radiomics features were extracted. Subsequently, near-zero variance and 

highly correlated features were eliminated, leaving 89 features for further analysis. Details of these filtering strategies are 

provided in SM, section S1. 

Feature Selection using Classical Methods 

Classical feature selection methods examined in this study encompassed three filter-based approaches: WLCX, MIM, and MRMR; 

one embedded method: LASSO; and wrapper methods built on top of the greedy backward elimination using four distinct 

inductive algorithms: SBS+LR, SBS+L-SVM, SBS+RF, and SBS+MLP. SBS iteratively removes the worst-performing feature by 

considering the mean 5-fold CV AUC score returned by the estimator. Let J (n = 89) denote the total number of input radiomics 

features. The objective of the FS algorithm is to rank these features from 1 to J, with 1 being the most important and J the least. 

If an FS method assigns the same importance to multiple features, those features receive the same rank. For example, LASSO will 

assign identical ranks to features with zero coefficients. All FS methods were developed using scikit-learn 62 library (v1.4.0), except 

for MRMR, which was implemented using the mrmr package (v0.2.9). The default hyperparameters of the inductive algorithms 

pre-defined in the scikit-learn package were utilized in this study to avoid biases. A fixed random seed was employed to ensure 

reproducibility of the stochastic components within the pipeline. It is crucial to highlight that the default implementation of LR in 

sklearn incorporates L2-regularization, akin to a Ridge classifier. Therefore, the penalty term was deliberately set to none. It is 

also worth noting that all input radiomics features were standardized using z-score normalization before FS. The normalization 

parameters were estimated only using the training set to prevent data leakage. 

Feature Selection using Autoencoder Variants 

The investigation of self-supervised deep sparse autoencoder (AE) variants in this work draws inspiration from Massi et al 42 , who 

proposed ensembleAE as a robust tool for FS in a class-imbalanced framework, a common challenge in the clinical domain. We 

systematically investigated three AE variants in the increasing order of their complexity: (a) singleAE, a standalone deep sparse 

autoencoder; (b) bayesianAE, a novel variant that uses a Bayesian formulation of a standard AE; and finally (c) ensembleAE, an 

approach that employs an ensemble or collection of AEs. A deep sparse autoencoder is the basic building block that underlies all 

these variants, and is designed as a fixed topology 8-layer artificial neural network (ANN) comprising input, output, and seven 

hidden layers. The number of neurons in the input/output layer corresponds to the total number of input features, J (n = 89); the 

encoding path consists of three hidden layers with 50, 30, and 20 neurons respectively, followed by an embedding layer with 10 

neurons. The decoding path mirrors the encoding path (see Figure S6 in SM). LeakyReLU was used as the activation function. 

Sparsity was imposed on the embedding-layer activations using L 1 regularization with a value of 10 -2 . The model was trained for 

1000 epochs using Adam optimizer with a fixed learning rate of 10 -3 .

Stability Analysis 

A feature selection algorithm may be deemed stable if its feature preference remains consistent over multiple runs, even when 

the training data is subjected to minor perturbations. Depending on the underlying FS algorithm, such perturbations may be 

devised in several ways. Data partitioning, bootstrapping, and class-based resampling are some of the most popular strategies 

used with FS methods 20 . The goal is to generate subsets of the original data that are then used to re-run the selection algorithm 

to assess its stability. Given that we work with a small dataset, a common challenge in radiomics studies, we propose stability 

analysis in a soft-perturbation setting 36 . Here, a stratified Monte Carlo subsampling technique without replacement was repeated 

## ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT ARTICLE IN PRESS ARTICLE IN PRESS 100 times to generate random data partitions containing 75% of the original dataset to be used as the training set for feature 

selection, while the remaining 25% served as the independent test set to estimate the unbiased performance of the selected 

feature. To ensure a fair comparison, these exact partitions were kept fixed across all FS algorithms investigated in this study. 

Although selection results may be influenced by the data distribution shifts that are more prominent while partitioning small 

datasets, a combination of other co-dependent factors, such as feature redundancy, and class imbalance also play a role here. 

Ultimately, each FS algorithm returns 100 feature rankings that can be utilized for stability quantification. Figure 3 illustrates an 

overview of the workflow associated with the stability analysis.

Signature Analysis 

Following the recommendations of Tsamardinos et al 48 , the radiomics signature associated with each FS algorithm was derived 

by applying the method to the entire available dataset and extracting the top-5 highly ranked features. Note that these signatures 

were selected in the context of predicting GS upgrade (GS > 6) using baseline radiomics from a small cohort of 100 prostate cancer 

patients undergoing active surveillance. Crucially, they were extracted strictly to compare, and benchmark the behavior of 

different FS methods rather than to propose a definitive clinically actionable radiomics biomarker. 

The similarity between FS derived signatures across FS methods was quantified using two approaches: a hard measure based on 

feature overlap, and a soft measure based on correlation analysis. For the soft measure, the similarity was defined as the average 

highest correlation of each feature in one set to features in the other, corresponding to maximum-weighted matching of an 

undirected weighted bipartite graph, a metric that was also used to quantify stability (see SM, section S2-iv). In stability analysis, 

MWM measured the average matching cost associated with feature subsets selected by the same FS method under slight data 

perturbations; however, here it was adapted to measure correlations across different FS methods. Both Pearson and Spearman 

correlation coefficients were considered. The degree of correlations was systematically assessed both within specific FS families 

and across different methodological families. 

Performance Analysis 

To evaluate the unbiased predictive performance of each FS method, they were paired with a multivariable LR classifier. During 

each individual data-perturbation run, the LR model was fitted on the training partition using exclusively the top-5 features 

selected during that specific run, and subsequently evaluated on the corresponding, independent test partition to prevent data 

leakage. The overall performance was estimated using a micro-averaged AUC by pooling predictions across all 100 data-

## ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT ARTICLE IN PRESS ARTICLE IN PRESS perturbation runs. Though repeated cross-validation is preferred based on the methodological recommendations of Tsamardinos 

et al 48 , the 100 times repeated holdout validation protocol for performance quantification naturally aligned with the soft data 

perturbation setting used for stability analysis. Note that, this approach is not yielding the performance of a particular signature 

on a specific instance of the LR model. Rather, it measures the average performance of top-5 feature subsets generated by a 

particular FS method. In small or severely class-imbalanced datasets, this unbiased estimate serves as a proxy for the expected 

performance of the final signature, which is ultimately derived from the entire dataset to maximize the use of the available data. 

Computational Cost 

The computational cost associated with each FS algorithm was measured in terms of its empirical execution time and memory 

usage during each soft data perturbation run. Memory profiling for classical FS methods relied entirely on the peak main memory 

(i.e., RAM) usage, whereas for AE variants, GPU memory usage was actively monitored. This was done with the help of a custom 

daemon thread that actively tracked peak PyTorch allocation at regular intervals. While these measurements are highly 

dependent on the specifications of the system used for experimentation, the relative difference observed among the investigated 

FS algorithms gives an idea of their underlying computational cost and guides researchers to make appropriate design choices 

considering different tradeoffs. The resources and computational specifications of the system that we used for conducting all the 

experiments are presented in the SM, Table S2. 

Statistics 

The Wilcoxon signed-rank test, a paired non-parametric test was used to compare the selection stability across competitive FS 

methods. This is because, the 100 softly perturbed data partitions were kept the same for all the investigated FS algorithms, 

making them inherently paired. Comparisons were only performed across three key stability measures: Spearman rank index, 

top-5 Kuncheva, and top-5 MWM cost. Note that, for each FS algorithm, these stability measures are calculated pairwise across 

100 perturbation runs, thus each method can yield 4950 unique unordered pairwise stability estimates. Consequently, the 

Wilcoxon signed-rank test was evaluated over these 4950 observations. Scipy 66 (v1.7.3) library was used for performing this 

statistical test. Similarly, in order to compare the statistical difference in performance (measured using micro-averaged AUC) of 

competitive methods, a DeLong’s test was performed using MLstatkit (v0.1.8). For all statistical tests, a P value < 0.05 was
