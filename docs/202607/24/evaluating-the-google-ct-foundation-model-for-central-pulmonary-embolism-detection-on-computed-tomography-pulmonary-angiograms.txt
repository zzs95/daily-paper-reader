Title: Evaluating the Google CT Foundation model for central pulmonary embolism detection on computed tomography pulmonary angiograms

## Abstract

Early detection of pulmonary embolism (PE) is critical for clinical outcomes, yet existing deep-learning models often fail to generalize across institutions. The recently released Google CT Foundation model, pre-trained on a large, diverse CT corpus, produces compact volumetric embeddings that may transfer to downstream tasks without fine-tuning. We evaluate a classification pipeline that pairs these frozen embeddings with three lightweight heads — Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP), Random Forest (RF), and a stacking ensemble — for central PE detection, training on the public RSNA CTPA dataset and externally validating on the Stanford INSPECT cohort. The pipeline first reproduces the published data-size scaling behavior of the foundation model on other medical pathologies. The highest point-estimate test AUC of 0.79 was obtained by the RF trained on a balanced training set of 408 CT studies and evaluated on the held-out RSNA test set of 144 studies; paired DeLong tests showed that this advantage over the MLP and stacking heads was not statistically significant. Direct comparison to specialized state-of-the-art PE pipelines is task-mismatched — those target any-PE rather than central PE — so the result anchors a non-fine-tuned baseline rather than a deficit. In a low-data regime, hard vascular segmentation did not improve performance. On the external INSPECT cohort, AUC dropped by 0.17 and specificity at the transferred operating point collapsed, so simple global threshold recalibration does not restore deployability—frozen generalist embeddings alone do not guarantee cross-institutional reliability.

### 1.2 Contributions

The primary objective of this study is to assess the capability of the Google CT Foundation model in a classification pipeline to accurately detect central PE in Computed Tomography Pulmonary Angiography (CTPA) images. Specifically, we:

•

Develop and evaluate a classification pipeline that combines frozen Google CT Foundation embeddings with a small classifier head (MLP, Random Forest, or stacking ensemble) for the automated detection of central PE on CTPA.

•

Validate the implementation of the Google CT Foundation model by benchmarking the pipeline’s performance against the published Google CT Foundation benchmark for other medical pathologies, specifically focusing on data-size scaling.

•

Assess whether hard vascular segmentation improves classifier performance within the foundation-model pipeline, in a low-data regime.

•

Characterize cross-institutional generalization on an independent cohort, Stanford Integrating Numerous Sources for Prognostic Evaluation of Clinical Timelines (INSPECT), and show that the RSNA-tuned operating point does not transport to INSPECT in a deployment-relevant sense, i.e. specificity collapse, reinforcing that frozen generalist embeddings do not by themselves guarantee cross-institutional reliability.

•

Provide a statistical comparison of the three classifier heads via paired DeLong tests and paired-bootstrap tests of the AUC difference with Holm–Bonferroni correction within each dataset configuration.

## 2 Methods

To examine the performance of the Google CT Foundation model, a classification pipeline is implemented as illustrated in [Fig. 1](https://www.ejradiology.com/article/S0720-048X(26)00440-7/fulltext#fig1). For each CT series, the pre-trained foundation model generates a 1408-dimensional embedding vector. These embeddings serve as inputs to a trained classifier, which determines the presence or absence of central PE within the original scan.

The structure of the methodology is organized as follows. Section [2.1](https://www.ejradiology.com/article/S0720-048X(26)00440-7/fulltext#sec-2-1) details the primary dataset and the stratification strategy used to create subsets for training and evaluation. The various classifier types, their specific architectures, and the corresponding hyperparameter tuning methods are described in Section [2.2](https://www.ejradiology.com/article/S0720-048X(26)00440-7/fulltext#sec-2-2). The performance metrics utilized during training and evaluation are introduced in Section [2.3](https://www.ejradiology.com/article/S0720-048X(26)00440-7/fulltext#sec-2-3). To assess whether focusing on a region of interest reduces problem complexity, a vascular segmentation technique is employed, as detailed in Section [2.5](https://www.ejradiology.com/article/S0720-048X(26)00440-7/fulltext#sec-2-5). Finally, Section [2.6](https://www.ejradiology.com/article/S0720-048X(26)00440-7/fulltext#sec-2-6) describes the methodology and external dataset used to evaluate the pipeline’s generalizability across different CT sources and image formats.

To facilitate model development and hyperparameter tuning, this primary dataset was split into a training pool and a test set using a stratified 85/15 partition, preserving the number of positive and negative cases in both sets. #### 2.2.1 MLP architecture and training

The classifier is an MLP designed to process the 1408-dimensional embeddings extracted from the foundation model. To improve model robustness, the input embeddings are first passed through a Gaussian Noise layer (with standard deviation

) before being reshaped and aggregated via a global average 1D pooling layer. This is followed by a sequence of hidden layers, where the configuration was treated as a tunable hyperparameter (either “32 × 32” or “64 × 64” neurons). Each dense layer utilizes ReLU activation and is regularized through a sequence of L1 regularization with a fixed weight decay 10−7, batch normalization, layer normalization, and dropout. The final layer consists of a single neuron with a sigmoid activation function to output the binary probability of central PE.

Hyperparameter optimization was performed using a cross-validation strategy within the Keras Tuner framework. A random search over 250 trials is employed. To prevent overfitting during tuning, each trial was evaluated using a stratified 5-fold cross-validation on the training pool. The objective was to maximize the mean Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) AUC across the folds. The tunable search space included the learning rate

, dropout rate

, and Gaussian noise standard deviation

. The L1 weight decay was held fixed at 10−7. The AdamW optimizer’s cosine decay restarts schedule is also optimized, specifically tuning the alpha parameter (0.1, 0.5, 0.8) and first decay steps (50, 100).

Once the optimal hyperparameters were identified from the cross-validation search, the final model was retrained on the training pool using a stratified 90/10 split: the 90% partition was used for parameter updates and the 10% partition was reserved as an internal early-stopping monitor (patience = 50 epochs). This 90/10 split is distinct from the 5-fold cross-validation folds used during hyperparameter search; the held-out RSNA test set was never touched during hyperparameter search or final training and was used only for the reported evaluation. Class weighting was applied using the inverse frequency of positive and negative samples to address data imbalance.

#### 2.2.2 Random forest training

For comparison, a RF classifier from scikit-learn was implemented. The hyperparameters were optimized using an exhaustive 5-fold cross-validation grid search (GridSearchCV) on the training dataset. The hyperparameter grid evaluated the number of trees (n_estimators: 50, 75, 100, 125, 150), maximum tree depth (max_depth: 3, 5, 7, 9, 11), minimum samples required to split an internal node (min_samples_split: 10, 15, 20), minimum samples per leaf (min_samples_leaf: 5, 7, 10, 15), and the number of features to consider when looking for the best split (max_features:

).

To address the class imbalance, the grid search also evaluated multiple class_weight strategies, including balanced_subsample (which computes weights based on the bootstrap sample for each tree), a fixed manual penalty (1:3 ratio for positive class), and weights derived from the global training set frequency. The configuration yielding the highest mean ROC AUC across the 5 folds was selected for final evaluation.

#### 2.2.3 Ensemble model architecture and training

An ensemble model was constructed using a feature-extraction (or “stacking”) approach. First, a base MLP model was trained and tuned on the 1408-dimensional embeddings, following the exact hyperparameter tuning procedure described in Section [2.2.1](https://www.ejradiology.com/article/S0720-048X(26)00440-7/fulltext#sec-2-2-1). Once the best base MLP was identified, a new feature-extractor model was instantiated from it. This new model used the MLP’s input layer but produced its output from the second-to-last hidden layer (the layer immediately preceding the final sigmoid activation). This intermediate model was then used to transform the original 1408-dimensional embeddings into a learned, lower-dimensional feature representation.

A RF classifier was then trained on these extracted features. This head RF model was tuned using the same 5-fold GridSearchCV process, hyperparameter grid, and class weighting strategies described in Section [2.2.2](https://www.ejradiology.com/article/S0720-048X(26)00440-7/fulltext#sec-2-2-2). This stacked architecture leverages the MLP’s ability to learn non-linear representations from the embeddings while utilizing the RF’s robustness for the final classification decision.

### 2.3 Evaluation and performance index

To rigorously evaluate the performance of the classification pipeline, Sensitivity, Specificity, ROC AUC, and Confidence Interval (CI) are utilized as the primary evaluation metrics. Sensitivity (Recall) measures the proportion of actual positive cases (studies with central PE) that were correctly identified by the model. It is a crucial indicator of the model’s ability to avoid false negatives [[24]](https://www.ejradiology.com/article/S0720-048X(26)00440-7/fulltext#)

### 2.5 Segmentation impact

Here, the objective is to determine whether the application of vascular segmentation simplifies the classification task, as evidenced by measurable improvements in the pipeline’s performance metrics, specifically test AUC and CI. To isolate the pulmonary vasculature, a dedicated preprocessing pipeline is implemented on the two smallest training datasets and the RSNA test dataset. The core of this pipeline involved segmenting the vessels from the surrounding anatomy using VesselFM [[29]](https://www.ejradiology.com/article/S0720-048X(26)00440-7/fulltext#)

29.

B. Wittmann, Y. Wattenberg, T. Amiranashvili, S. Shit, B. Menze, vesselFM: A Foundation Model for Universal 3D Blood Vessel Segmentation, in: Proceedings of the Computer Vision and Pattern Recognition Conference, 2025, pp. 20874–20884.

, a state-of-the-art 3D foundation model designed specifically for medical image segmentation. As a specialized tool, VesselFM excels at generating precise, zero-shot 3D segmentations of complex vascular structures directly from CT scans.

VesselFM is applied to each raw chest CT scan to generate a 3D binary mask that identifies the voxels corresponding to the pulmonary vasculature. From this mask and the original CT volume, a new masked CT series is created that contains only the areas identified as blood vessels by the VesselFM algorithm. To ensure compatibility with the downstream classification pipeline, the resulting series was reconstructed while preserving the critical metadata from the original scan, including image dimensions, slice thickness, and pixel spacing. The Hounsfield Units (HU) values of the voxels within the segmented vessels were also maintained. These preprocessed, vessel-centric CT series were then used as the direct input for the Google CT Foundation model to generate embeddings for the classification pipeline. One sample of the segmentation of VesselFM on a CT slice is depicted in [Fig. 2](https://www.ejradiology.com/article/S0720-048X(26)00440-7/fulltext#fig2). The figure reflects how the VesselFM algorithm segments the pulmonary arteries and ribs. No secondary processing is applied to remove the ribs from the segmented images that were passed back into the Google CT Foundation model.

To visually assess whether segmentation simplifies the classification task, the t-distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE) is applied as a dimensionality reduction technique that projects high-dimensional embeddings into a lower-dimensional space while preserving local structures [[30]](https://www.ejradiology.com/article/S0720-048X(26)00440-7/fulltext#)

30.

Van der Maaten, L. ∙ Hinton, G.

**Visualizing data using t-SNE**

_J. Mach. Learn. Res._ 2008; **9**:2579-2605

. By comparing the t-SNE plots of the original and segmented datasets, one can observe whether the segmentation leads to a more distinct separation between positive and negative classes, which indicates a simplified classification problem. Following this visual analysis, three classifiers are trained independently for each of the two smaller sub-datasets listed in [Table 1](https://www.ejradiology.com/article/S0720-048X(26)00440-7/fulltext#tbl1). Finally, the AUC and CI of these classifiers are evaluated on the segmented RSNA test dataset to determine whether the segmentation process objectively enhances the performance of the classification pipeline in the specified low-data regime.

Similarly, [Fig. 4(b)](https://www.ejradiology.com/article/S0720-048X(26)00440-7/fulltext#fig4b) indicates vascular segmentation does not necessarily improve class separation. This conclusion is inferred by comparing t-SNE projections with and without segmentation, which show comparable overlap between positive and negative cases.

The classification pipeline is trained using two segmented datasets and three distinct classifiers, with performance evaluated on the segmented RSNA test set as detailed in [Table 5](https://www.ejradiology.com/article/S0720-048X(26)00440-7/fulltext#tbl5). For the segmented CT scans, the RF classifier consistently yields the best results, demonstrating a positive correlation between dataset size and test AUC. However, comparing these outcomes to the performance on the original CT scans in [Table 2](https://www.ejradiology.com/article/S0720-048X(26)00440-7/fulltext#tbl2) suggests that segmentation does not improve the classification pipeline’s performance. In fact, segmentation may reduce the ability of the Google CT Foundation model to generate embeddings containing high-utility information.

Qualitatively, the t-SNE projection ([Fig. 5](https://www.ejradiology.com/article/S0720-048X(26)00440-7/fulltext#fig5)) reveals a visible separation between the embedding clusters of the two datasets. As a third quantitative test, the linear-probe analysis (Section [2.6.2](https://www.ejradiology.com/article/S0720-048X(26)00440-7/fulltext#sec-2-6-2)) directly assesses the linear separability of the embeddings on both domains. On the large-balanced training set, the linear probe achieves test AUC 0.795 [0.697–0.882] on RSNA — statistically indistinguishable from the RF (AUC 0.79, paired DeLong

). This confirms that the embeddings carry meaningful linearly separable structure for central PE on the source domain, despite the intermingled t-SNE projection in [Fig. 4](https://www.ejradiology.com/article/S0720-048X(26)00440-7/fulltext#fig4); the t-SNE visual overlap is therefore uninformative about the linear-probe geometry. On INSPECT, however, the linear probe degrades to 0.556 [0.469–0.642] while the RF holds at 0.62 (paired DeLong

). The much larger degradation of the simpler linear head versus the non-linear RF under domain shift is itself consistent with the MMD/C2ST-detected feature shift: under cross-domain conditions the discriminative signal that survives in the embedding is no longer linearly accessible.

This distributional shift negatively impacts downstream generalization performance. [Table 6](https://www.ejradiology.com/article/S0720-048X(26)00440-7/fulltext#tbl6) presents the performance of RF classifiers trained on RSNA splits and evaluated on the INSPECT test set. A substantial performance degradation is observed, with AUC values dropping to approximately 0.62–0.63 across all training strategies.

Crucially, when applying the decision threshold (operating point) optimized on the RSNA test set, models exhibited poor specificity (ranging from 0.08 to 0.36), resulting in a high number of FP. This suggests that the distribution of output probabilities is shifted in the target domain, further corroborating the presence of a domain gap that simple threshold calibration cannot fully resolve. In clinical practice, AI-based pulmonary embolism detection systems are primarily used to prioritize emergency department and inpatient CT scans for urgent review. In this context, low specificity would be a problem because the model would generate excessive false-positive alerts which would contribute to “alarm-fatigue” among radiologists and would delay the reading of other critical CT scans.

#### 3.2.1 Threshold transport and recalibration analysis

A natural question is whether simple recalibration of the operating point can recover deployable performance on INSPECT. To assess this, the RSNA-OOF threshold for the best RF model (Large Balanced) at three target sensitivities — 0.85, 0.90, and 0.95 — is computed, and each resulting threshold is applied to both the RSNA test set and the INSPECT test set. [Table 7](https://www.ejradiology.com/article/S0720-048X(26)00440-7/fulltext#tbl7) summarizes the outcome.

Two observations follow from [Table 7](https://www.ejradiology.com/article/S0720-048X(26)00440-7/fulltext#tbl7). First, RSNA test sensitivity remains essentially flat at 0.86 across the three targets, because the OOF-derived threshold does not transfer with full fidelity to the small (

) RSNA test set. Second — and more importantly — on INSPECT, specificity collapses from 0.36 to 0.14 as the sensitivity target is raised, while sensitivity gains only modestly. Simple global recalibration of the threshold does not restore a deployable operating point on the external cohort. At a clinically relevant target of 0.95 sensitivity, the INSPECT specificity of 0.14 would generate approximately 3 false-positive alerts for every true positive in a 1:3 prevalence-matched setting, which is well below the alarm-fatigue threshold that radiologists tolerate in practice.

In a clinical triage setting, a specificity below 0.2 would generate enough false positives to produce “alarm-fatigue” and would likely delay the reading of other emergency-department CTs, halting adoption. The deployment limitation we identify is therefore not the AUC alone but the non-transportability of the operating point.

A single embedding generated by such a model can serve as a common representation for assessing the presence or absence of many different pathologies, enabling a streamlined multi-task analysis from a single forward pass. A potential additional advantage — which is not directly evidenced by experiments and results of this study — is that operating within a shared embedding space may facilitate reasoning about relationships between pathologies. For example, the presence of one abnormality may implicitly reduce the likelihood of another, a relationship that can be more naturally modeled when multiple conditions are inferred from the same latent representation. In contrast, using dozens or hundreds of independently trained, task-specific algorithms makes it difficult to compare outputs or model interactions between conditions in a consistent manner. Validating this hypothesis would require multi-task downstream experiments outside the scope of the current study.

One might argue that increasing the dataset size would bridge this performance gap between this work and state-of-the-art algorithms. Benchmarks for the CT foundation model across seven diverse medical imaging tasks — using MLP heads for evaluation — demonstrate that while performance generally scales with data, this trend is task-dependent [[7]](https://www.ejradiology.com/article/S0720-048X(26)00440-7/fulltext#)

A naïve extrapolation from the Google CT Foundation benchmark curves _for other pathologies_ suggests a possible plateau approximately near AUC 0.90 at full RSNA scale; because those curves are for different pathologies and classifier heads, this extrapolation is illustrative only and should not be read as a quantitative prediction for central PE. Ideally, a foundation model should capture sufficiently rich features to make PE detection a relatively simple task, allowing a small dataset to produce a pipeline that rivals specialized models. However, the observed test AUC suggests that this goal of “high performance with low data” remains elusive for this specific pathology.

Cross-institutional generalization is the second key finding. We hypothesized that a foundation model pre-trained on a massive and diverse corpus would generalize reliably to a different cohort. The data do not support this hypothesis for central PE: the best AUC on the INSPECT test set was 0.17 lower than on RSNA, and — as discussed above — the operating point does not transport even after global threshold recalibration. The objective tests of feature shift (MMD

, C2ST accuracy 0.839) and the linear-probe degradation, from 0.795 to 0.556, are consistent with a persistent domain-specific signal in the embeddings (scanner, reconstruction kernel, noise textures) that the frozen foundation model does not abstract away. A classification pipeline built on a non-fine-tuned generalist foundation model, therefore, does not, on this evidence, offer better cross-institutional reliability than a specialized, task-specific algorithm would.

### 4.1 Limitations and future work

#### Reproducibility of the foundation model.

The Google CT Foundation Model is not open-source. For this study, it is accessed via the Google Health Research API during 2024–2025; access is granted by Google Health Research to selected research groups and is not openly distributable. The embedding-extraction code, including preprocessing (resampling, HU clamping), input size (

), and batching, is publicly released on Google Health GitHub repository as Jupyter notebooks (CT_Foundation_Demo.ipynb for DICOM, CT_Foundation_NIfTI_Demo.ipynb for NIfTI). To the best of our knowledge, no peer-reviewed publication or arXiv preprint dedicated to this specific embedding model has been released. The access constraint is a structural reproducibility limitation of any study built on this model. #### Segmentation ablations are restricted to the low-data regime.

The segmentation experiments in Section [2.5](https://www.ejradiology.com/article/S0720-048X(26)00440-7/fulltext#sec-2-5) were run only on the two smallest training subsets (D1,

; D2,

), where the baseline AUCs are near chance, and the test is statistically under-powered. The conclusion “hard vessel masking did not improve performance” should therefore be read as conditional on the low-data regime and on the specific VesselFM hard-masking approach. Additional ablations that would test the underlying “context matters” hypothesis include: dilated-vessel masks; lung-parenchyma-preserving masks; and region-guided attention or cropping around the main pulmonary artery instead of hard masking.

#### Rib co-segmentation in the segmented inputs.

As visible in [Fig. 2](https://www.ejradiology.com/article/S0720-048X(26)00440-7/fulltext#fig2), the VesselFM output for the scans co-segments the ribs together with the pulmonary vasculature. As no secondary rib-removal post-processing step is applied, the segmented-input experiments contain residual osseous structure. Rib contamination could plausibly drive part of the null segmentation result; a rib-removal ablation is identified as a follow-up. Accordingly, the segmentation null result should be read as conditional on this residual osseous signal, and not as evidence that vascular localisation is uninformative for this pipeline, until a rib-removal sensitivity check has been performed.

However, the public RSNA CTPA challenge manifest does not expose per-study age or sex, so a paired RSNA-versus-INSPECT demographics table cannot be constructed from the data used in this study. Both cohorts are US-based (RSNA — multi-institutional US contributors; INSPECT — Stanford Medicine), which limits external-domain-shift conclusions to within-US-style imaging protocols. #### Statistical power and within-task specialized baseline.

The RSNA hold-out test set (

, 36 positive) is relatively small. Pairwise DeLong tests confirmed that this sample size is statistically underpowered to declare AUC differences of the observed magnitudes between the three classifier heads as significant (Section [2.4](https://www.ejradiology.com/article/S0720-048X(26)00440-7/fulltext#sec-2-4)). Consequently, evaluating the pipeline on a larger, multi-institutional held-out test set represents a natural next step. Furthermore, a strict like-for-like comparison would benefit from re-training a specialized PE-detection baseline architecture (e.g., ResNet+LSTM) restricted to central PE on the identical RSNA split.

#### Other PE subtypes and prospective evaluation.

This study primarily focused on central PE; the performance of the classification pipeline on other PE subtypes remains to be investigated. Moreover, this study is retrospective, and further analysis in a prospective clinical setting is required to compare the utility of foundation models versus specialized algorithms in real-world practice. Specifically, it is crucial to evaluate whether integrating a foundation model into clinical workflows can measurably improve radiologist performance and patient outcomes compared to multiple task-specific algorithms.
